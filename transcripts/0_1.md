# 0.1 Angles and Radian Measure

## Measuring a turn
**On screen:** A bicycle wheel of radius 1 rolls along the ground. A red dot on the tire leaves a trail; when the wheel has turned a quarter, a half, a full turn, the ground under it is marked 1.57, 3.14, 6.28. Beside it, a protractor with "360°" and a question mark.
> Degrees split a full turn into three hundred sixty pieces. That number goes back to ancient Babylon, and nothing about a circle forces it. [pause]
> Calculus needs a way of measuring angles that comes from the circle itself. Watch this wheel. Its radius is one, and as it turns, the distance it rolls is exactly the length of tire that touched the ground. [pause]
> Measure the turn by that distance, and you have radians. Every formula in calculus that involves sine or cosine assumes the angle is in radians. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 0.1 · Angles and Radian Measure." Unit 0 · Trig Review.

## Angles in standard position
**On screen:** Axes. A ray starts on the positive x-axis (labeled "initial side") and swings counterclockwise to a new position ("terminal side"), with a curved arrow for the angle θ. Then a second ray swings clockwise with "negative angle".
> An angle in standard position starts on the positive x-axis. That's the initial side. It turns to the terminal side, and the amount of turning is the angle. [pause]
> Turning counterclockwise gives a positive angle. Turning clockwise gives a negative angle. [long pause]

## What a radian is
**On screen:** A circle of radius r. The radius is copied, bent, and laid along the circle starting at the positive x-axis; the angle it cuts off is shaded and labeled "1 radian". Then more radius lengths are laid around: 2, 3, a bit more than 6, and the label "2π ≈ 6.28 radius lengths in a full turn".
> Take a circle of any size, and take its radius. Bend a copy of the radius and lay it along the circle. [pause]
> The angle that one radius of arc cuts off is one radian. It's a little under sixty degrees, about fifty-seven point three. [pause]
> The circumference is two pi r, so exactly two pi radius lengths fit all the way around. [pause]
> So a full turn is two pi radians, about six point two eight. Half a turn is pi radians. [long pause]

## Converting
**On screen:** "half a turn: 180° = π radians" boxed. Under it two conversion rules: "degrees → radians: multiply by π/180" and "radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π".
> Half a turn is a hundred eighty degrees and also pi radians. That one fact does all the converting. [pause]
> To go from degrees to radians, multiply by pi over a hundred eighty. To go back, multiply by a hundred eighty over pi. [pause]
> The units cancel the way they would in any unit conversion: degrees over degrees. [long pause]

## Converting 150 degrees
**On screen:** The problem: "Convert 150° to radians." Board: 150° · π/180°; = 150π/180; = 5π/6 (divide top and bottom by 30).
> Convert a hundred fifty degrees to radians. [pause]
> Multiply by pi over a hundred eighty. [pause]
> That's a hundred fifty pi over a hundred eighty. [pause]
> Both numbers are divisible by thirty. A hundred fifty over thirty is five, a hundred eighty over thirty is six. So the angle is five pi over six radians. [long pause]

## Landmark angles
**On screen:** A unit circle with the angles 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2, 2π/3, 3π/4, 5π/6, π, 7π/6, 5π/4, 4π/3, 3π/2, 5π/3, 7π/4, 11π/6 appearing quadrant by quadrant, each with its degree measure in gray.
> A few angles come up constantly, so it pays to know them on sight. A quarter turn, ninety degrees, is pi over two. Half a turn is pi. Three quarters is three pi over two. [pause]
> Thirty degrees is pi over six, forty-five is pi over four, and sixty is pi over three. [pause]
> The rest are multiples of those. Count in sixths, fourths, or thirds of pi: five pi over six is five thirty-degree steps. [long pause]

## Coterminal angles
**On screen:** A ray at π/3. A second ray spins a full extra turn and stops in the same place, labeled "π/3 + 2π = 7π/3". A third spins clockwise to the same place, "π/3 − 2π = −5π/3".
> Spin an extra full turn and you end up exactly where you started. Angles that share a terminal side are called coterminal. [pause]
> Adding or subtracting two pi, as many times as you like, gives a coterminal angle. Pi over three, seven pi over three, and negative five pi over three all point the same way. [long pause]

## Arc length and sector area
**On screen:** A circle of radius r with a highlighted arc and a shaded sector for angle θ. Formulas: "arc length s = rθ" and "sector area A = ½r²θ", with "θ in radians" beneath both. A wedge of pizza illustrates the sector.
> Radians make two formulas simple. An angle of theta radians cuts off theta radius lengths of arc, so the arc length is r times theta. [pause]
> The sector is that fraction of the whole disk. A full turn of two pi gives area pi r squared, so theta radians gives one half r squared theta. [pause]
> Both formulas need theta in radians. With degrees they'd need an extra pi over a hundred eighty. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "180° = π radians". "Full turn: 2π". "Coterminal: add or subtract 2π". "s = rθ, A = ½r²θ (radians)".
> Half a turn is pi radians, a full turn is two pi. Add or subtract two pi for a coterminal angle. With theta in radians, arc length is r theta and sector area is one half r squared theta. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Radians to degrees
**On screen:** "Convert 5π/4 to degrees." Board: 5π/4 · 180°/π; the π cancels; = 5 · 180°/4; = 5 · 45°; = 225°.
> Convert five pi over four radians to degrees. [pause]
> Multiply by a hundred eighty over pi. [pause]
> The pi on top cancels the pi on the bottom, leaving five times a hundred eighty over four.
> A hundred eighty over four is forty-five, and five times forty-five is two hundred twenty-five degrees. [long pause]

## Example 2: Coterminal angles
**On screen:** "Find the angle between 0 and 2π that is coterminal with 17π/6." Board: 2π = 12π/6; 17π/6 − 12π/6 = 5π/6; 5π/6 is between 0 and 2π.
> Find the angle between zero and two pi that's coterminal with seventeen pi over six. [pause]
> Write two pi with the same denominator: twelve pi over six. [pause]
> Subtract it: seventeen pi over six minus twelve pi over six is five pi over six.
> Five pi over six is between zero and two pi, so that's the angle. [long pause]

## Example 3: Arc length
**On screen:** "A pendulum 80 cm long swings through an angle of 30°. How far does its tip travel?" A pendulum with its arc highlighted. Board: 30° = π/6; s = rθ = 80 · π/6; = 40π/3; ≈ 41.9 cm.
> A pendulum eighty centimeters long swings through an angle of thirty degrees. How far does its tip travel? [pause]
> The arc length formula needs radians, so convert first: thirty degrees is pi over six. [pause]
> Arc length is r theta: eighty times pi over six.
> That's forty pi over three, about forty-one point nine centimeters. [long pause]

## Example 4: Sector area
**On screen:** "A pizza has radius 8 inches and is cut into 8 equal slices. Find the area of one slice." A pizza with one slice pulled out. Board: θ = 2π/8 = π/4; A = ½r²θ = ½ · 64 · π/4; = 8π; ≈ 25.1 in².
> A pizza has radius eight inches and is cut into eight equal slices. Find the area of one slice. [pause]
> The full turn of two pi is split eight ways, so each slice has angle pi over four. [pause]
> Sector area is one half r squared theta: one half times sixty-four times pi over four.
> That's eight pi, about twenty-five point one square inches. [long pause]
