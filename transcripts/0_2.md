# 0.2 Angles and Radian Measure

## Measuring a turn
**On screen:** A bicycle wheel of radius 1 rolls along the ground. A red dot on the tire leaves a trail; when the wheel has turned a quarter, a half, a full turn, the ground under it is marked 1.57, 3.14, 6.28. Beside it, a protractor with "360°" and a question mark.
> Degrees split a full turn into three hundred sixty pieces. That number goes back to ancient Babylon, and nothing about a circle forces it. [pause]
> Calculus needs a way of measuring angles that comes from the circle itself. Watch this wheel. Its radius is one, and as it turns, the distance it rolls is exactly the length of tire that touched the ground. [pause]
> Measure the turn by that distance, and you have radians. Every formula in calculus that involves sine or cosine assumes the angle is in radians. [long pause]

## Angles in standard position
**On screen:** Axes. A ray starts on the positive x-axis (labeled "initial side") and swings counterclockwise to a new position ("terminal side"), with a curved arrow for the angle θ. Then a second ray swings clockwise with "negative angle".
> An angle in standard position starts on the positive x-axis. That's the initial side. It turns to the terminal side, and the amount of turning is the angle. [pause]
> Turning counterclockwise gives a positive angle. Turning clockwise gives a negative angle. [long pause]

## What a radian is
**On screen:** A circle of radius 1 centered at the vertex of an angle. The arc the angle cuts off is highlighted: "radian measure = arc length on a circle of radius 1". The radius is copied, bent, and laid along the circle; the angle it cuts off is shaded and labeled "1 radian ≈ 57.3°". More radius lengths are laid around: 2, 3, a bit more than 6. Then "circumference = 2πr = 2π(1) = 2π" and a box: "full turn = 2π radians, half turn = π radians".
> Radians measure an angle with the circle itself. Draw a circle of radius one, centered at the corner of the angle. [pause]
> The angle's radian measure is the length of the arc it cuts off on that circle. [pause]
> Bend a copy of the radius and lay it along the circle. An arc one radius long is one radian. That's a little under sixty degrees, about fifty-seven point three. [pause]
> A full turn cuts off the whole circle, so its radian measure is the whole circumference. The circumference is two pi r, and with r equal to one that's two pi. About six point two eight radius lengths fit around. [pause]
> So a full turn is two pi radians, and half a turn is half the circumference: pi radians. [long pause]

## Converting
**On screen:** "half a turn: 180° = π radians" boxed. Under it two conversion rules: "degrees → radians: multiply by π/180" and "radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π".
> Half a turn is a hundred eighty degrees and also pi radians. That one fact does all the converting. [pause]
> To go from degrees to radians, multiply by pi over a hundred eighty. To go back, multiply by a hundred eighty over pi. [pause]
> The units cancel the way they would in any unit conversion: degrees over degrees. [long pause]

## Converting 150 degrees
**On screen:** The problem: "Convert 150° to radians." Board: 150° · π/180°; = 150π/180; = 5π/6 (divide top and bottom by 30).
> Convert a hundred fifty degrees to radians. [pause]
> Multiply by pi over a hundred eighty. [pause]
> That's a hundred fifty pi over a hundred eighty. [pause]
> Both numbers are divisible by thirty. A hundred fifty over thirty is five, a hundred eighty over thirty is six. So the angle is five pi over six radians. [long pause]

## Landmark angles
**On screen:** A unit circle with the angles 0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2, 2π/3, 3π/4, 5π/6, π, 7π/6, 5π/4, 4π/3, 3π/2, 5π/3, 7π/4, 11π/6 appearing quadrant by quadrant, each with its degree measure in gray.
> A few angles come up constantly, so it pays to know them on sight. A quarter turn, ninety degrees, is pi over two. Half a turn is pi. Three quarters is three pi over two. [pause]
> Thirty degrees is pi over six, forty-five is pi over four, and sixty is pi over three. [pause]
> The rest are multiples of those. Count in sixths, fourths, or thirds of pi: five pi over six is five thirty-degree steps. [long pause]

## Coterminal angles
**On screen:** A ray at π/3. A second ray spins a full extra turn and stops in the same place, labeled "π/3 + 2π = 7π/3". A third spins clockwise to the same place, "π/3 − 2π = −5π/3".
> Spin an extra full turn and you end up exactly where you started. Angles that share a terminal side are called coterminal. [pause]
> Adding or subtracting two pi, as many times as you like, gives a coterminal angle. Pi over three, seven pi over three, and negative five pi over three all point the same way. [long pause]

## What fraction of the circle
**On screen:** A circle with a shaded wedge for angle θ, and "θ out of 2π" above it. The fraction "fraction of the circle = θ/2π". Then three cases fill in: θ = π/2: (π/2)/(2π) = 1/4, a quarter of the circle shaded; θ = π: 1/2; θ = π/3: 1/6.
> Since a full turn is two pi radians, an angle of theta radians covers theta out of two pi. So theta over two pi is the fraction of the circle the angle covers. [pause]
> Pi over two divided by two pi is one fourth: a quarter of the circle. Pi is half the circle. Pi over three is one sixth. [long pause]

## Arc length
**On screen:** A circle of radius r with the arc for angle θ highlighted, and the whole circumference traced in a lighter color. The board: s = (θ/2π) · 2πr; the 2π's cancel; s = rθ. A boxed "s = rθ (θ in radians)".
> The arc the angle cuts off is that same fraction of the whole circumference. On a circle of radius r, the circumference is two pi r. [pause]
> So the arc length is theta over two pi, times two pi r. [pause]
> The two pi on the bottom cancels the two pi in the circumference, and that leaves s equals r theta. An angle of theta radians cuts off theta radius lengths of arc. [long pause]

## Sector area
**On screen:** The same circle with the sector shaded and the whole disk shaded faintly. Board: A = (θ/2π) · πr²; the π's cancel; A = r²θ/2 = ½r²θ. A pizza with one slice pulled out. A note: "θ in radians. In degrees, the fraction is θ/360°."
> The sector, the wedge between the two sides of the angle, is the same fraction of the whole disk. The whole disk has area pi r squared. [pause]
> So the sector area is theta over two pi, times pi r squared. The pis cancel and the two stays on the bottom: one half r squared theta. [pause]
> Both formulas need theta in radians, because they're built on a full turn being two pi. With degrees, the fraction of the circle would be theta over three hundred sixty instead. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "180° = π radians". "Full turn: 2π". "Coterminal: add or subtract 2π". "Fraction of the circle: θ/2π". "s = (θ/2π)(2πr) = rθ, A = (θ/2π)(πr²) = ½r²θ (radians)".
> An angle's radian measure is the arc it cuts off on a circle of radius one, so a full turn is two pi and half a turn is pi. Add or subtract two pi for a coterminal angle. An angle of theta radians covers theta over two pi of the circle, so arc length is that fraction of two pi r, which is r theta, and sector area is that fraction of pi r squared, which is one half r squared theta. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Radians to degrees
**On screen:** "Convert 5π/4 to degrees." Board: 5π/4 · 180°/π; the π cancels; = 5 · 180°/4; = 5 · 45°; = 225°.
> Convert five pi over four radians to degrees. [pause]
> Multiply by a hundred eighty over pi. [pause]
> The pi on top cancels the pi on the bottom, leaving five times a hundred eighty over four.
> A hundred eighty over four is forty-five, and five times forty-five is two hundred twenty-five degrees. [long pause]

## Example 2: Coterminal angles
**On screen:** "Find the angle between 0 and 2π that is coterminal with 17π/6." Board: 2π = 12π/6; 17π/6 − 12π/6 = 5π/6; 5π/6 is between 0 and 2π.
> Find the angle between zero and two pi that's coterminal with seventeen pi over six. [pause]
> Write two pi with the same denominator: twelve pi over six. [pause]
> Subtract it: seventeen pi over six minus twelve pi over six is five pi over six.
> Five pi over six is between zero and two pi, so that's the angle. [long pause]

## Example 3: Arc length
**On screen:** "A pendulum 80 cm long swings through an angle of 30°. How far does its tip travel?" A pendulum with its arc highlighted. Board: 30° = π/6; fraction of the circle: (π/6)/(2π) = 1/12; s = (1/12) · 2π(80) = 40π/3; ≈ 41.9 cm; same as rθ = 80 · π/6.
> A pendulum eighty centimeters long swings through an angle of thirty degrees. How far does its tip travel? [pause]
> Work in radians, so convert first: thirty degrees is pi over six. [pause]
> Pi over six out of two pi is one twelfth, so the tip travels one twelfth of the way around a circle of radius eighty.
> One twelfth of two pi times eighty is forty pi over three, about forty-one point nine centimeters. [pause]
> The shortcut r theta gives the same thing: eighty times pi over six. [long pause]

## Example 4: Sector area
**On screen:** "A pizza has radius 8 inches and is cut into 8 equal slices. Find the area of one slice." A pizza with one slice pulled out. Board: θ = 2π/8 = π/4; fraction: (π/4)/(2π) = 1/8; A = (1/8) · π(8)² = 8π; ≈ 25.1 in²; same as ½r²θ = ½ · 64 · π/4.
> A pizza has radius eight inches and is cut into eight equal slices. Find the area of one slice. [pause]
> The full turn of two pi is split eight ways, so each slice has angle pi over four. [pause]
> Pi over four out of two pi is one eighth, which makes sense: one slice is one eighth of the pizza.
> The whole pizza has area pi times eight squared, sixty-four pi. One eighth of that is eight pi, about twenty-five point one square inches. [pause]
> The formula one half r squared theta agrees: one half times sixty-four times pi over four is eight pi. [long pause]
