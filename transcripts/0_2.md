# 0.2 The Unit Circle

## A point going around
**On screen:** A Ferris wheel turns slowly. One red gondola is tracked: a dot at its position, with dashed lines across to a height scale on the right and down to a sideways scale underneath. The two readouts change as the wheel turns.
> A rider on a Ferris wheel goes around and around. Two numbers describe where they are at any moment: how far across from the center, and how far up. [pause]
> As the wheel turns, both numbers rise and fall, over and over. [pause]
> Sine and cosine are exactly those two numbers, for a wheel of radius one. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 0.2 · The Unit Circle." Unit 0 · Trig Review.

## Sine and cosine on the unit circle
**On screen:** The unit circle: center at the origin, radius 1. A ray at angle θ from the positive x-axis meets the circle at a point P. Dashed lines drop from P to each axis. Labels: "x = cos θ" on the horizontal leg, "y = sin θ" on the vertical leg, "P = (cos θ, sin θ)". Plain words beneath: "cosine: how far across. sine: how far up."
> The unit circle is the circle of radius one centered at the origin. Turn through an angle theta from the positive x-axis, and you land on a point of the circle. [pause]
> The x-coordinate of that point is called the cosine of theta. The y-coordinate is called the sine of theta. [pause]
> In plain words: cosine is how far across, sine is how far up. [pause]
> Since every point on the circle is one unit from the center, x squared plus y squared is one. So cosine squared plus sine squared is always one. [long pause]

## The quadrantal angles
**On screen:** The four points where the circle meets the axes light up one at a time: angle 0 at (1, 0), π/2 at (0, 1), π at (−1, 0), 3π/2 at (0, −1). A small table fills in: θ, cos θ, sin θ.
> The easiest angles land on the axes. At zero, the point is one comma zero: cosine one, sine zero. [pause]
> A quarter turn, pi over two, lands at the top: zero comma one. So cosine of pi over two is zero, and sine is one. [pause]
> Pi lands on the left at negative one comma zero, and three pi over two at the bottom, zero comma negative one. [long pause]

## The special triangles
**On screen:** First a 45-45-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1: both legs labeled √2/2, with the work "a² + a² = 1, a² = ½, a = 1/√2 = √2/2". Then a 30-60-90 triangle with hypotenuse 1, made by cutting an equilateral triangle of side 1 in half: short leg ½, long leg √3/2. Then the three first-quadrant points: π/6 at (√3/2, ½), π/4 at (√2/2, √2/2), π/3 at (½, √3/2).
> Two triangles give every other value you need. Drop a vertical line from the point at pi over four. The triangle has two forty-five degree angles, so its legs are equal. [pause]
> Call each leg a. Then a squared plus a squared is one, so a squared is one half, and a is one over root two, which is root two over two. [pause]
> For pi over three, cut an equilateral triangle of side one in half. The short leg is half the side: one half. Then the Pythagorean theorem gives the long leg: root three over two. [pause]
> So pi over six lands at root three over two comma one half. Pi over three swaps them: one half comma root three over two. [pause]
> A quick check: the smaller angle, pi over six, is lower, so its sine is the smaller number, one half. [long pause]

## Reference angles
**On screen:** An angle in the second quadrant, 5π/6. Its reflection across the y-axis lands at π/6. The acute angle between the terminal side and the x-axis is shaded and labeled "reference angle π/6". The two points are shown as mirror images: (√3/2, ½) and (−√3/2, ½).
> Every other landmark angle is a reflection of one of these. The reference angle is the acute angle between the terminal side and the x-axis. [pause]
> Five pi over six is pi over six short of pi, so its reference angle is pi over six. The point at five pi over six is the mirror image of the point at pi over six. [pause]
> Same numbers, possibly different signs. The quadrant decides the signs. [long pause]

## Signs by quadrant
**On screen:** The unit circle with four quadrants labeled. In each quadrant the signs of x (cos) and y (sin) appear: I: cos +, sin +. II: cos −, sin +. III: cos −, sin −. IV: cos +, sin −. Then tan's sign (sin/cos) is added in each: +, −, +, −.
> Cosine is the x-coordinate, so it's positive on the right half of the circle and negative on the left. [pause]
> Sine is the y-coordinate: positive on the top half, negative on the bottom. [pause]
> So in the first quadrant both are positive. In the second, sine is positive and cosine negative. In the third, both are negative. In the fourth, cosine is positive and sine negative. [long pause]

## Sine and cosine of 5π/6
**On screen:** The problem: "Find cos(5π/6) and sin(5π/6)." Board: reference angle: π − 5π/6 = π/6; at π/6: (√3/2, ½); quadrant II: cos < 0, sin > 0; cos(5π/6) = −√3/2; sin(5π/6) = ½. The unit circle with both points marked.
> Find the cosine and sine of five pi over six. [try it]
> First the reference angle. Five pi over six is pi over six short of pi, so the reference angle is pi over six. [pause]
> At pi over six, the point is root three over two comma one half. [pause]
> Five pi over six is in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative and sine is positive.
> So cosine of five pi over six is negative root three over two. [pause]
> And sine of five pi over six is one half. [long pause]

## Tangent
**On screen:** The ray to P = (cos θ, sin θ). Rise over run along the ray: "slope = sin θ / cos θ = tan θ". At θ = π/2 the ray is vertical and the label "tan θ undefined (cos θ = 0)" appears.
> One more function comes straight from the circle. The tangent of theta is sine over cosine. [pause]
> That's rise over run along the ray, so tangent is the slope of the terminal side. [pause]
> When cosine is zero, at pi over two and three pi over two, the ray is vertical and tangent is undefined. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "(cos θ, sin θ) is the point on the unit circle at angle θ." "cos² θ + sin² θ = 1." The first-quadrant values π/6, π/4, π/3. "Reference angle gives the numbers; the quadrant gives the signs." "tan θ = sin θ/cos θ."
> The point at angle theta on the unit circle is cosine theta comma sine theta, and cosine squared plus sine squared is one. Learn the values at pi over six, pi over four and pi over three. For any other landmark angle, the reference angle gives the numbers and the quadrant gives the signs. Tangent is sine over cosine. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An angle in the third quadrant
**On screen:** "Find cos(4π/3) and sin(4π/3)." Board: 4π/3 − π = π/3, the reference angle; at π/3: (½, √3/2); quadrant III: both negative; cos(4π/3) = −½; sin(4π/3) = −√3/2.
> Find the cosine and sine of four pi over three. [pause]
> Four pi over three is pi over three past pi, so the reference angle is pi over three. [pause]
> At pi over three the point is one half comma root three over two.
> Four pi over three is in the third quadrant, where both coordinates are negative. [pause]
> So cosine is negative one half, and sine is negative root three over two. [long pause]

## Example 2: A negative angle
**On screen:** "Find sin(−π/4) and cos(−π/4)." Board: −π/4 turns clockwise into quadrant IV; reference angle π/4; at π/4: (√2/2, √2/2); quadrant IV: cos > 0, sin < 0; sin(−π/4) = −√2/2; cos(−π/4) = √2/2.
> Find the sine and cosine of negative pi over four. [pause]
> Negative pi over four turns clockwise, an eighth of a turn, into the fourth quadrant. The reference angle is pi over four. [pause]
> At pi over four, both coordinates are root two over two.
> In the fourth quadrant cosine is positive and sine is negative. [pause]
> So sine of negative pi over four is negative root two over two, and cosine is root two over two. [long pause]

## Example 3: Quadrantal angles
**On screen:** "Find cos(3π) and sin(3π/2)." Board: 3π − 2π = π; the point at π is (−1, 0); cos(3π) = −1; the point at 3π/2 is (0, −1); sin(3π/2) = −1.
> Find the cosine of three pi and the sine of three pi over two. [pause]
> Three pi is a full turn plus pi, so it's coterminal with pi. [pause]
> The point at pi is negative one comma zero. Cosine is the x-coordinate, so cosine of three pi is negative one.
> Three pi over two lands at the bottom of the circle, zero comma negative one. [pause]
> Sine is the y-coordinate, so sine of three pi over two is negative one. [long pause]

## Example 4: From sine to cosine
**On screen:** "sin θ = 3/5 and θ is in quadrant II. Find cos θ and tan θ." Board: cos² θ + sin² θ = 1; cos² θ + 9/25 = 1; cos² θ = 16/25; cos θ = ±4/5; quadrant II: cos θ < 0, so cos θ = −4/5; tan θ = (3/5)/(−4/5) = −3/4.
> The sine of theta is three fifths, and theta is in the second quadrant. Find cosine of theta and tangent of theta. [pause]
> Start from cosine squared plus sine squared equals one. Sine squared is nine twenty-fifths. [pause]
> So cosine squared is one minus nine twenty-fifths: sixteen twenty-fifths.
> Square root: cosine is plus or minus four fifths. In the second quadrant cosine is negative, so cosine of theta is negative four fifths. [pause]
> Tangent is sine over cosine: three fifths over negative four fifths, which is negative three fourths. [long pause]
