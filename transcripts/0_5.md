# 0.5 Trigonometric Identities

## Two formulas, one graph
**On screen:** Axes with π-labeled ticks. y = sin² x is drawn in one color. Then y = (1 − cos 2x)/2 is drawn on top in another color, dashed, and lands exactly on the first curve everywhere. Caption: "different formulas, same function".
> Here are two formulas that look nothing alike: sine squared x, and one minus cosine two x, all over two. [pause]
> Graph them both, and the curves land exactly on top of each other. They're the same function written two ways. [pause]
> An equation that's true for every input is called an identity. Calculus uses identities constantly, to rewrite an expression into a form that's easier to work with. [long pause]

## The Pythagorean identities
**On screen:** "sin² θ + cos² θ = 1" boxed, with the unit circle beside it. Then a division line under it: "÷ cos² θ" gives "tan² θ + 1 = sec² θ"; "÷ sin² θ" gives "1 + cot² θ = csc² θ".
> You've already met the most important identity: sine squared plus cosine squared equals one. It's called the Pythagorean identity, because it's the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle. [pause]
> Divide every term by cosine squared. Sine squared over cosine squared is tangent squared, cosine squared over cosine squared is one, and one over cosine squared is secant squared. So tangent squared plus one equals secant squared. [pause]
> Divide by sine squared instead, and you get one plus cotangent squared equals cosecant squared. [pause]
> The first one is worth knowing by heart. The other two are one division away. [long pause]

## Simplifying an expression
**On screen:** The problem: "Simplify (1 − cos² x)/(sin x cos x)." Board: 1 − cos² x = sin² x; = sin² x/(sin x cos x); cancel one sin x: = sin x/cos x; = tan x.
> Simplify one minus cosine squared x, over sine x cosine x. [pause]
> From the Pythagorean identity, one minus cosine squared is sine squared. [pause]
> So the expression is sine squared over sine times cosine. [pause]
> One factor of sine cancels, leaving sine over cosine.
> And sine over cosine is tangent x. [long pause]

## Even and odd
**On screen:** A unit circle with the angles θ and −θ: mirror images across the x-axis. Their points: (cos θ, sin θ) and (cos θ, −sin θ). Results: "cos(−θ) = cos θ (even)", "sin(−θ) = −sin θ (odd)", "tan(−θ) = −tan θ". Then the cofunction identities with a right triangle whose acute angles are θ and π/2 − θ: "sin(π/2 − θ) = cos θ, cos(π/2 − θ) = sin θ".
> Turning by negative theta is the mirror image of turning by theta, reflected across the x-axis. [pause]
> The reflection keeps the x-coordinate and flips the sign of the y-coordinate. So cosine of negative theta is cosine theta, and sine of negative theta is negative sine theta. Cosine is called even and sine is called odd. [pause]
> In a right triangle, the two acute angles add to pi over two, and the side opposite one is adjacent to the other. So sine of pi over two minus theta is cosine theta. That's where the "co" in cosine comes from: the sine of the complementary angle. [long pause]

## Sum and difference formulas
**On screen:** Two formula boxes: "sin(a ± b) = sin a cos b ± cos a sin b" and "cos(a ± b) = cos a cos b ∓ sin a sin b". A warning in red: "sin(a + b) ≠ sin a + sin b", with the counterexample a = b = π/2: sin π = 0 but sin(π/2) + sin(π/2) = 2.
> Sine of a sum doesn't split into a sum of sines. With a and b both pi over two, sine of a plus b is sine of pi, zero. But sine a plus sine b is two. [pause]
> Instead: sine of a plus b is sine a cosine b plus cosine a sine b. For a minus b, the plus becomes a minus. [pause]
> Cosine of a plus b is cosine a cosine b minus sine a sine b. Notice the sign flips: a sum gives a minus, a difference gives a plus. [pause]
> You won't need to prove these. Know where to find them, and use them to get exact values at angles like pi over twelve. [long pause]

## Double-angle formulas
**On screen:** "Set b = a:" sin(a + a) = sin a cos a + cos a sin a → "sin 2a = 2 sin a cos a". cos(a + a) = cos² a − sin² a → "cos 2a = cos² a − sin² a", then using sin² a = 1 − cos² a: "= 2cos² a − 1", and using cos² a = 1 − sin² a: "= 1 − 2sin² a".
> Set b equal to a in the sum formulas. Sine of a plus a is sine a cosine a plus cosine a sine a, which is two sine a cosine a. So sine two a equals two sine a cosine a. [pause]
> Cosine of a plus a is cosine squared a minus sine squared a. That's cosine two a. [pause]
> Replace sine squared with one minus cosine squared, and cosine two a becomes two cosine squared a minus one. Replace cosine squared instead, and it becomes one minus two sine squared a. Three versions; pick whichever fits the problem. [long pause]

## Power-reducing formulas
**On screen:** "cos 2x = 2cos² x − 1" solved one step per line for cos² x: 2cos² x = 1 + cos 2x; cos² x = (1 + cos 2x)/2. Likewise "cos 2x = 1 − 2sin² x" solved for sin² x: 2sin² x = 1 − cos 2x; sin² x = (1 − cos 2x)/2. The opening graph returns: sin² x and (1 − cos 2x)/2 on top of each other.
> Solve two of those for the square. Start with cosine two x equals two cosine squared x minus one. Add one: two cosine squared x equals one plus cosine two x. Divide by two: cosine squared x is one plus cosine two x, over two. [pause]
> The same steps on cosine two x equals one minus two sine squared x give sine squared x equals one minus cosine two x, over two. [pause]
> That's the identity from the start. These trade a square for a double angle, and in calculus that's exactly what you'll need to integrate sine squared and cosine squared. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "sin² θ + cos² θ = 1; tan² θ + 1 = sec² θ; 1 + cot² θ = csc² θ." "cos(−θ) = cos θ, sin(−θ) = −sin θ." "sin 2a = 2 sin a cos a; cos 2a = cos² a − sin² a = 2cos² a − 1 = 1 − 2sin² a." "cos² x = (1 + cos 2x)/2, sin² x = (1 − cos 2x)/2."
> Sine squared plus cosine squared is one, and dividing it by cosine squared or sine squared gives the other two Pythagorean identities. Cosine is even, sine is odd. Sine two a is two sine a cosine a, cosine two a has three forms, and solving those for the squares gives the power-reducing formulas. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An exact value
**On screen:** "Find the exact value of cos(π/12)." Board: π/12 = π/3 − π/4; cos(a − b) = cos a cos b + sin a sin b; = cos(π/3)cos(π/4) + sin(π/3)sin(π/4); = (½)(√2/2) + (√3/2)(√2/2); = √2/4 + √6/4; = (√6 + √2)/4.
> Find the exact value of cosine of pi over twelve. [pause]
> Pi over twelve is the difference of two landmark angles: pi over three minus pi over four. Four twelfths minus three twelfths. [pause]
> Cosine of a minus b is cosine a cosine b plus sine a sine b. So this is cosine pi over three cosine pi over four, plus sine pi over three sine pi over four.
> Fill in the values: one half times root two over two, plus root three over two times root two over two. [pause]
> That's root two over four plus root six over four: root six plus root two, all over four. [long pause]

## Example 2: Verifying an identity
**On screen:** "Show that sec x − cos x = sin x tan x." Board: start with the left side: sec x − cos x = 1/cos x − cos x; = 1/cos x − cos² x/cos x; = (1 − cos² x)/cos x; = sin² x/cos x; = sin x · (sin x/cos x); = sin x tan x. ✓
> Show that secant x minus cosine x equals sine x tangent x. [pause]
> Work on one side only, usually the messier one, until it turns into the other. Start on the left: secant is one over cosine. [pause]
> To subtract, give cosine x the same denominator: cosine squared over cosine. That makes one minus cosine squared, over cosine.
> One minus cosine squared is sine squared. So we have sine squared x over cosine x. [pause]
> Split off one sine: sine x times sine over cosine. That's sine x tangent x, the right side. [long pause]

## Example 3: Double angles from one value
**On screen:** "sin x = 3/5 and x is in quadrant II. Find sin 2x and cos 2x." Board: cos² x = 1 − 9/25 = 16/25; quadrant II: cos x = −4/5; sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x = 2(3/5)(−4/5) = −24/25; cos 2x = 1 − 2sin² x = 1 − 18/25 = 7/25.
> Sine x is three fifths and x is in the second quadrant. Find sine two x and cosine two x. [pause]
> Both formulas need cosine too. Cosine squared is one minus nine twenty-fifths, sixteen twenty-fifths, and cosine is negative in quadrant two: negative four fifths. [pause]
> Sine two x is two sine x cosine x: two times three fifths times negative four fifths, which is negative twenty-four twenty-fifths.
> For cosine two x, the version with only sine is quickest: one minus two sine squared. That's one minus two times nine twenty-fifths, one minus eighteen twenty-fifths. [pause]
> So cosine two x is seven twenty-fifths. [long pause]

## Example 4: Simplifying with double angles
**On screen:** "Simplify sin 2x/(1 + cos 2x)." Board: sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x; 1 + cos 2x = 1 + (2cos² x − 1) = 2cos² x; = 2 sin x cos x/(2cos² x); = sin x/cos x; = tan x.
> Simplify sine two x over one plus cosine two x. [pause]
> The top is two sine x cosine x. [pause]
> For the bottom, pick the version of cosine two x that cancels the one: two cosine squared x minus one. Then one plus that is two cosine squared x.
> So the fraction is two sine x cosine x over two cosine squared x. [pause]
> The twos cancel and one cosine cancels, leaving sine over cosine: tangent x. [long pause]
