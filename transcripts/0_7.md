# 0.7 Inverse Trigonometric Functions

## Working backward
**On screen:** A wheelchair ramp beside a building entrance: it rises 1 ft over a horizontal run of 12 ft. The angle at the bottom is marked with a question mark, and "tan θ = 1/12" appears. Then "θ = ?".
> A wheelchair ramp rises one foot over a horizontal run of twelve feet. Building codes care about its angle. [pause]
> The tangent of the angle is one twelfth. So far we've gone from angles to ratios. Here we have the ratio and need the angle. [pause]
> Going backward is what the inverse trig functions do. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 0.7 · Inverse Trigonometric Functions." Unit 0 · Trig Review.

## Why the domain is restricted
**On screen:** y = sin x on [−2π, 2π]. The horizontal line y = ½ cuts it at many points, each marked. Then all of the graph fades except the piece from −π/2 to π/2, which is highlighted: on that piece the line y = ½ meets it once.
> To run a function backward, each output has to come from just one input. Sine fails badly: the output one half comes from pi over six, five pi over six, and infinitely many other angles. [pause]
> So we keep only one piece of sine that takes every output once. The standard piece runs from negative pi over two to pi over two. [pause]
> On that piece, sine rises steadily from negative one to one, hitting each value exactly once. [long pause]

## Arcsine
**On screen:** "arcsin x = the angle θ in [−π/2, π/2] with sin θ = x." Inputs: −1 ≤ x ≤ 1. Outputs: −π/2 ≤ θ ≤ π/2. The graph of the restricted sine and its reflection across y = x, labeled y = arcsin x. A unit circle with the right half highlighted: arcsine's answers live there.
> Arcsine undoes that piece. Arcsine of x is the angle between negative pi over two and pi over two whose sine is x. [pause]
> Its inputs are sines, so they run from negative one to one. Its outputs are angles from negative pi over two to pi over two: the right half of the unit circle. [pause]
> As with any inverse, its graph is the reflection of the restricted sine across the line y equals x. [long pause]

## Arccosine and arctangent
**On screen:** Cosine restricted to [0, π] (falling from 1 to −1), and the definition "arccos x: the angle in [0, π] with cos θ = x", with the top half of the unit circle highlighted. Then tangent restricted to (−π/2, π/2) with its asymptotes, and "arctan x: the angle in (−π/2, π/2) with tan θ = x"; its graph has horizontal asymptotes y = ±π/2. A summary table of inputs and outputs.
> Cosine needs a different piece, because on the right half of the circle cosine is never negative. Cosine takes every value once on zero to pi, the top half. So arccosine's outputs run from zero to pi. [pause]
> Tangent takes every real number once between negative pi over two and pi over two. So arctangent accepts any input, and its outputs are strictly between negative pi over two and pi over two. [pause]
> Its graph levels off toward those two values, with horizontal asymptotes at y equals pi over two and negative pi over two. [long pause]

## Evaluating inverse trig functions
**On screen:** The problem: "Find arcsin(−½), arccos(−½), and arctan √3." Board: arcsin(−½): angle in [−π/2, π/2] with sine −½; = −π/6; arccos(−½): angle in [0, π] with cosine −½; = 2π/3; arctan √3: angle in (−π/2, π/2) with tangent √3; = π/3. The unit circle with the three answers marked.
> Find arcsine of negative one half, arccosine of negative one half, and arctangent of root three. [pause]
> Arcsine of negative one half asks for the angle between negative pi over two and pi over two whose sine is negative one half. [pause]
> Sine is one half at pi over six, and sine is odd, so the answer is negative pi over six. [pause]
> Arccosine of negative one half needs an angle from zero to pi. The reference angle is pi over three, and cosine is negative in the second quadrant. [pause]
> So it's pi minus pi over three: two pi over three.
> Arctangent of root three: tangent is root three at pi over three, which is in the allowed range. So it's pi over three. [long pause]

## Notation
**On screen:** "sin⁻¹ x = arcsin x" in large type. Beneath it, struck through in red: "sin⁻¹ x ≠ 1/sin x". Then "1/sin x = csc x = (sin x)⁻¹".
> You'll also see arcsine written sine to the negative one of x. That negative one means inverse function, not reciprocal. [pause]
> Sine to the negative one of x is arcsine. One over sine x is cosecant. [long pause]

## Undoing, within limits
**On screen:** "sin(arcsin x) = x for −1 ≤ x ≤ 1." Then "arcsin(sin(5π/6)) = ?" with the unit circle: 5π/6 is outside [−π/2, π/2]; sin(5π/6) = ½; arcsin(½) = π/6. Result: "arcsin(sin(5π/6)) = π/6, not 5π/6".
> Sine of arcsine of x is always x, for any x from negative one to one. Arcsine gives an angle, and sine takes it right back. [pause]
> The other order can surprise you. Arcsine of sine of five pi over six: sine of five pi over six is one half, and arcsine of one half is pi over six. [pause]
> Arcsine always answers in its own range, so you get pi over six, not five pi over six. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A table: arcsin: inputs [−1, 1], outputs [−π/2, π/2]. arccos: inputs [−1, 1], outputs [0, π]. arctan: inputs all reals, outputs (−π/2, π/2). "sin⁻¹ x means arcsin x, not 1/sin x."
> Arcsine gives an angle from negative pi over two to pi over two, arccosine an angle from zero to pi, and arctangent an angle strictly between negative pi over two and pi over two. The inputs of arcsine and arccosine run from negative one to one; arctangent takes anything. And sine to the negative one means arcsine, not one over sine. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Two more values
**On screen:** "Find arctan(−1) and arccos(−√2/2)." Board: tan(π/4) = 1, and tan is odd: arctan(−1) = −π/4; reference angle π/4, cos < 0 in quadrant II: arccos(−√2/2) = π − π/4 = 3π/4.
> Find arctangent of negative one and arccosine of negative root two over two. [pause]
> Tangent of pi over four is one. Tangent is odd, so tangent of negative pi over four is negative one, and negative pi over four is in arctangent's range. [pause]
> So arctangent of negative one is negative pi over four.
> For arccosine, the reference angle is pi over four, and the answer has to be in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative. [pause]
> Pi minus pi over four is three pi over four. [long pause]

## Example 2: A triangle for a composition
**On screen:** "Find cos(arcsin(2/3))." Board: let θ = arcsin(2/3), so sin θ = 2/3 with θ in [−π/2, π/2]; triangle: opposite 2, hypotenuse 3; adjacent = √(9 − 4) = √5; θ is in quadrant I, so cos θ > 0; cos θ = √5/3. A right triangle with sides 2, √5, 3.
> Find cosine of arcsine of two thirds. [pause]
> Give the inside a name: theta is arcsine of two thirds. That means sine theta is two thirds, and theta is in the first quadrant since two thirds is positive. [pause]
> Draw a right triangle with opposite side two and hypotenuse three. The adjacent side is the square root of nine minus four: root five.
> Theta is in the first quadrant, so its cosine is positive. [pause]
> Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse: root five over three. [long pause]

## Example 3: A composition in terms of x
**On screen:** "Write tan(arccos x) as an algebraic expression, for 0 < x ≤ 1." Board: θ = arccos x, cos θ = x = x/1; triangle: adjacent x, hypotenuse 1; opposite = √(1 − x²); tan θ = opposite/adjacent = √(1 − x²)/x.
> Write tangent of arccosine x without any trig, for x between zero and one. [pause]
> Let theta be arccosine x, so cosine theta is x. Write x as x over one: adjacent side x, hypotenuse one. [pause]
> The opposite side is the square root of one minus x squared.
> Tangent is opposite over adjacent. [pause]
> So tangent of arccosine x is the square root of one minus x squared, over x. [long pause]

## Example 4: Solving with a calculator
**On screen:** "Solve 3 sin x = 1 for 0 ≤ x < 2π, to three decimal places." Board: sin x = 1/3; arcsin(1/3) ≈ 0.340 (radian mode); sine is positive in quadrants I and II; x ≈ 0.340; x ≈ π − 0.340 ≈ 2.802. The unit circle with the line y = 1/3 cutting it twice.
> Solve three sine x equals one for x from zero up to two pi, to three decimal places. [pause]
> Divide by three: sine x is one third. One third isn't a landmark value, so use a calculator in radian mode: arcsine of one third is about point three four zero. [pause]
> That's the first-quadrant solution. But arcsine only ever gives one angle, and sine is also positive in the second quadrant.
> The second-quadrant angle with the same sine is pi minus point three four zero. [pause]
> That's about two point eight zero two. So x is about point three four zero or two point eight zero two. [long pause]
