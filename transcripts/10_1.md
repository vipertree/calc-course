# 10.1 Defining Convergent and Divergent Infinite Series

## Adding forever
**On screen:** A chocolate bar. Half is eaten, then half of what's left, then half of that, and so on; the eaten pieces line up along a number line from 0 to 1: ½, ¼, ⅛, ... Running totals appear: 0.5, 0.75, 0.875, 0.9375. A question: "½ + ¼ + ⅛ + ... = ?"
> Eat half a chocolate bar. Then half of what's left, then half of that, forever. [pause]
> You're adding one half, plus one fourth, plus one eighth, and so on, infinitely many numbers. Can a sum with infinitely many terms have a finite value? [pause]
> The running totals answer the question: they creep up toward one and never pass it. So we say the infinite sum equals one. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.1 · Defining convergent and divergent infinite series." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## Partial sums
**On screen:** Board: a series Σ_{n=1}^∞ aₙ = a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + ...; the nth partial sum Sₙ = a₁ + a₂ + ... + aₙ; "the series converges to S if lim_{n→∞} Sₙ = S"; "otherwise it diverges". Plain words: "add up more and more terms; if the running total settles on a number, that number is the sum".
> An infinite series is a sum of infinitely many terms, a one plus a two plus a three, and so on. [pause]
> You can't add infinitely many numbers directly, so add the first n of them. That's the nth partial sum, S sub n. [pause]
> If the partial sums approach a limit S, the series converges, and its sum is S. If they don't approach a limit, the series diverges. [pause]
> In plain words: keep a running total. If it settles down on a number, that's the sum. [long pause]

## Three ways to behave
**On screen:** Three small graphs of Sₙ against n. First: Sₙ = n for Σ 1, climbing without bound ("diverges to ∞"). Second: Sₙ for Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ jumping 1, 0, 1, 0 ("diverges: no single value"). Third: Sₙ for Σ (½)ⁿ leveling off at 1 ("converges to 1").
> Partial sums can behave in a few ways. For one plus one plus one, they grow without bound: divergent. [pause]
> For one minus one plus one minus one, they bounce between one and zero forever, never settling: also divergent. [pause]
> For one half plus one fourth plus one eighth, they level off at one: convergent, with sum one. [long pause]

## A telescoping series
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the sum of Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(n(n + 1))." Board, one step per line: 1/(n(n + 1)) = 1/n − 1/(n + 1); Sₙ = (1 − ½) + (½ − ⅓) + (⅓ − ¼) + ... + (1/n − 1/(n + 1)); middle terms cancel; Sₙ = 1 − 1/(n + 1); lim_{n→∞} Sₙ = 1; the series converges to 1. The cancelling terms are struck through in pairs.
> Find the sum of one over n times n plus one, from n equals one to infinity. [pause]
> First, a partial fraction trick: one over n times n plus one equals one over n, minus one over n plus one. You can check by combining the fractions. [pause]
> Now write out the partial sum: one minus one half, plus one half minus one third, plus one third minus one fourth, and so on, up to one over n minus one over n plus one. [pause]
> Almost everything cancels in pairs, like a telescope collapsing. Only the first and last pieces survive: S sub n is one minus one over n plus one. [pause]
> As n goes to infinity, one over n plus one goes to zero, so the partial sums approach one. The series converges, and its sum is one. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Sₙ = a₁ + ... + aₙ"; "converges to S if Sₙ → S; otherwise diverges"; "telescoping: terms cancel, leaving the first and last".
> A series converges when its partial sums approach a limit, and that limit is the sum. When the partial sums grow without bound or never settle, the series diverges. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Partial sums that grow
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 2 converge?" Board: Sₙ = 2 + 2 + ... + 2 = 2n; lim 2n = ∞; diverges.
> Does two plus two plus two, forever, converge? [pause]
> The nth partial sum is two n, which grows without bound. The series diverges. [long pause]

## Example 2: A partial sum from a formula
**On screen:** "The nth partial sum of a series is Sₙ = 3n/(n + 1). Does the series converge? If so, to what?" Board: lim_{n→∞} 3n/(n + 1) = 3; converges to 3. Also: a₁ = S₁ = 3/2.
> The nth partial sum of a series is three n over n plus one. Does the series converge? [pause]
> Take the limit as n goes to infinity: the leading terms give three. The partial sums approach three, so the series converges to three. [pause]
> As a bonus, the first term is S sub one: three halves. [long pause]

## Example 3: Another telescoping sum
**On screen:** "Find Σ_{n=1}^∞ (1/n − 1/(n + 2))." Board: Sₙ = (1 − ⅓) + (½ − ¼) + (⅓ − ⅕) + ... ; surviving terms: 1 + ½ − 1/(n + 1) − 1/(n + 2); limit: 3/2.
> Find the sum of one over n minus one over n plus two. [pause]
> Write out the terms. The negative one third cancels with a positive one third two terms later, and so on, but this time the first two positive terms survive: one and one half. [pause]
> At the far end, two negative terms survive, one over n plus one and one over n plus two, and both go to zero. [pause]
> The sum is one plus one half: three halves. [long pause]
