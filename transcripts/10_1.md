# 10.1 Defining Convergent and Divergent Infinite Series

## A bar joke
**On screen:** A wooden bar counter. Pint glasses appear on it one at a time, each holding the order: half a beer, a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth, a thirty-second (painted pint sprites, filled by volume), with ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16, 1/32 above them and "..." after. The bartender (painted sprite) walks in from the right; "Oh, brother." appears above the bartender; a full pint is set down on the counter; the bartender walks off to the right. Then an empty pint in the middle: each small glass slides over and pours into it, and the level rises to ½, ¾, ⅞, 15/16, 31/32 with the running total written above it. A question at the top: "½ + ¼ + ⅛ + ⋯ = ?", then "= 1".
> Infinitely many mathematicians walk into a bar. The first one orders half a beer. The second orders a quarter of a beer. The third orders an eighth, and so on. [pause]
> The bartender says, "Oh, brother," pours one beer, and walks away. [long pause]
> Why is one beer enough? Pour the orders into one glass. Half a beer, then three quarters, then seven eighths. [pause]
> Each order fills half of the room that's left, so the glass gets closer and closer to full and never runs over. [pause]
> One half, plus one fourth, plus one eighth, forever, adds up to exactly one beer. A sum with infinitely many terms can have a finite value. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.1 · Defining convergent and divergent infinite series." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series.

## Separate numbers, added up
**On screen:** Two panels side by side. Left, dimmed: a continuous curve with the area under it shaded, labeled "Units 6 to 8: integrals add up a continuous function". Right: separate bars of heights a₁, a₂, a₃, a₄, a₅ standing at n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, with gaps between them, labeled "Unit 10: separate numbers, added one at a time". The left panel gets a cross over it.
> In the last few units, integrals added up a continuous function: the area under a curve. [pause]
> This unit is different. We are not doing integrals. There is no curve here, just a list of separate numbers, a one, a two, a three, and so on, and we add them up one at a time. [long pause]

## Partial sums
**On screen:** Board: a series Σ_{n=1}^∞ aₙ = a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + ...; the nth partial sum Sₙ = a₁ + a₂ + ... + aₙ; "the series converges to S if lim_{n→∞} Sₙ = S"; "otherwise it diverges". Plain words: "add up more and more terms; if the running total settles on a number, that number is the sum".
> An infinite series is a sum of infinitely many terms, a one plus a two plus a three, and so on. [pause]
> You can't add infinitely many numbers directly, so add the first n of them. That's the nth partial sum, S sub n. [pause]
> If the partial sums approach a limit S, the series converges, and its sum is S. If they don't approach a limit, the series diverges. [pause]
> In plain words: keep a running total. If it settles down on a number, that's the sum. [long pause]

## Three ways to behave
**On screen:** Three small graphs of Sₙ against n. First: Sₙ = n for Σ 1, climbing without bound ("diverges to ∞"). Second: Sₙ for Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹ jumping 1, 0, 1, 0 ("diverges: no single value"). Third: Sₙ for Σ (½)ⁿ leveling off at 1 ("converges to 1").
> Partial sums can behave in a few ways. For one plus one plus one, they grow without bound: divergent. [pause]
> For one minus one plus one minus one, they bounce between one and zero forever, never settling: also divergent. [pause]
> For one half plus one fourth plus one eighth, they level off at one: convergent, with sum one. [long pause]

## The terms settle, the sum doesn't
**On screen:** Two graphs side by side for aₙ = n/(n + 1). Left, "the sequence: the list of terms": dots at ½, ⅔, ¾, ⅘, ... leveling off under a dashed line at 1, labeled "the sequence converges to 1". Right, "the series: the running total": Sₙ = ½, 7/6, 23/12, ... climbing in a nearly straight line, labeled "the series diverges". Under both: "sequence: a list of numbers"; "series: the sum of that list".
> One more case, and it's the one people mix up. Take the terms n over n plus one: one half, two thirds, three fourths, four fifths. [pause]
> Those terms settle down. They get closer and closer to one. The sequence, the list of terms, converges to one. [pause]
> Now add them up. Each new term adds almost a whole one to the total, so the running total keeps climbing, forever. The series, the sum, diverges. [pause]
> A sequence is a list of numbers. A series is what you get when you add that list up. The list can settle down while its sum grows without bound. [long pause]

## A telescoping series
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the sum of Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(n(n + 1))." Board, one step per line: 1/(n(n + 1)) = 1/n − 1/(n + 1); Sₙ = (1 − ½) + (½ − ⅓) + (⅓ − ¼) + ... + (1/n − 1/(n + 1)); middle terms cancel; Sₙ = 1 − 1/(n + 1); lim_{n→∞} Sₙ = 1; the series converges to 1. The cancelling terms are struck through in pairs.
> Find the sum of one over n times n plus one, from n equals one to infinity. [pause]
> First, a partial fraction trick: one over n times n plus one equals one over n, minus one over n plus one. You can check by combining the fractions. [pause]
> Now write out the partial sum: one minus one half, plus one half minus one third, plus one third minus one fourth, and so on, up to one over n minus one over n plus one. [pause]
> Almost everything cancels in pairs, the way a telescope collapses. Only the first and last pieces survive: S sub n is one minus one over n plus one. [pause]
> As n goes to infinity, one over n plus one goes to zero, so the partial sums approach one. The series converges, and its sum is one. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "sequence: a list; series: its sum"; "Sₙ = a₁ + ... + aₙ"; "converges to S if Sₙ → S; otherwise diverges"; "telescoping: terms cancel, leaving the first and last".
> A sequence is a list of numbers, and a series adds them up. A series converges when its partial sums approach a limit, and that limit is the sum. When the partial sums grow without bound or never settle, the series diverges. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Partial sums that grow
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 2 converge?" Board: Sₙ = 2 + 2 + ... + 2 = 2n; lim 2n = ∞; diverges.
> Does two plus two plus two, forever, converge? [pause]
> The nth partial sum is two n, which grows without bound. The series diverges. [long pause]

## Example 2: A partial sum from a formula
**On screen:** "The nth partial sum of a series is Sₙ = 3n/(n + 1). Does the series converge? If so, to what?" (follow along). Board, one step per line: Sₙ = a₁ + a₂ + ⋯ + aₙ = 3n/(n + 1) "(a total, not a term)"; lim_{n→∞} 3n/(n + 1) = lim_{n→∞} 3n/n; = lim_{n→∞} 3 = 3, so the series converges to 3; a₁ = S₁ = 3/2.
> The nth partial sum of a series is three n over n plus one. Does the series converge? [pause]
> Stop and read that carefully. S sub n is the total after adding up the first n terms. So three n over n plus one is a formula for that total. We are not adding three n over n plus one again and again; it already is the sum. [long pause]
> The series converges when that total approaches a limit, so take the limit of three n over n plus one as n goes to infinity. [pause]
> As in earlier units, only the leading terms matter in a limit at infinity. The leading term on top is three n, and on the bottom it's n. So the limit is the same as the limit of three n over n. [pause]
> Three n over n is three, so the limit is three. The partial sums approach three, so the series converges to three. [pause]
> As a bonus, the first term is S sub one: three halves. [long pause]

## Example 3: Another telescoping sum
**On screen:** "Find Σ_{n=1}^∞ (1/n − 1/(n + 2))." Board, one step per line: Sₙ = (1 − ⅓) + (½ − ¼) + (⅓ − ⅕) + ⋯ + (1/(n − 1) − 1/(n + 1)) + (1/n − 1/(n + 2)), the first terms and the last two terms both written out; surviving terms: Sₙ = 1 + ½ − 1/(n + 1) − 1/(n + 2); lim_{n→∞} Sₙ = 1 + ½ − 0 − 0; = 3/2.
> Find the sum of one over n minus one over n plus two. [pause]
> Write out the partial sum. It starts one minus one third, plus one half minus one fourth, plus one third minus one fifth. S sub n has an end, so write the last terms too: one over n minus one, minus one over n plus one, and then one over n, minus one over n plus two. [pause]
> The negative one third cancels with a positive one third two terms later, and so on, but this time the first two positive terms survive: one and one half. [pause]
> At the far end, two negative terms survive, one over n plus one and one over n plus two. So S sub n is one plus one half, minus one over n plus one, minus one over n plus two. [pause]
> As n goes to infinity, both of those fractions go to zero. The sum is one plus one half: three halves. [long pause]
