# 10.10 Alternating Series Error Bound

## How far off is a partial sum?
**On screen:** The frog from 10.7 zigzagging toward the sum. After four jumps it has stopped at S₄. The distance from S₄ to the true sum is shaded, and the next jump, of length b₅, is drawn: it reaches past the sum. Caption: "the next jump always overshoots the target".
> When we stop an alternating series after n terms, the partial sum is an estimate of the true sum. How far off can it be? [pause]
> Watch the frog. Each jump overshoots the target, so the target always lies between where the frog is and where its next jump lands. [pause]
> That means the error is smaller than the next jump. [long pause]

**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.10 · Alternating series error bound." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The error bound
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "If Σ(−1)ⁿ⁺¹bₙ satisfies the alternating series test (bₙ decreasing to 0) with sum S, then |S − Sₙ| ≤ bₙ₊₁." Plain words: "the error is at most the first term you left out". Second line: "S lies between Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁, so the sign of the error matches the first omitted term".
> Here's the bound. For an alternating series whose sizes decrease to zero, the error from stopping after n terms is at most b sub n plus one. [pause]
> In words: the error is no bigger than the first term you left out. [pause]
> And the true sum lies between S sub n and S sub n plus one, so you can also tell whether your estimate is too high or too low: the error has the sign of the first omitted term. [long pause]

## Bounding an error
**On screen:** The problem: "Use S₄ to approximate Σ_{n=1}^∞ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n². Bound the error, and say whether S₄ is an overestimate or an underestimate." Board, one step per line: the series satisfies the AST (1/n² decreases to 0); S₄ = 1 − ¼ + ⅑ − 1/16; = 115/144 ≈ 0.7986; error ≤ b₅ = 1/25 = 0.04; the first omitted term, +1/25, is positive, so S < S₄ + 1/25 and S > S₄: S₄ is an underestimate. (True sum: π²/12 ≈ 0.8225.)
> Use the first four terms to approximate the alternating series of one over n squared. Bound the error, and decide whether the estimate is too big or too small. [try it]
> First, the series satisfies the alternating series test: one over n squared decreases to zero. [pause]
> S four is one, minus a fourth, plus a ninth, minus a sixteenth. With a common denominator of one forty-four, that's one fifteen over one forty-four, about point seven nine eight six. [pause]
> The first term left out is the fifth: plus one over twenty-five, which is point zero four. So the error is at most point zero four. [pause]
> That omitted term is positive, so adding more terms pushes the sum up from S four. S four is an underestimate. [pause]
> The true sum is pi squared over twelve, about point eight two two five. The actual error, about point zero two four, is indeed under point zero four. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "|S − Sₙ| ≤ bₙ₊₁ (the first omitted term)"; "S is between Sₙ and Sₙ₊₁"; "first omitted term positive: Sₙ underestimates; negative: overestimates"; "check the AST conditions first".
> For an alternating series that passes the alternating series test, the error is at most the first omitted term, and its sign tells you whether the partial sum is too high or too low. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: How many terms?
**On screen:** "How many terms of Σ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n are needed to guarantee an error less than 0.001?" Board: error after n terms ≤ 1/(n + 1); need 1/(n + 1) < 0.001; n + 1 > 1000; n ≥ 1000.
> How many terms of the alternating harmonic series guarantee an error less than point zero zero one? [pause]
> After n terms, the error is at most one over n plus one. We need that to be less than point zero zero one. [pause]
> So n plus one must be bigger than a thousand: n at least one thousand. This series converges very slowly. [long pause]

## Example 2: Approximating 1/e
**On screen:** "1/e = Σ_{n=0}^∞ (−1)ⁿ/n!. Approximate 1/e with the terms through n = 4 and bound the error." Board: S = 1 − 1 + ½ − ⅙ + 1/24 = 3/8 = 0.375; error ≤ 1/5! = 1/120 ≈ 0.0083; (1/e ≈ 0.3679).
> One over e equals the alternating series of one over n factorial. Approximate it using the terms through n equals four, and bound the error. [pause]
> One minus one, plus one half, minus one sixth, plus one twenty-fourth: three eighths, point three seven five. [pause]
> The first omitted term is one over five factorial, one over one twenty, about point zero zero eight three. So the error is at most that. [pause]
> One over e is about point three six seven nine: off by about point zero zero seven one. [long pause]

## Example 3: A sine estimate
**On screen:** "sin(0.5) = 0.5 − 0.5³/3! + 0.5⁵/5! − ... . Estimate sin(0.5) with two terms and bound the error." Board: 0.5 − 0.125/6 = 0.479167; error ≤ 0.5⁵/120 = 0.00026; the omitted term is positive: the estimate is low.
> The sine of point five equals an alternating series: point five, minus point five cubed over three factorial, plus point five to the fifth over five factorial, and so on. Estimate it with two terms. [pause]
> Point five minus point one two five over six: about point four seven nine one seven. [pause]
> The first omitted term is point five to the fifth over one twenty, about point zero zero zero two six. That's the error bound, and since the omitted term is positive, the estimate is a little low. [long pause]
