# 10.11 Finding Taylor Polynomial Approximations of Functions

## Better than a tangent line
**On screen:** The graph of y = ln x near x = 1. The tangent line at (1, 0) hugs the curve only very near x = 1. Then a parabola that matches the curve's value, slope, and bending at x = 1 hugs it longer; then a cubic hugs it longer still. Labels: "P₁: matches value and slope", "P₂: also matches concavity", "P₃: also matches f‴".
> The tangent line is the best line near a point: it matches the function's value and slope there. But it peels away from the curve quickly. [pause]
> A parabola can also match how the curve bends: the second derivative. It stays close longer. A cubic can match the third derivative too. [pause]
> Polynomials built to match more and more derivatives at a point are called Taylor polynomials. [long pause]

**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.11 · Finding Taylor polynomial approximations of functions." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The Taylor polynomial formula
**On screen:** Board: Pₙ(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) + f″(a)/2! (x − a)² + f‴(a)/3! (x − a)³ + ... + f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! (x − a)ⁿ, boxed. Below: "the coefficient of (x − a)ᵏ is f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k!"; "centered at a = 0: a Maclaurin polynomial". A small aside showing why the k! appears: differentiating c(x − a)³ three times gives 3! · c.
> The nth-degree Taylor polynomial centered at a starts with f of a, then f prime of a times x minus a, then f double prime of a over two factorial times x minus a squared, and so on up to the nth derivative over n factorial. [pause]
> So the coefficient of x minus a to the k is the kth derivative at a, divided by k factorial. The factorial is there because differentiating x minus a to the k, k times, produces k factorial. [pause]
> When the center is zero, it's called a Maclaurin polynomial. [long pause]

## ln x near 1
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the third-degree Taylor polynomial for f(x) = ln x centered at x = 1, and use it to approximate ln 1.2." A table builds row by row: k | f⁽ᵏ⁾(x) | f⁽ᵏ⁾(1) | coefficient f⁽ᵏ⁾(1)/k!; 0 | ln x | 0 | 0; 1 | 1/x | 1 | 1; 2 | −1/x² | −1 | −½; 3 | 2/x³ | 2 | 2/6 = ⅓. Board: P₃(x) = (x − 1) − ½(x − 1)² + ⅓(x − 1)³; P₃(1.2) = 0.2 − ½(0.04) + ⅓(0.008); = 0.2 − 0.02 + 0.002667 = 0.182667; (ln 1.2 ≈ 0.182322).
> Find the third-degree Taylor polynomial for ln x centered at one, and use it to estimate ln one point two. [try it]
> Make a table of derivatives. F is ln x, and ln one is zero. [pause]
> F prime is one over x, which is one at x equals one. F double prime is negative one over x squared: negative one. F triple prime is two over x cubed: two. [pause]
> Divide each by its factorial: zero, one, negative one half, and two over six, which is one third. [pause]
> So P three is x minus one, minus one half x minus one squared, plus one third x minus one cubed. [pause]
> At one point two, x minus one is point two. That's point two, minus one half of point zero four, plus one third of point zero zero eight: about point one eight two six seven. [pause]
> The true value of ln one point two is about point one eight two three two. Very close. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Pₙ(x) = Σ_{k=0}^n f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k! (x − a)ᵏ"; "coefficient of (x − a)ᵏ: f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k!"; "make a table: derivative, value at a, divide by k!".
> A Taylor polynomial matches a function's derivatives at the center. Make a table of derivatives at the center, divide each by k factorial, and those are the coefficients. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: e to the x
**On screen:** "Find the third-degree Maclaurin polynomial for eˣ, and use it to approximate e^{0.1}." Board: every derivative of eˣ is eˣ, equal to 1 at 0; P₃(x) = 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6; P₃(0.1) = 1 + 0.1 + 0.005 + 0.000167 = 1.105167; (e^{0.1} ≈ 1.105171).
> Find the third-degree Maclaurin polynomial for e to the x, and estimate e to the point one. [pause]
> Every derivative of e to the x is e to the x, which is one at zero. So the coefficients are one over k factorial. [pause]
> P three is one plus x plus x squared over two plus x cubed over six. [pause]
> At point one: one plus point one plus point zero zero five plus about point zero zero zero one six seven. That's about one point one zero five one six seven, very close to the true value. [long pause]

## Example 2: cosine
**On screen:** "Find the fourth-degree Maclaurin polynomial for cos x." A table: derivatives cos, −sin, −cos, sin, cos; values at 0: 1, 0, −1, 0, 1. Board: P₄(x) = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! = 1 − x²/2 + x⁴/24.
> Find the fourth-degree Maclaurin polynomial for cosine x. [pause]
> The derivatives cycle: cosine, negative sine, negative cosine, sine, and back to cosine. At zero they're one, zero, negative one, zero, one. [pause]
> The odd terms vanish. P four is one minus x squared over two plus x to the fourth over twenty-four. [long pause]

## Example 3: From a table of values
**On screen:** "f(2) = 3, f′(2) = −1, f″(2) = 4, f‴(2) = 6. Find the third-degree Taylor polynomial for f about x = 2 and approximate f(2.1)." Board: P₃(x) = 3 − (x − 2) + (4/2!)(x − 2)² + (6/3!)(x − 2)³; = 3 − (x − 2) + 2(x − 2)² + (x − 2)³; P₃(2.1) = 3 − 0.1 + 0.02 + 0.001 = 2.921.
> A function has these values at two: three, negative one, four, and six for f and its first three derivatives. Find the third-degree Taylor polynomial about two, and estimate f of two point one. [pause]
> Divide each derivative by its factorial: three, negative one, four over two is two, and six over six is one. [pause]
> P three is three minus x minus two, plus two x minus two squared, plus x minus two cubed. [pause]
> At two point one: three minus point one plus point zero two plus point zero zero one, which is two point nine two one. [long pause]
