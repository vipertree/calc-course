# 10.12 Lagrange Error Bound

## How good is the approximation?
**On screen:** The graph of eˣ and its third-degree Maclaurin polynomial, nearly on top of each other near 0. A magnifying glass over x = 0.1 shows a tiny gap between them, labeled "error = |f(x) − P₃(x)|". Question: "how big can this gap be, without knowing f(0.1)?"
> A Taylor polynomial is an approximation, and approximations have errors. Near zero, e to the x and its cubic Taylor polynomial are almost indistinguishable, but there is a tiny gap. [pause]
> We'd like to know how big that gap can be, without already knowing the exact value of the function. [pause]
> The Lagrange error bound does exactly that. It works for any Taylor polynomial, not just alternating ones. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.12 · Lagrange error bound." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The bound
**On screen:** Board, boxed: |f(x) − Pₙ(x)| ≤ M/(n + 1)! · |x − a|ⁿ⁺¹, "where M ≥ |f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(z)| for every z between a and x". Labels on the pieces: "M: biggest size of the next derivative", "(n + 1)!: next factorial", "|x − a|ⁿ⁺¹: next power of the distance". Plain words: "it looks like the next term of the Taylor polynomial, with the next derivative replaced by its largest possible size".
> Here's the bound. The error is at most M over n plus one factorial, times the distance from the center, to the n plus one power. [pause]
> M is a number at least as big as the n plus first derivative, anywhere between the center and x. You don't need the exact maximum; any upper bound you can justify works. [pause]
> Notice the shape: it's the next term of the Taylor polynomial, with the next derivative replaced by its worst case. [long pause]

## Bounding the error for e to the 0.1
**On screen:** The problem: "P₃(x) = 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6 approximates eˣ. Use the Lagrange error bound to bound the error in approximating e^{0.1}." Board, one step per line: n = 3, a = 0, x = 0.1; f⁽⁴⁾(z) = eᶻ; for 0 ≤ z ≤ 0.1, eᶻ ≤ e^{0.1} < 1.2, so take M = 1.2; error ≤ 1.2/4! · (0.1)⁴; = 1.2 · 0.0001/24; = 0.000005.
> Use the Lagrange error bound for the cubic Maclaurin polynomial of e to the x, at x equals point one. [pause]
> The degree is three, so we need the fourth derivative: e to the z. [pause]
> Between zero and point one, e to the z is largest at point one, and e to the point one is less than one point two. So M can be one point two. [pause]
> The bound is one point two over four factorial, times point one to the fourth. [pause]
> That's one point two times point zero zero zero one over twenty-four: five millionths. [pause]
> So P three of point one, one point one zero five one six seven, is within five millionths of the true value. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "|f(x) − Pₙ(x)| ≤ M/(n + 1)! · |x − a|ⁿ⁺¹"; "M: an upper bound for |f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾| between a and x"; "same shape as the next Taylor term".
> The Lagrange error bound is the next Taylor term with the next derivative replaced by an upper bound M for its size between the center and x. Justify your M, then compute. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Sine
**On screen:** "Bound the error in approximating sin(0.5) with P₃(x) = x − x³/6." Board: f⁽⁴⁾(x) = sin x, |sin z| ≤ 1, M = 1; error ≤ 1/4! · 0.5⁴ = 0.0625/24 ≈ 0.0026.
> Bound the error in approximating sine of point five with x minus x cubed over six. [pause]
> The fourth derivative of sine is sine, which is never bigger than one in size. So M is one. [pause]
> One over twenty-four, times point five to the fourth, point zero six two five: about point zero zero two six. [long pause]

## Example 2: ln x near 1
**On screen:** "P₃(x) = (x − 1) − ½(x − 1)² + ⅓(x − 1)³ approximates ln x near 1. Bound the error at x = 1.2." Board: f⁽⁴⁾(x) = −6/x⁴; on [1, 1.2], |−6/z⁴| ≤ 6/1⁴ = 6; error ≤ 6/4! · 0.2⁴ = 6 · 0.0016/24 = 0.0004.
> Bound the error in the cubic Taylor polynomial for ln x about one, at x equals one point two. [pause]
> The fourth derivative is negative six over x to the fourth. Its size, six over z to the fourth, is biggest when z is smallest: at z equals one, where it's six. [pause]
> The bound: six over twenty-four, times point two to the fourth. That's point zero zero zero four. [long pause]

## Example 3: A given derivative bound
**On screen:** "f has |f⁽⁴⁾(x)| ≤ 12 for 1 ≤ x ≤ 3. P₃ is the third-degree Taylor polynomial for f about x = 2. Bound |f(2.5) − P₃(2.5)|." Board: M = 12, n = 3, |x − a| = 0.5; error ≤ 12/4! · 0.5⁴ = 12 · 0.0625/24 = 0.03125.
> A function's fourth derivative is never bigger than twelve in size between one and three. Bound the error of its cubic Taylor polynomial about two, at two point five. [pause]
> M is given: twelve. The distance from the center is point five. [pause]
> Twelve over twenty-four, times point five to the fourth: point zero three one two five. On the AP exam, M is often given like this. [long pause]
