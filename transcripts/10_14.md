# 10.14 Finding Taylor or Maclaurin Series for a Function

## From polynomials to series
**On screen:** On the left, the graph of eˣ. Its Maclaurin polynomials P₁ and P₂ are drawn, while the right side recalls 10.11 and 10.12: "a few terms: an estimate", "P₃(0.1) = 1.105167", "e^{0.1} ≈ 1.105171", "error ≤ 0.000005". Then P₄ and P₈, each hugging the curve over a wider stretch. The right side changes to "infinitely many terms:" and eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + ⋯, "for every x". Then the screen clears to a two-row comparison: "10.11 · a Taylor polynomial: a few terms", eˣ ≈ 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3!, "an estimate, with an error"; and "10.14 · the Taylor series: infinitely many terms", eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + ⋯ = Σ xⁿ/n!, "exact, for every x". Last, along the bottom: "eˣ, sin x, cos x, 1/(1 − x)".
> In 10.11, Taylor polynomials were a way to estimate. Take a few terms and you get close: P three of point one was within five millionths of e to the point one, and 10.12 measured how close. [pause]
> Each Taylor polynomial for e to the x hugs the curve over a wider stretch than the last. [pause]
> Now take infinitely many terms. In 10.13 we saw that an infinite power series can converge, and for e to the x, this one converges to e to the x itself, for every x. [pause]
> That's the Taylor series. With infinitely many terms, the estimate becomes an exact formula: e to the x equals the sum of x to the n over n factorial. [pause]
> Taking all the terms gives some elegant formulas for common functions: e to the x, sine, cosine, and one over one minus x. [long pause]

## Four series to know by heart
**On screen:** A chart built row by row, each row with the series written out, the general term, and the interval: "eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + ⋯ = Σ xⁿ/n!, all x"; "sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − ⋯ = Σ (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n + 1)!, all x"; "cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − ⋯ = Σ (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ/(2n)!, all x"; "1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + ⋯ = Σ xⁿ, −1 < x < 1". Memory tips: "sine: odd powers, starts with x"; "cosine: even powers, starts with 1"; "signs alternate for both".
> Four Maclaurin series show up again and again; it pays to know them by heart. [pause]
> E to the x: the sum of x to the n over n factorial, for every x. [pause]
> Sine: the odd powers, starting with x, with alternating signs and odd factorials. Cosine: the even powers, starting with one, alternating signs, even factorials. Both for every x. [pause]
> And one over one minus x: the geometric series, the sum of x to the n, but only for x between negative one and one. [long pause]

## New series from old
**On screen:** The series for eᵘ stays boxed in the top right corner. The problem: "Find the Maclaurin series for e^{−x²}: the first four nonzero terms and the general term." Board, one step per line: eᵘ = 1 + u + u²/2! + u³/3! + ⋯; substitute u = −x²; e^{−x²} = 1 + (−x²) + (−x²)²/2! + (−x²)³/3! + ⋯; = 1 − x² + x⁴/2 − x⁶/6 + ⋯; general term: (−x²)ⁿ/n! = (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ/n!; e^{−x²} = Σ_{n=0}^∞ (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ/n!, all x.
> Find the Maclaurin series for e to the negative x squared. [pause]
> Don't take derivatives: they get messy fast. Instead, start from the series for e to the u, and substitute u equals negative x squared. [pause]
> Replace every u: one, plus negative x squared, plus negative x squared squared over two factorial, plus negative x squared cubed over three factorial. [pause]
> Simplify the signs and powers: one minus x squared plus x to the fourth over two minus x to the sixth over six. [pause]
> The general term is negative x squared to the n over n factorial, which is negative one to the n, x to the two n, over n factorial. Since e to the u works for every u, this works for every x. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Multiply by x
**On screen:** The cosine series stays boxed in the top right corner. "Find the Maclaurin series for x cos x." Board: cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − ⋯; x cos x = x − x³/2! + x⁵/4! − ⋯; = Σ (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n)!.
> Find the Maclaurin series for x times cosine x. [pause]
> Take the cosine series and multiply every term by x: x minus x cubed over two factorial plus x to the fifth over four factorial, and so on. [pause]
> The general term is negative one to the n, x to the two n plus one, over two n factorial. [long pause]

## Example 2: Substitute 3x
**On screen:** The sine series stays boxed in the top right corner. "Find the first three nonzero terms and the general term of the Maclaurin series for sin(3x)." Board: sin u = u − u³/3! + u⁵/5! − ⋯ with u = 3x; = 3x − 27x³/6 + 243x⁵/120 − ⋯; = 3x − (9/2)x³ + (81/40)x⁵ − ⋯; general term (−1)ⁿ(3x)²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n + 1)!.
> Find the first three nonzero terms of the series for sine of three x, and its general term. [pause]
> Substitute u equals three x into the sine series: three x, minus three x cubed over six, plus three x to the fifth over one twenty. [pause]
> Three cubed is twenty-seven and three to the fifth is two forty-three. So: three x, minus nine halves x cubed, plus eighty-one fortieths x to the fifth. [pause]
> The general term is negative one to the n, times three x to the two n plus one, over two n plus one factorial. [long pause]

## Example 3: A Taylor series not at zero
**On screen:** The Taylor series formula stays boxed in the top right corner. "Find the Taylor series for eˣ about x = 1." Board: f⁽ⁿ⁾(x) = eˣ, f⁽ⁿ⁾(1) = e; eˣ = Σ_{n=0}^∞ e(x − 1)ⁿ/n! = e + e(x − 1) + e(x − 1)²/2! + ⋯.
> Find the Taylor series for e to the x about x equals one. [pause]
> Every derivative is e to the x, which equals e at x equals one. [pause]
> So each coefficient is e over n factorial: the series is the sum of e times x minus one to the n, over n factorial. [long pause]
