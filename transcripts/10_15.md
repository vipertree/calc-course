# 10.15 Representing Functions as Power Series

## Calculus on a series
**On screen:** The geometric series 1/(1 − x) = 1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯ in the center. An arrow labeled "d/dx" points to one side, producing 1/(1 − x)² = 1 + 2x + 3x² + ⋯; an arrow labeled "∫" points to the other, producing −ln(1 − x) = x + x²/2 + x³/3 + ⋯.
> A power series is like a polynomial that never ends. And just like a polynomial, you can differentiate it or integrate it one term at a time. [pause]
> Start from the geometric series for one over one minus x. Differentiate every term and you get a series for one over one minus x squared. Integrate every term and you get a series for a logarithm. [pause]
> From a handful of known series, calculus produces many more. [long pause]

## Term by term
**On screen:** Board: if f(x) = Σ cₙ(x − a)ⁿ for |x − a| < R, then f′(x) = Σ n cₙ(x − a)ⁿ⁻¹ and ∫ f(x) dx = C + Σ cₙ(x − a)ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1), boxed. Under it: "same radius R"; "endpoints can change: check them again".
> If f is a power series with radius R, its derivative is the series of derivatives, and its integral is the series of integrals, plus a constant. [pause]
> The radius stays the same. But the behavior at the endpoints can change, so check them again whenever an endpoint matters. [long pause]

## A series for ln(1 + x)
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the Maclaurin series for ln(1 + x) and its interval of convergence." Board, one step per line: 1/(1 + x) = 1 − x + x² − x³ + ⋯ = Σ (−1)ⁿxⁿ, |x| < 1; ln(1 + x) = ∫₀ˣ dt/(1 + t); = ∫₀ˣ (1 − t + t² − t³ + ⋯) dt; = x − x²/2 + x³/3 − x⁴/4 + ⋯; = Σ_{n=0}^∞ (−1)ⁿxⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1); R = 1; x = 1: 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ⋯ converges (AST); x = −1: −(1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯) diverges; interval (−1, 1].
> Find the Maclaurin series for ln of one plus x, and where it converges. [try it]
> Start with one over one plus x. That's the geometric series with ratio negative x: one minus x plus x squared minus x cubed, for x between negative one and one. [pause]
> Ln of one plus x is the integral of one over one plus t, from zero to x. The constant is zero, because ln one is zero. [pause]
> Integrate term by term: x minus x squared over two plus x cubed over three minus x to the fourth over four, and so on. [pause]
> The general term is negative one to the n, x to the n plus one, over n plus one. The radius is still one. [pause]
> Now the endpoints. At x equals one, the series is the alternating harmonic series: it converges. At x equals negative one, every term is negative and it's the harmonic series: it diverges. [pause]
> So the interval is from negative one, not included, to one, included. Integrating gained an endpoint that the geometric series didn't have. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "differentiate or integrate term by term"; "radius unchanged; recheck endpoints"; "use a known series (often geometric) as the starting point; fix the constant with a known value".
> Differentiate or integrate a power series term by term. The radius doesn't change, but recheck the endpoints. Start from a series you know, often a geometric one, and find any constant of integration from a known value of the function. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Arctangent
**On screen:** "Find the Maclaurin series for arctan x." Board: 1/(1 + t²) = Σ (−1)ⁿt²ⁿ, |t| < 1; arctan x = ∫₀ˣ dt/(1 + t²); = Σ (−1)ⁿx²ⁿ⁺¹/(2n + 1) = x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − ⋯; interval [−1, 1].
> Find the Maclaurin series for arctangent x. [pause]
> Its derivative is one over one plus x squared, a geometric series with ratio negative x squared. [pause]
> Integrate from zero to x, where arctangent zero is zero: x minus x cubed over three plus x to the fifth over five, and so on. [pause]
> Both endpoints converge by the alternating series test, so the interval is from negative one to one, closed. At x equals one this gives pi over four. [long pause]

## Example 2: Differentiating the geometric series
**On screen:** "Find a power series for 1/(1 − x)²." Board: 1/(1 − x) = Σ xⁿ; d/dx: 1/(1 − x)² = Σ_{n=1}^∞ n xⁿ⁻¹ = 1 + 2x + 3x² + ⋯, |x| < 1.
> Find a power series for one over one minus x, squared. [pause]
> That's the derivative of one over one minus x. Differentiate the geometric series term by term: one plus two x plus three x squared, and so on. [pause]
> The general term is n x to the n minus one, and the radius is still one. [long pause]

## Example 3: Summing a series with calculus
**On screen:** "Find Σ_{n=1}^∞ n/2ⁿ." Board: Σ n xⁿ⁻¹ = 1/(1 − x)², so Σ n xⁿ = x/(1 − x)²; at x = ½: Σ n/2ⁿ = (½)/(½)² = 2.
> Find the sum of n over two to the n. [pause]
> Multiply the previous series by x: the sum of n x to the n equals x over one minus x squared. [pause]
> Plug in x equals one half, which is inside the interval: one half over one quarter, which is two. [long pause]
