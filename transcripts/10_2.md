# 10.2 Working with Geometric Series

## A bouncing ball
**On screen:** A ball dropped from 10 feet bounces, each bounce reaching 60% of the previous height: 10, 6, 3.6, 2.16, ... The heights are listed, each one 0.6 times the one before. A question: "total distance traveled?"
> Drop a ball from ten feet. Each bounce reaches sixty percent of the height before: six feet, then three point six, then two point one six, forever. [pause]
> Each term is the previous one times the same number, point six. A series like that is called geometric. [pause]
> Geometric series are the one kind of infinite series whose sums we can always find exactly. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.2 · Working with geometric series." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The partial sum formula
**On screen:** Board: a geometric series a + ar + ar² + ... with first term a and ratio r. Sₙ = a + ar + ... + arⁿ⁻¹; rSₙ = ar + ar² + ... + arⁿ; subtract: Sₙ − rSₙ = a − arⁿ; Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r). The matching terms in the two lines are struck through as they cancel.
> A geometric series starts with a first term a, and each term is multiplied by the ratio r to get the next. [pause]
> Write the partial sum S sub n. Then multiply everything by r: r S sub n is the same list, shifted over by one. [pause]
> Subtract. Everything cancels except the first term of S sub n and the last term of r S sub n: a minus a r to the n. [pause]
> Solve for S sub n: a times one minus r to the n, over one minus r. [long pause]

## When it converges
**On screen:** Board: if |r| < 1, rⁿ → 0, so Sₙ → a/(1 − r); boxed: "Σ arⁿ converges to a/(1 − r) when |r| < 1, diverges when |r| ≥ 1". Plain words: "first term over one minus the ratio". Three small sketches of rⁿ: r = ½ shrinking to 0, r = 1 constant, r = 2 growing.
> Now let n go to infinity. If the ratio is between negative one and one, r to the n shrinks to zero, and the partial sums approach a over one minus r. [pause]
> If the ratio is one or more in size, r to the n doesn't shrink, and the series diverges. [pause]
> So a geometric series converges exactly when the absolute value of r is less than one, and then its sum is the first term over one minus the ratio. [long pause]

## Summing a geometric series
**On screen:** The problem: "Find Σ_{n=0}^∞ 3(2/5)ⁿ." Board, one step per line: first term (n = 0): 3(2/5)⁰ = 3; ratio r = 2/5; |r| < 1, so it converges; sum = 3/(1 − 2/5); = 3/(3/5); = 5.
> Find the sum of three times two fifths to the n, from n equals zero to infinity. [try it]
> The first term comes from n equals zero: three times one, three. [pause]
> Each term is two fifths of the one before, so the ratio is two fifths. Its absolute value is less than one: the series converges. [pause]
> The sum is the first term over one minus the ratio: three over three fifths, which is five. [long pause]

## Watch the starting index
**On screen:** Σ_{n=1}^∞ (1/2)ⁿ: first term ½, sum ½/(1 − ½) = 1. Σ_{n=0}^∞ (1/2)ⁿ: first term 1, sum 2. Labels: "same ratio, different first term". A warning: "a is the first term actually in the sum, not always the coefficient".
> Be careful with the starting index. The sum of one half to the n starting at n equals one has first term one half, and its sum is one. [pause]
> Starting at n equals zero, the first term is one, and the sum is two. Same ratio, different first term. [pause]
> Always find a by plugging in the first value of n. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "a + ar + ar² + ⋯ converges iff |r| < 1"; "sum = a/(1 − r) = first term/(1 − ratio)"; "find a from the first index".
> A geometric series converges when the ratio has absolute value less than one, and then its sum is the first term over one minus the ratio. Find the first term from the starting index. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A negative ratio
**On screen:** "Find Σ_{n=1}^∞ (−1/3)ⁿ." Board: a = −1/3, r = −1/3, |r| < 1; sum = (−1/3)/(1 + 1/3) = (−1/3)/(4/3) = −1/4.
> Find the sum of negative one third to the n, starting at n equals one. [pause]
> The first term is negative one third, and so is the ratio. Its absolute value is less than one. [pause]
> Negative one third over one plus one third: negative one third over four thirds, negative one fourth. [long pause]

## Example 2: A ratio too big
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=0}^∞ 2(3/2)ⁿ converge?" Board: r = 3/2, |r| ≥ 1: diverges (terms grow).
> Does two times three halves to the n converge? [pause]
> The ratio is three halves, bigger than one. The terms grow, so the series diverges. [long pause]

## Example 3: A repeating decimal
**On screen:** "Write 0.777... as a fraction." Board: 0.777... = 7/10 + 7/100 + 7/1000 + ...; a = 7/10, r = 1/10; sum = (7/10)/(9/10) = 7/9.
> Write zero point seven repeating as a fraction. [pause]
> It's seven tenths plus seven hundredths plus seven thousandths, and so on: geometric with first term seven tenths and ratio one tenth. [pause]
> Seven tenths over nine tenths: seven ninths. [long pause]
