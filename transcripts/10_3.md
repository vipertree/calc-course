# 10.3 The nth Term Test for Divergence

## Orders that stay big
**On screen:** The soda fountain counter from 10.1. Six glasses appear on it one at a time, holding 1, ⅔, ⅗, 4/7, 5/9, 6/11 of a cola (painted glasses, filled by volume), each fraction above its glass, then "...". Above the row: "the sequence (each order): 1, ⅔, ⅗, 4/7, ... → ½". To the right, a running tab ticks up with each glass: 1, 1.67, 2.27, 2.84, 3.39, 3.94, labeled "the series (the total)". Then "→ ∞" after the tab. Caption: "if the terms don't shrink to zero, the total can't settle".
> Back to the soda fountain. This time the first mathematician orders a whole cola, the second two thirds of a cola, the third three fifths, the fourth four sevenths. [pause]
> The orders shrink, but they head toward half a cola, never toward zero. Each new order adds about half a cola to the tab, so the total keeps growing, with no end. [pause]
> The list of orders is the sequence. The running total is the series. The sequence settles at one half, and the series grows forever. [pause]
> That's the idea behind the simplest test for series: if the terms don't shrink to zero, the sum can't settle down. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.3 · The nth term test for divergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series.

## Sequence or series
**On screen:** Two columns side by side. Left, "sequence: a list": a₁, a₂, a₃, ...; ½, ¼, ⅛, ...; "converges to 0". Right, "series: a sum": a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + ⋯; ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + ⋯; "converges to 1". A dividing line between them.
> Two words to keep straight. A sequence is a list of numbers: a one, a two, a three, and so on. A series adds that list up: a one plus a two plus a three. [pause]
> The sequence one half, one fourth, one eighth converges to zero. The series, one half plus one fourth plus one eighth, converges to one. Same numbers, two different questions. [pause]
> The nth term test looks at the sequence of terms and uses it to say something about the series. [long pause]

## The test
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "If lim_{n→∞} aₙ ≠ 0 (or does not exist), then Σ aₙ diverges." Braces under the pieces: "the sequence" under lim aₙ, "the series" under Σ aₙ. Under it, the reason: "if Σ aₙ converges to S, then aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁ → S − S = 0". Then a red warning: "If lim aₙ = 0, the test says nothing."
> Here's the test. If the sequence of terms a sub n doesn't approach zero, either because the limit is some other number or because it doesn't exist, then the series diverges. [pause]
> Why? If the series converged to S, then each term is the difference of two partial sums, S sub n minus S sub n minus one. Both approach S, so their difference approaches zero. [pause]
> The test only works one way. If the terms do approach zero, the test tells you nothing. The series might converge, or it might not. [long pause]

## Using the test
**On screen:** The problem: "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ n/(2n + 1) converge or diverge?" Board, one step per line: aₙ = n/(2n + 1); lim_{n→∞} n/(2n + 1) = ½ (divide top and bottom by n); ½ ≠ 0; by the nth term test, the series diverges.
> Does the series of n over two n plus one converge or diverge? [pause]
> Look at the terms: n over two n plus one. [pause]
> As n goes to infinity, divide top and bottom by n: one over two plus one over n, which approaches one half. [pause]
> The terms approach one half, not zero. So by the nth term test, the series diverges. Adding about one half infinitely many times can't settle down. [long pause]

## Zero isn't enough
**On screen:** Two graphs of partial sums Sₙ. Σ 1/n: "sequence 1/n → 0; series diverges (10.5)", a slowly rising graph. Σ 1/n²: "sequence 1/n² → 0; series converges", leveling off near 1.645. Caption: "sequence → 0: the nth term test is silent; use another test".
> The test can't go the other way. The sequence one over n shrinks to zero, yet the series diverges, very slowly. We'll prove that in topic 10.5. [pause]
> The sequence one over n squared also shrinks to zero, and that series converges. [pause]
> Same limit, opposite outcomes. When the terms go to zero, you need a different test. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "sequence: a list; series: its sum"; "lim aₙ ≠ 0 or does not exist: Σ aₙ diverges"; "lim aₙ = 0: inconclusive"; "check it first; it's quick".
> A sequence is a list, and a series is its sum. If the sequence of terms doesn't go to zero, the series diverges. If they do go to zero, the nth term test is inconclusive. It's a quick first check before trying anything harder. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An oscillating series
**On screen:** The test stays boxed in the top right corner through every worked example. "Does Σ (−1)ⁿ converge?" Board: aₙ = (−1)ⁿ alternates −1, 1, −1, ...; lim aₙ does not exist; diverges.
> Does the series of negative one to the n converge? [pause]
> The terms bounce between negative one and one, so their limit doesn't exist. By the nth term test, the series diverges. [long pause]

## Example 2: A limit that's e
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ (1 + 1/n)ⁿ converge?" Board: lim (1 + 1/n)ⁿ = e ≠ 0; diverges.
> Does the series of one plus one over n, to the n, converge? [pause]
> That's the famous limit that equals e, about two point seven one eight. Not zero, so the series diverges. [long pause]

## Example 3: When the test is silent
**On screen:** "What does the nth term test say about Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/√n?" Board: lim 1/√n = 0; the test is inconclusive; (it diverges: a p-series with p = ½, topic 10.5).
> What does the nth term test say about one over root n? [pause]
> The terms go to zero, so the test says nothing at all. [pause]
> In fact this series diverges, but we need the p-series test from topic 10.5 to show it. Never write "converges by the nth term test". [long pause]
