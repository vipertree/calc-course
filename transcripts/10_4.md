# 10.4 Integral Test for Convergence

## Rectangles and a curve
**On screen:** The graph of a decreasing positive function f(x) for x ≥ 1. Rectangles of width 1 with heights f(1), f(2), f(3), ... are drawn, their total area equal to the series Σ f(n). The curve passes through the top corners. Caption: "series = area of rectangles; integral = area under the curve".
> Here's a decreasing, positive function, and rectangles of width one whose heights are f of one, f of two, f of three, and so on. [pause]
> The total area of the rectangles is the series: the sum of f of n. The area under the curve is an improper integral. [pause]
> When the rectangles and the curve hug each other this closely, either both areas are finite or both are infinite. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.4 · Integral test for convergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The test and why it works
**On screen:** Two pictures. Left: rectangles starting at x = 1 hanging below the curve (right endpoints), so Σ_{n=2}^∞ f(n) ≤ ∫₁^∞ f(x) dx. Right: rectangles poking above the curve (left endpoints), so ∫₁^∞ f(x) dx ≤ Σ_{n=1}^∞ f(n). Boxed statement: "If f is positive, continuous, and decreasing for x ≥ 1, and aₙ = f(n), then Σ aₙ and ∫₁^∞ f(x) dx both converge or both diverge."
> Shift the rectangles one way, and they sit under the curve: so if the integral is finite, the series is too. [pause]
> Shift them the other way, and they cover the curve: so if the integral is infinite, the series is too. [pause]
> That's the integral test. If f is positive, continuous, and decreasing, the series of f of n and the integral of f from one to infinity either both converge or both diverge. [pause]
> Check all three conditions out loud before using it. And the test tells you whether the series converges, not what it adds up to. [long pause]

## Using the integral test
**On screen:** The problem: "Use the integral test to decide whether Σ_{n=1}^∞ n/(n² + 1) converges." Board, one step per line: f(x) = x/(x² + 1); positive for x ≥ 1; continuous; f′(x) = (1 − x²)/(x² + 1)² ≤ 0 for x ≥ 1, so decreasing; ∫₁^∞ x/(x² + 1) dx = lim_{b→∞} ½ln(x² + 1)|₁^b; = lim_{b→∞} ½[ln(b² + 1) − ln 2] = ∞; the integral diverges, so the series diverges.
> Use the integral test on the series of n over n squared plus one. [try it]
> Let f of x be x over x squared plus one. Check the conditions. It's positive for x at least one, and it's continuous. [pause]
> Decreasing: the quotient rule gives f prime equal to one minus x squared, over x squared plus one squared. For x at least one, the top is zero or negative, so f is decreasing. [pause]
> Now the integral. Substitute u equals x squared plus one: the antiderivative is one half ln of x squared plus one. [pause]
> From one to b, that's one half ln of b squared plus one, minus one half ln two. As b goes to infinity, ln of b squared plus one grows without bound. [pause]
> The integral diverges, so by the integral test, the series diverges too. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "f positive, continuous, decreasing; aₙ = f(n)"; "Σ aₙ and ∫ f(x) dx behave the same"; "the integral's value is not the series' sum".
> If f is positive, continuous, and decreasing, the series of f of n behaves exactly like the integral of f: both converge or both diverge. Check the conditions, and remember the integral's value is not the sum of the series. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: One over n squared
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/n² converge?" Board: f(x) = 1/x² is positive, continuous, decreasing for x ≥ 1; ∫₁^∞ x⁻² dx = lim_{b→∞} [−1/x]₁^b = 1; converges; (the sum is π²/6 ≈ 1.645, not 1).
> Does the series of one over n squared converge? [pause]
> One over x squared is positive, continuous, and decreasing for x at least one. [pause]
> Its integral from one to infinity is negative one over x, from one to b: as b goes to infinity, that's one. The integral converges, so the series converges. [pause]
> The series' sum is actually pi squared over six, about one point six four five. Not one. The test only tells you convergence. [long pause]

## Example 2: An exponential times n
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ n e^{−n²} converge?" Board: f(x) = xe^{−x²}: positive, continuous; f′ = e^{−x²}(1 − 2x²) < 0 for x ≥ 1; ∫₁^∞ xe^{−x²} dx = lim [−½e^{−x²}]₁^b = 1/(2e); converges.
> Does the series of n times e to the negative n squared converge? [pause]
> The function x e to the negative x squared is positive and continuous, and its derivative, e to the negative x squared times one minus two x squared, is negative for x at least one. [pause]
> The integral: negative one half e to the negative x squared, from one to b. As b grows, that approaches one over two e. [pause]
> The integral converges, so the series converges. [long pause]

## Example 3: n ln n
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=2}^∞ 1/(n ln n) converge?" Board: f(x) = 1/(x ln x): positive, continuous, decreasing for x ≥ 2; ∫₂^∞ dx/(x ln x), u = ln x: = lim [ln(ln x)]₂^b = ∞; diverges.
> Does the series of one over n ln n, starting at n equals two, converge? [pause]
> The function is positive, continuous, and decreasing for x at least two, since its denominator grows. [pause]
> Substitute u equals ln x: the antiderivative is ln of ln x, which grows without bound, even if very slowly. [pause]
> The integral diverges, so the series diverges. [long pause]
