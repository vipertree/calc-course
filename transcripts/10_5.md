# 10.5 Harmonic Series and p-Series

## The harmonic series grows forever
**On screen:** The terms 1, ½, ⅓, ¼, ... written out, then grouped: 1 + ½ + (⅓ + ¼) + (⅕ + ⅙ + ⅐ + ⅛) + ... Under each group, "≥ ½". The partial sums S₁, S₂, S₄, S₈, S₁₆ are shown as at least 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3.
> The harmonic series is one plus one half plus one third plus one fourth, and so on. Its terms shrink to zero. Does it converge? [pause]
> Group the terms: one third and one fourth together are at least two fourths, a half. The next four terms, from one fifth to one eighth, are each at least one eighth, so together they're at least a half. The next eight terms are at least a half too. [pause]
> So you keep adding at least one half, forever. The partial sums grow without bound: the harmonic series diverges, even though its terms go to zero. [long pause]

## The p-series test
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/nᵖ converges if p > 1 and diverges if p ≤ 1". Under it, the integral test reason: ∫₁^∞ x⁻ᵖ dx = [x^{1−p}/(1 − p)]₁^∞ is finite exactly when p > 1 (and ∫ 1/x dx = ln x → ∞ when p = 1). A number line for p with the cut at 1: "diverges" to the left (including 1), "converges" to the right.
> A p-series is the sum of one over n to the p, for a constant p. The harmonic series is the case p equals one. [pause]
> The integral test settles every p-series at once. The integral of x to the negative p from one to infinity is finite exactly when p is greater than one. [pause]
> So a p-series converges when p is greater than one and diverges when p is one or less. The dividing line is p equals one, and p equals one itself diverges. [long pause]

## Classifying p-series
**On screen:** The problem: "Classify each series: (a) Σ 1/n^{1.01} (b) Σ 1/√n (c) Σ 1/n^π (d) Σ n^{−0.99}." Board: (a) p = 1.01 > 1: converges; (b) 1/√n = 1/n^{1/2}, p = ½ ≤ 1: diverges; (c) p = π > 1: converges; (d) n^{−0.99} = 1/n^{0.99}, p = 0.99 ≤ 1: diverges. The number line for p fills in with a dot for each.
> Classify each of these series. [pause]
> The first has p equals one point zero one, just past one: converges. [pause]
> One over root n is one over n to the one half. P is one half: diverges. [pause]
> One over n to the pi: p is pi, more than one, converges. [pause]
> N to the negative point nine nine is one over n to the point nine nine: just short of one, so it diverges. [pause]
> Being close to one doesn't matter. Only which side of one you're on. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "harmonic Σ 1/n diverges"; "Σ 1/nᵖ: converges if p > 1, diverges if p ≤ 1"; "rewrite roots and negative exponents to read off p".
> The harmonic series diverges. A p-series converges exactly when p is greater than one. Rewrite roots and negative powers so you can read p off directly. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A constant multiple
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 3/n⁴ converge?" Board: 3 Σ 1/n⁴; p = 4 > 1: converges (a constant multiple doesn't change convergence).
> Does the series of three over n to the fourth converge? [pause]
> It's three times a p-series with p equals four. A constant multiple doesn't change whether a series converges, so it converges. [long pause]

## Example 2: Simplify first
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ n²/n^{3.5} converge?" Board: n²/n^{3.5} = 1/n^{1.5}; p = 1.5 > 1: converges.
> Does the series of n squared over n to the three point five converge? [pause]
> Simplify: subtract exponents to get one over n to the one point five. That's a p-series with p bigger than one, so it converges. [long pause]

## Example 3: A cube root
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/∛(n²) converge?" Board: ∛(n²) = n^{2/3}; p = 2/3 ≤ 1: diverges.
> Does one over the cube root of n squared converge? [pause]
> The cube root of n squared is n to the two thirds. P is two thirds, less than one, so it diverges. [long pause]
