# 10.6 Comparison Tests for Convergence

## Smaller than something finite
**On screen:** Two rows of bars, one for bₙ = 1/n² and, inside each, a shorter bar for aₙ = 1/(n² + 3). The total of the taller bars is labeled "finite". Caption: "if the bigger series converges, the smaller one does too". Then a second pair: bars for 1/n and taller bars for (ln n)/n, with "infinite" under 1/n: "if the smaller series diverges, the bigger one does too".
> Suppose every term of one positive series is smaller than the matching term of another. If the bigger series has a finite sum, the smaller one can't be infinite. [pause]
> And if the smaller series adds up to infinity, the bigger one must too. [pause]
> That's comparison: decide a hard series by lining it up against one you already know, usually a p-series or a geometric series. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.6 · Comparison tests for convergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## Direct comparison
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "If 0 ≤ aₙ ≤ bₙ for all n (from some point on): Σ bₙ converges ⟹ Σ aₙ converges; Σ aₙ diverges ⟹ Σ bₙ diverges." Plain words: "smaller than convergent: converges; bigger than divergent: diverges". A warning: "smaller than divergent, or bigger than convergent: no conclusion".
> The direct comparison test: if zero is at most a sub n, which is at most b sub n, then convergence of the bigger series gives convergence of the smaller. And divergence of the smaller gives divergence of the bigger. [pause]
> In plain words: smaller than something convergent converges; bigger than something divergent diverges. [pause]
> The other two combinations tell you nothing. Being smaller than a divergent series, or bigger than a convergent one, settles nothing. [long pause]

## A direct comparison
**On screen:** The problem: "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(n² + 3) converge?" Board: n² + 3 > n², so 1/(n² + 3) < 1/n²; Σ 1/n² converges (p = 2); by direct comparison, Σ 1/(n² + 3) converges.
> Does the series of one over n squared plus three converge? [try it]
> For large n, the plus three barely matters, so compare with one over n squared. Since n squared plus three is bigger than n squared, one over it is smaller. [pause]
> The series of one over n squared converges: it's a p-series with p equals two. [pause]
> Our series is smaller than a convergent series, so it converges by direct comparison. [long pause]

## Limit comparison
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "If aₙ, bₙ > 0 and lim aₙ/bₙ = c with 0 < c < ∞, then Σ aₙ and Σ bₙ both converge or both diverge." Then the problem: "Does Σ (2n + 1)/(n³ − n + 5) converge?" Board: choose bₙ = n/n³ = 1/n² (dominant terms); lim [(2n + 1)/(n³ − n + 5)] / (1/n²) = lim (2n³ + n²)/(n³ − n + 5) = 2; 0 < 2 < ∞ and Σ 1/n² converges, so the series converges.
> Sometimes the inequality goes the wrong way or is messy to prove. Then use limit comparison: if the ratio a sub n over b sub n approaches a positive, finite number, the two series behave the same. [pause]
> Try it on two n plus one over n cubed minus n plus five. Keep only the biggest power on top and on bottom: n over n cubed, which is one over n squared. That's b sub n. [pause]
> The ratio: multiply by n squared, getting two n cubed plus n squared over n cubed minus n plus five. The limit is two. [pause]
> Two is positive and finite, and one over n squared converges, so our series converges too. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "direct: smaller than convergent converges; bigger than divergent diverges"; "limit: lim aₙ/bₙ = c, 0 < c < ∞: same behavior"; "compare with p-series or geometric series; pick bₙ from the dominant terms".
> Compare with a series you know. Direct comparison needs the right inequality; limit comparison just needs the ratio to approach a positive, finite number. Build b sub n from the dominant terms. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Bigger than harmonic
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=3}^∞ (ln n)/n converge?" Board: for n ≥ 3, ln n > 1, so (ln n)/n > 1/n; Σ 1/n diverges; by direct comparison, diverges.
> Does the series of ln n over n, starting at three, converge? [pause]
> For n at least three, ln n is bigger than one, so ln n over n is bigger than one over n. [pause]
> The harmonic series diverges, and ours is bigger, so it diverges too. [long pause]

## Example 2: A bounded numerator
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ sin²n/n² converge?" Board: 0 ≤ sin²n ≤ 1, so 0 ≤ sin²n/n² ≤ 1/n²; Σ 1/n² converges; converges by direct comparison.
> Does the series of sine squared n over n squared converge? [pause]
> Sine squared is between zero and one, so each term is between zero and one over n squared. [pause]
> Smaller than a convergent p-series: it converges. [long pause]

## Example 3: Limit comparison with a geometric series
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(2ⁿ − 1) converge?" Board: bₙ = 1/2ⁿ; lim [1/(2ⁿ − 1)]/(1/2ⁿ) = lim 2ⁿ/(2ⁿ − 1) = 1; Σ 1/2ⁿ converges (geometric); so the series converges. (Direct comparison fails: 1/(2ⁿ − 1) > 1/2ⁿ.)
> Does the series of one over two to the n minus one converge? [pause]
> Compare with one over two to the n. Direct comparison goes the wrong way here: our terms are a bit bigger. [pause]
> So take the limit of the ratio: two to the n over two to the n minus one, which approaches one. [pause]
> One is positive and finite, and the geometric series converges, so ours converges too. [long pause]
