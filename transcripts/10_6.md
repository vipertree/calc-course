# 10.6 Comparison Tests for Convergence

## A ceiling and a floor
**On screen:** Two graphs side by side, each term its own mark. Left, n = 1 to 10: our series aₙ = 1/(n² + 3) as thin filled bars, one per n. Above each bar, a dot for the known series bₙ = 1/n², with a faint dashed line through the dots as a guide. Labels above the graph: "known: Σ 1/n² is finite" and "ours: aₙ = 1/(n² + 3)". Caption under it: "under a finite ceiling: finite". Right, n = 4 to 15: our series (ln n)/n as bars, rising above dots for the harmonic series 1/n, which have their own faint dashed guide. Labels: "known: Σ 1/n is infinite" and "ours: aₙ = (ln n)/n". Caption: "over an infinite floor: infinite". The dashed lines are only guides; each series is its separate terms.
> Here's our series, one bar for each term. Above it is a series we already know, one over n squared, drawn as dots with a faint dashed line through them. The line is only a guide: each series is its separate terms. [pause]
> Every one of our terms sits under the known one, so the known series is a ceiling. Its total is finite, so ours can't add up to infinity. [pause]
> Now flip it. Here the known series is the harmonic series, one over n, which adds up to infinity. Every one of our terms, ln n over n, sits above it, so it's a floor. Ours adds up to infinity too. [pause]
> That's comparison: decide a hard series by lining it up against one you already know, usually a p-series or a geometric series. [long pause]

## Direct comparison
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "0 ≤ aₙ ≤ bₙ (from some point on): Σ bₙ converges, so Σ aₙ converges; Σ aₙ diverges, so Σ bₙ diverges." Plain words beneath, one line at a time: "smaller than something small (convergent): small, so it converges"; "bigger than something big (divergent): big, so it diverges".
> The direct comparison test: suppose a sub n is between zero and b sub n. If the bigger series, the sum of b sub n, converges, then the smaller series converges too. If the smaller series diverges, then the bigger one diverges too. [pause]
> Here's how to think about it. Call a convergent series small and a divergent series big. If your series is smaller than something known to be small, it's small: it converges. [pause]
> If your series is bigger than something known to be big, it's big: it diverges. [long pause]

## When it settles nothing
**On screen:** Four rows. The two from before, with checks: "smaller than small: converges"; "bigger than big: diverges". Then, in red with a cross, one at a time: "bigger than small: tells you nothing"; "smaller than big: tells you nothing". A red box closes around the last two: "settles nothing".
> Now the other two cases, and they're the trap. Knowing your series is bigger than something small tells you nothing. [long pause]
> Knowing your series is smaller than something big tells you nothing either. [long pause]
> Those two cases settle nothing. If your comparison comes out one of those ways, pick a different series to compare with, or a different test. [long pause]

Then the person, car and lamppost fade, and an arrow shoots up from the ground off toward the top, labeled "infinitely tall"; beside it: "smaller than this: no bound at all" and "bigger than something small: could still be anything".
> Here's a way to remember it. If you know a building is bigger than the Empire State Building, you know it's huge. [pause]
> If you know something is smaller than the Empire State Building, you don't know much about its size. It could be a person, a car, or a lamppost. [pause]
> At least that gives you a finite bound: it's under one thousand four hundred fifty four feet. [pause]
> A divergent series is infinitely big, and being smaller than something infinitely big doesn't narrow anything down at all. Being bigger than something small doesn't either: it could still be small, or it could be huge. [long pause]

## A direct comparison
**On screen:** The direct comparison test stays boxed in the top right corner (here and in Examples 1 and 2; the limit comparison test in Example 3). The problem: "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(n² + 3) converge?" Board: n² + 3 > n², so 1/(n² + 3) < 1/n²; Σ 1/n² converges (p = 2); by direct comparison, Σ 1/(n² + 3) converges.
> Does the series of one over n squared plus three converge? [pause]
> For large n, the plus three barely matters, so compare with one over n squared. Since n squared plus three is bigger than n squared, one over it is smaller. [pause]
> The series of one over n squared converges: it's a p-series with p equals two. [pause]
> Our series is smaller than a convergent series, so it converges by direct comparison. [long pause]

## Limit comparison
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "If aₙ, bₙ > 0 and lim aₙ/bₙ = c with 0 < c < ∞, then Σ aₙ and Σ bₙ both converge or both diverge." Then the problem: "Does Σ (2n + 1)/(n³ − n + 5) converge?" Board: choose bₙ = n/n³ = 1/n² (dominant terms); lim [(2n + 1)/(n³ − n + 5)] / (1/n²) = lim (2n³ + n²)/(n³ − n + 5) = 2; 0 < 2 < ∞ and Σ 1/n² converges, so the series converges.
> Sometimes the inequality goes the wrong way or is messy to prove. Then use limit comparison: if the ratio a sub n over b sub n approaches a positive, finite number, the two series behave the same. [pause]
> Try it on two n plus one over n cubed minus n plus five. Keep only the biggest power on top and on bottom: n over n cubed, which is one over n squared. That's b sub n. [pause]
> The ratio: multiply by n squared, getting two n cubed plus n squared over n cubed minus n plus five. The limit is two. [pause]
> Two is positive and finite, and one over n squared converges, so our series converges too. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "direct: smaller than small converges; bigger than big diverges"; "bigger than small, or smaller than big: settles nothing"; "limit: lim aₙ/bₙ = c, 0 < c < ∞: same behavior"; "compare with p-series or geometric series; pick bₙ from the dominant terms".
> Compare with a series you know. Smaller than something small converges, and bigger than something big diverges; the other two cases settle nothing. Limit comparison needs the ratio to approach a positive, finite number. Build b sub n from the dominant terms. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Bigger than harmonic
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=3}^∞ (ln n)/n converge?" Board: for n ≥ 3, ln n > 1, so (ln n)/n > 1/n; Σ 1/n diverges; by direct comparison, diverges.
> Does the series of ln n over n, starting at three, converge? [pause]
> For n at least three, ln n is bigger than one, so ln n over n is bigger than one over n. [pause]
> The harmonic series diverges, and ours is bigger, so it diverges too. [long pause]

## Example 2: A bounded numerator
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ sin²n/n² converge?" Board: 0 ≤ sin²n ≤ 1, so 0 ≤ sin²n/n² ≤ 1/n²; Σ 1/n² converges; converges by direct comparison.
> Does the series of sine squared n over n squared converge? [pause]
> Sine squared is between zero and one, so each term is between zero and one over n squared. [pause]
> Smaller than a convergent p-series: it converges. [long pause]

## Example 3: Limit comparison with a geometric series
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ 1/(2ⁿ − 1) converge?" Board: bₙ = 1/2ⁿ; lim [1/(2ⁿ − 1)]/(1/2ⁿ) = lim 2ⁿ/(2ⁿ − 1) = 1; Σ 1/2ⁿ converges (geometric); so the series converges. (Direct comparison fails: 1/(2ⁿ − 1) > 1/2ⁿ.)
> Does the series of one over two to the n minus one converge? [pause]
> Compare with one over two to the n. Direct comparison goes the wrong way here: our terms are a bit bigger. [pause]
> So take the limit of the ratio: two to the n over two to the n minus one, which approaches one. [pause]
> One is positive and finite, and the geometric series converges, so ours converges too. [long pause]
