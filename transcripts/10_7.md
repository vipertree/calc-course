# 10.7 Alternating Series Test for Convergence

## Two steps forward, one step back
**On screen:** A frog on a number line jumping right 1, left ½, right ⅓, left ¼, ... Each jump is shorter than the last; its positions zigzag and close in on a point near 0.69, marked with a dashed line. The partial sums S₁ = 1, S₂ = 0.5, S₃ ≈ 0.83, S₄ ≈ 0.58, ... are listed.
> A frog jumps one unit right, then half a unit left, then a third right, a fourth left, and so on. [pause]
> Each jump is shorter than the one before, and it reverses direction every time. So the frog overshoots, comes back, overshoots by less, and closes in on one spot. [pause]
> That's the alternating harmonic series: one minus one half plus one third minus one fourth. Unlike the harmonic series, it converges. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.7 · Alternating series test for convergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The test
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "Σ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹ bₙ with bₙ > 0 converges if (1) bₙ₊₁ ≤ bₙ (decreasing) and (2) lim bₙ = 0." The zigzag of partial sums on a number line, nested intervals shrinking. Plain words: "terms alternate in sign, shrink in size, and go to zero".
> An alternating series switches sign every term. Write it as the sum of negative one to the n plus one, times b sub n, with b sub n positive. [pause]
> The alternating series test says it converges if two things are true: the sizes b sub n are decreasing, and they go to zero. [pause]
> Then the partial sums zigzag back and forth inside shrinking intervals, and they have to settle on a limit. [pause]
> Check both conditions and say them when you use the test. [long pause]

## The alternating harmonic series
**On screen:** The problem: "Show that Σ_{n=1}^∞ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹/n converges." Board, one step per line: bₙ = 1/n > 0; bₙ₊₁ = 1/(n + 1) < 1/n = bₙ: decreasing; lim 1/n = 0; by the alternating series test, the series converges. A note: "(its sum is ln 2 ≈ 0.693)".
> Show that one minus one half plus one third minus one fourth, and so on, converges. [pause]
> The sizes are b sub n equals one over n, which are positive. [pause]
> They're decreasing: one over n plus one is less than one over n. [pause]
> And they go to zero. Both conditions hold, so by the alternating series test, the series converges. Its sum turns out to be ln two. [long pause]

## When the test fails
**On screen:** Σ (−1)ⁿ n/(n + 1): sizes n/(n + 1) → 1, not 0. A red note: "the alternating series test doesn't apply; the nth term test says diverges". A second note: "if bₙ ↛ 0, the series diverges; if bₙ isn't decreasing, the test is inconclusive".
> If the sizes don't go to zero, the alternating series test doesn't apply. For negative one to the n times n over n plus one, the sizes approach one, so the terms don't go to zero and the series diverges by the nth term test. [pause]
> If the sizes go to zero but aren't decreasing, the alternating series test says nothing, and you'd need another method. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Σ (−1)ⁿ bₙ, bₙ > 0: converges if bₙ is decreasing and bₙ → 0"; "state both conditions"; "bₙ ↛ 0: diverges (nth term test)".
> An alternating series converges when its sizes decrease and go to zero. State both conditions. If the sizes don't go to zero, the series diverges by the nth term test. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Root of n
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ (−1)ⁿ/√n converge?" Board: bₙ = 1/√n > 0, decreasing, → 0; converges by the alternating series test.
> Does the series of negative one to the n over root n converge? [pause]
> The sizes, one over root n, are positive, decreasing, and go to zero. By the alternating series test, it converges. [pause]
> Notice that without the alternating signs, one over root n would diverge. The signs make the difference. [long pause]

## Example 2: Sizes that don't shrink to zero
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ (−1)ⁿ (2n)/(n + 3) converge?" Board: bₙ = 2n/(n + 3) → 2 ≠ 0; the terms do not approach 0; diverges by the nth term test.
> Does the series of negative one to the n times two n over n plus three converge? [pause]
> The sizes approach two, not zero. So the terms bounce near plus and minus two forever. The series diverges by the nth term test. [long pause]

## Example 3: A log in the denominator
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=2}^∞ (−1)ⁿ/ln n converge?" Board: bₙ = 1/ln n > 0 for n ≥ 2; ln n increases, so bₙ decreases; lim 1/ln n = 0; converges by the alternating series test.
> Does the series of negative one to the n over ln n, starting at two, converge? [pause]
> The sizes, one over ln n, are positive. Since ln n increases, one over ln n decreases, and it goes to zero. [pause]
> Both conditions hold, so the series converges. [long pause]
