# 10.8 Ratio Test for Convergence

## Behaving like a geometric series
**On screen:** Two rows of terms. Top: a geometric series with ratio ⅓, each term ⅓ of the one before. Bottom: the terms of n/3ⁿ: ⅓, 2/9, 3/27, 4/81, ... Under the bottom row, the ratios of consecutive terms: 0.67, 0.5, 0.44, 0.42, ... creeping toward ⅓.
> A geometric series converges when each term is a fixed fraction of the one before, as long as that fraction is less than one. [pause]
> Many series aren't exactly geometric, but far out they act like one. For n over three to the n, the ratio of each term to the one before creeps toward one third. [pause]
> If the ratio settles below one, the terms shrink like a convergent geometric series. That's the ratio test. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.8 · Ratio test for convergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## The test
**On screen:** Board, boxed: "L = lim_{n→∞} |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|. If L < 1, Σ aₙ converges (absolutely). If L > 1 (or ∞), Σ aₙ diverges. If L = 1, the test is inconclusive." A number line for L: converges left of 1, diverges right of 1, a question mark at 1.
> Compute the limit of the absolute value of a sub n plus one over a sub n. Call it L. [pause]
> If L is less than one, the series converges, and in fact converges absolutely. If L is greater than one, or infinite, the series diverges. [pause]
> If L equals one, the ratio test tells you nothing. Then try another test. [long pause]

## Using the ratio test
**On screen:** The problem: "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ n/3ⁿ converge?" Board, one step per line: aₙ = n/3ⁿ, aₙ₊₁ = (n + 1)/3ⁿ⁺¹; aₙ₊₁/aₙ = [(n + 1)/3ⁿ⁺¹] · [3ⁿ/n]; = (n + 1)/n · 3ⁿ/3ⁿ⁺¹; = (n + 1)/n · ⅓; L = lim (n + 1)/(3n) = ⅓; ⅓ < 1: converges.
> Does the series of n over three to the n converge? [try it]
> Write the next term: replace n with n plus one everywhere. That's n plus one over three to the n plus one. [pause]
> Divide by a sub n, which means multiplying by its reciprocal, three to the n over n. [pause]
> Group the like pieces: n plus one over n, times three to the n over three to the n plus one. The powers of three leave one third. [pause]
> As n goes to infinity, n plus one over n goes to one. So L is one third. [pause]
> One third is less than one, so the series converges. [long pause]

## Factorials
**On screen:** Board: n! = n · (n − 1) · ... · 2 · 1; (n + 1)! = (n + 1) · n!; so (n + 1)!/n! = n + 1 and n!/(n + 1)! = 1/(n + 1). A note: "the ratio test is the tool of choice for factorials and exponentials".
> The ratio test shines with factorials. N factorial is n times n minus one times every whole number down to one. [pause]
> So n plus one factorial is just n plus one, times n factorial. When you divide them, almost everything cancels: n plus one factorial over n factorial is n plus one. [pause]
> Use the ratio test whenever you see factorials or powers like three to the n. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "L = lim |aₙ₊₁/aₙ|"; "L < 1 converges; L > 1 diverges; L = 1 inconclusive"; "(n + 1)! = (n + 1) · n!"; "best for factorials and exponentials".
> The ratio test compares each term to the one before. A limit below one means convergence, above one means divergence, and exactly one means try something else. It's built for factorials and exponentials. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A factorial in the denominator
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=0}^∞ 2ⁿ/n! converge?" Board: aₙ₊₁/aₙ = [2ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1)!] · [n!/2ⁿ] = 2/(n + 1); L = 0 < 1: converges.
> Does the series of two to the n over n factorial converge? [pause]
> The ratio: two to the n plus one over n plus one factorial, times n factorial over two to the n. The powers leave a two, and the factorials leave one over n plus one. [pause]
> So the ratio is two over n plus one, which goes to zero. L is zero, less than one: converges. [long pause]

## Example 2: A factorial on top
**On screen:** "Does Σ_{n=1}^∞ n!/10ⁿ converge?" Board: aₙ₊₁/aₙ = [(n + 1)!/10ⁿ⁺¹] · [10ⁿ/n!] = (n + 1)/10; L = ∞: diverges.
> Does the series of n factorial over ten to the n converge? [pause]
> The ratio simplifies to n plus one over ten, which grows without bound. L is infinite: the series diverges. Factorials eventually beat any exponential. [long pause]

## Example 3: When L = 1
**On screen:** "What does the ratio test say about Σ 1/n²?" Board: aₙ₊₁/aₙ = n²/(n + 1)² → 1; inconclusive; (converges by the p-series test, p = 2).
> What does the ratio test say about one over n squared? [pause]
> The ratio is n squared over n plus one squared, which goes to one. L equals one: inconclusive. [pause]
> We know from the p-series test that this one converges. The ratio test is the wrong tool for p-series; it always gives one. [long pause]
