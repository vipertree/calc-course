# 10.9 Determining Absolute or Conditional Convergence

## Two kinds of convergence
**On screen:** Two alternating series side by side. Left: 1 − ¼ + ⅑ − 1/16 + ... with its absolute values 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ... shown converging too. Right: 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + ... with its absolute values 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ... shown diverging. Labels: "converges even without the signs" and "converges only because of the signs".
> Both of these alternating series converge. But they converge for different reasons. [pause]
> The first, one over n squared with alternating signs, would converge even if you made every term positive. [pause]
> The second, the alternating harmonic series, only converges because the signs cancel; with every term positive, it diverges. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 10.9 · Determining absolute or conditional convergence." Unit 10 · Infinite Sequences and Series. A small "BC" tag.

## Definitions
**On screen:** Board: "absolutely convergent: Σ|aₙ| converges"; "conditionally convergent: Σaₙ converges but Σ|aₙ| diverges"; boxed: "If Σ|aₙ| converges, then Σaₙ converges." A three-box chart: absolutely convergent / conditionally convergent / divergent.
> A series converges absolutely if the series of absolute values converges. [pause]
> It converges conditionally if the series itself converges, but the series of absolute values diverges. [pause]
> Absolute convergence is the stronger kind: if the absolute values converge, the original series does too. So every series lands in exactly one of three boxes: absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent. [long pause]

## A plan for classifying
**On screen:** A short ordered list, built step by step: "1. Test Σ|aₙ| (p-series, comparison, ratio, integral). Converges? absolutely convergent." "2. If Σ|aₙ| diverges, test Σaₙ itself (usually the alternating series test). Converges? conditionally convergent." "3. Otherwise: divergent." Beside it: "first, the nth term test: if aₙ ↛ 0, divergent".
> To classify a series, start with a quick nth term check: if the terms don't go to zero, it diverges. [pause]
> Otherwise, test the absolute values first, using any test for positive series. If they converge, you're done: absolutely convergent. [pause]
> If the absolute values diverge, go back to the original series, usually with the alternating series test. If it converges, it's conditionally convergent. [long pause]

## Three classifications
**On screen:** The problem: "Classify as absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent, or divergent: (a) Σ (−1)ⁿ/n² (b) Σ (−1)ⁿ/n (c) Σ (−1)ⁿ n/(n + 1)." Board: (a) Σ 1/n² converges (p = 2): absolutely convergent; (b) Σ 1/n diverges (harmonic); Σ (−1)ⁿ/n converges (AST: 1/n decreasing to 0): conditionally convergent; (c) n/(n + 1) → 1 ≠ 0: divergent (nth term test).
> Classify each of these three. [try it]
> For the first, take absolute values: one over n squared, a convergent p-series. Absolutely convergent. [pause]
> For the second, the absolute values give the harmonic series, which diverges. But the original series converges by the alternating series test. Conditionally convergent. [pause]
> For the third, the sizes approach one, so the terms don't go to zero. Divergent. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "absolute: Σ|aₙ| converges (and then Σaₙ converges)"; "conditional: Σaₙ converges, Σ|aₙ| diverges"; "check the absolute values first".
> Absolute convergence means the series converges even with all its terms made positive. Conditional convergence means it converges only thanks to its signs. Test the absolute values first, then the series itself. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Root n with alternating signs
**On screen:** "Classify Σ (−1)ⁿ/√n." Board: Σ 1/√n diverges (p = ½); Σ (−1)ⁿ/√n converges (AST); conditionally convergent.
> Classify the series of negative one to the n over root n. [pause]
> The absolute values form a p-series with p equals one half: divergent. [pause]
> The alternating series converges by the alternating series test. So it's conditionally convergent. [long pause]

## Example 2: Signs that don't alternate
**On screen:** "Classify Σ sin n/n²." Board: |sin n/n²| ≤ 1/n²; Σ 1/n² converges; so Σ |sin n/n²| converges by comparison; absolutely convergent (hence convergent).
> Classify the series of sine n over n squared. Its signs don't follow a simple pattern, so the alternating series test doesn't apply. [pause]
> But the absolute values are at most one over n squared, which converges. By comparison, the absolute values converge. [pause]
> So the series is absolutely convergent, and therefore convergent. Absolute convergence handles messy signs. [long pause]

## Example 3: n ln n
**On screen:** "Classify Σ_{n=2}^∞ (−1)ⁿ/(n ln n)." Board: Σ 1/(n ln n) diverges (integral test, 10.4); 1/(n ln n) decreases to 0, so Σ (−1)ⁿ/(n ln n) converges (AST); conditionally convergent.
> Classify the series of negative one to the n over n ln n, starting at two. [pause]
> The absolute values, one over n ln n, diverge: we showed that with the integral test. [pause]
> The sizes decrease to zero, so the alternating series converges. Conditionally convergent. [long pause]
