# 1.1 Introducing Calculus: Can Change Occur at an Instant?

## Zeno's arrow
**On screen:** Black screen. A line of text fades in, attributed beneath: "If everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if that which is in locomotion is always occupying such a space at any moment, the flying arrow is therefore motionless." Aristotle, Physics VI.9, describing Zeno's argument (trans. Hardie and Gaye).
> About two and a half thousand years ago, the philosopher Zeno of Elea made an argument that still bothers people.
> [pause] Here is how Aristotle recorded it.
> If everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if that which is in locomotion is always occupying such a space at any moment, [pause] the flying arrow is therefore motionless.
> [long pause]

## Unpacking the paradox
**On screen:** An arrow flies in and freezes. A clock face beside it shows a single instant. Around the arrow, a dashed outline exactly its own size. Then the arrow blinks from place to place along its flight, frozen in a new spot at each tick of the clock.
> Here's Zeno's idea in plain words. Freeze time at one instant. At that instant, the arrow is sitting in a space exactly its own size.
> It isn't moving through that space. There's no time for it to move. [pause]
> But every moment of the flight is an instant like this one. If the arrow isn't moving at any instant, [pause] when does it ever move?

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.1 · Can change occur at an instant?" Unit 1 · Limits and Continuity.

## What a single frame can't show
**On screen:** One snapshot of the arrow, captioned "t = 1 s." Then the same snapshot at t = 2 s: the arrow farther along, with a faint ghost where it was a second earlier.
> Zeno put his finger on something real. A single photograph of the arrow shows where it is, but not how fast it's going.
> Speed needs two moments. [pause] Where the arrow is now, and where it is a little later.

## Distance and time
**On screen:** A graph of distance against time. The formula appears: s of t equals 60t minus 12 t squared, in meters. A small inset shows the arrow's real flight, an arc through the air, with its horizontal distance from the bow marked along the ground; that distance is what the graph's vertical axis records, and the horizontal axis is labeled "time." The dots sit at (1, 48) and (2, 72). The curve bends over visibly as the arrow slows.
> Suppose the arrow's distance from the bow, in meters, after t seconds, is s of t equals sixty t minus twelve t squared. This arrow slows down a lot as it flies, which is why its graph bends.
> One warning about this graph. [pause] It is not a picture of the arrow's path. The arrow really flies in an arc through the air, but we only track how far it is from the bow, measured along the ground. That distance goes up the vertical axis. The horizontal axis is time. [pause]
> At one second, it's forty-eight meters out. At two seconds, seventy-two meters. [pause]

## An average isn't an instant
**On screen:** A ghost arrow replays the second from t = 1 to t = 2, visibly slowing down. Speed readouts flicker past: about 36 near t = 1, down to about 12 near t = 2.
> But twenty-four is an average over a whole second. The arrow was slowing down that entire time.
> At the start of that second, it was going faster than twenty-four. At the end, slower. [pause]
> So how fast was it going at exactly one second? [long pause]

## Shrinking the interval
**On screen:** The second dot slides toward t = 1 and the secant line pivots with it. A table fills in one row at a time: interval length 1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, with average velocities 24, 30, 34.8, 35.88, 35.988.
> Here's the plan. We'll keep the starting time at one second, and make the interval shorter and shorter.
> From one second to one and a half, the average velocity is thirty. [pause]
> From one second to one point one, thirty-four point eight. [pause]
> To one point zero one, thirty-five point eight eight. To one point zero zero one, thirty-five point nine eight eight. [pause]
> Watch the numbers climb, and slow down as they climb. Each step adds less than the one before. These averages are closing in on thirty-six.

## From the other side
**On screen:** The second dot now sits just before t = 1. New rows: interval length negative 0.1 gives 37.2; negative 0.01 gives 36.12.
> Try the other side, using times just before one second. The averages come down toward thirty-six from above: thirty-seven point two, then thirty-six point one two. [pause]
> From both sides, the same target. Thirty-six.

## Why not zero?
**On screen:** The two dots merge into one. The quotient shows (48 minus 48) over (1 minus 1), becomes 0 over 0, and then fades to a question mark.
> So why not just make the interval zero seconds long? [pause]
> Then both times are the same. The change in distance is zero, the change in time is zero, [pause] and zero divided by zero has no value.
> This is Zeno's point, stated as arithmetic. An instant, by itself, contains no change. [long pause]

## Two average velocities
**On screen:** The problem, then the three positions, then each average velocity as change in position over change in time.
> Find the arrow's average velocity from t equals zero to two, and then from t equals one to two. [try it]
> First the positions. s of zero is zero, s of one is forty-eight, and s of two is seventy-two.
> From zero to two: seventy-two minus zero, over two. Thirty-six meters per second. [pause]
> From one to two: seventy-two minus forty-eight, over one. Twenty-four meters per second.
> The arrow is slowing down, so its average over the later second is smaller. [long pause]

## A sprinter
**On screen:** The sprinter's table: t = 0 to 4, D = 0, 3, 9, 17, 26. The columns t = 1 and t = 3 light up, and (17 − 3)/(3 − 1) = 7 m/s.
> Here's a sprinter's distance at each second. Estimate the speed at two seconds. [try it]
> We can't shrink the interval below the data, so use the shortest interval with two in the middle: one to three seconds. [pause]
> Seventeen minus three, over three minus one: fourteen over two. About seven meters per second. [long pause]

## Closer and closer
**On screen:** The column of averages streams toward a highlighted target, 36. A magnifier zooms in on the last row, 35.988, and the interval [1, 1.001] under it. Inside the magnifier, the arrow still moves a tiny distance.
> So we can get as close as we like. [pause]
> An interval of a thousandth of a second gives thirty-five point nine eight eight. A millionth of a second would get even closer.
> But look at what each of these numbers is. [pause] Every one is an average over some stretch of time, and during that stretch, the arrow moves.
> However short we make the interval, it is still an interval. [pause] It is never the instant itself.

## The question stays open
**On screen:** The frozen arrow returns, with the dashed outline from the opening around it. Next to it, the number 36 with a large question mark.
> So, Zeno. Is the arrow moving at an instant? [pause]
> The averages close in on thirty-six. It's tempting to say the arrow is going thirty-six meters per second at t equals one.
> But nothing we've done yet tells us that number belongs to the instant. [long pause]
> This is the problem calculus was invented to solve. The tool it needs is called a limit, and building it is what this unit is about. [pause]
> We'll come back to the arrow. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An average rate from a formula
**On screen:** "Find the average rate of change of f(x) = x² − 3x on [1, 4]." The work appears one line at a time: f(4) = 16 − 12 = 4; f(1) = 1 − 3 = −2; (4 − (−2)) / (4 − 1) = 6/3 = 2. The graph of f with the secant line through (1, −2) and (4, 4).
> Find the average rate of change of f of x equals x squared minus three x, on the interval from one to four. [pause]
> Start with the outputs at the ends. f of four is sixteen minus twelve, which is four. f of one is one minus three, negative two.
> Change in output over change in input: four minus negative two, over four minus one. [pause]
> That's six over three, which is two.
> On the graph, two is the slope of the secant line through those two points. [pause]

## Example 2: A rate from a table
**On screen:** A table for a kettle, temperature H(t) in degrees Celsius after t minutes: t = 0, 2, 5, 6, 9; H = 20, 34, 52, 57, 70. The question: "Estimate how fast the temperature is rising at t = 4." The columns t = 2 and t = 5 highlight.
> A kettle heats up. The table gives its temperature, in degrees Celsius, every so often. [pause]
> Estimate how fast the temperature is rising at four minutes.
> Four isn't in the table, so we can't compute anything at four itself. The best we can do is an average over a short interval around it. [pause]
> The closest times on either side are two and five. Fifty-two minus thirty-four, over five minus two. [pause]
> Eighteen over three is six. About six degrees per minute.
> Notice the units: degrees, divided by minutes. [pause]

## Example 3: Shrinking an interval
**On screen:** "Estimate the rate of change of g(x) = x³ at x = 2." A table fills in row by row: [2, 2.1] gives 12.61; [2, 2.01] gives 12.0601; [1.99, 2] gives 11.9401. The numbers settle toward 12 from both sides.
> Estimate the rate of change of g of x equals x cubed at x equals two. [pause]
> Use the interval from two to two point one. g of two point one is nine point two six one, and g of two is eight. The change is one point two six one, and dividing by a tenth gives twelve point six one. [pause]
> Shrink it: from two to two point zero one, we get twelve point zero six zero one.
> From the left, one point nine nine to two, eleven point nine four zero one. [pause]
> From both sides, the averages are closing in on twelve.
> And just like the arrow, every one of them is still an average over an interval. [long pause]
