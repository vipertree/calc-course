# 1.10 Exploring Types of Discontinuities

## The pencil test
**On screen:** A pencil traces a smooth curve from left to right without lifting. Then it traces a second graph and has to lift at three places: a hole, a jump, and a vertical asymptote. Each lift point glows.
> Informally, a function is continuous if you can draw its graph without lifting your pencil. [pause]
> This graph makes you lift the pencil three times. Each spot is a discontinuity, and each one breaks in a different way. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.10 · Exploring types of discontinuities."

## Removable: a hole
**On screen:** Zoom in on the first break: the curve arrives at an open circle from both sides. A stray filled dot sits elsewhere at the same x. Label: "removable".
> The first break is a hole. The graph heads to the same height from both sides, so the limit exists. [pause]
> The problem is only at the point itself: the value is missing, or it sits somewhere else.
> We call this a removable discontinuity, because moving a single point would repair it. [long pause]

## Jump
**On screen:** Zoom in on the second break: the left piece ends at one height, the right piece starts at another. A vertical gap is highlighted and labeled "jump".
> The second break is a jump. The left and right sides head to different heights. [pause]
> The one-sided limits both exist, but they disagree, so the two-sided limit doesn't exist. No single point can fix this. [long pause]

## Infinite
**On screen:** Zoom in on the third break: the graph shoots up along a dashed vertical line on one side and down on the other. Label: "infinite".
> The third break is infinite. Near the dashed line, the graph grows without bound. This is a vertical asymptote, and it's called an infinite discontinuity. [long pause]

## Reading the formula
**On screen:** The rational function x squared minus x minus 2, over x squared minus 4. The numerator and denominator factor: (x minus 2)(x plus 1) over (x minus 2)(x plus 2). The canceling factor x minus 2 turns into a hole at x equals 2; the remaining factor x plus 2 in the denominator turns into a dashed asymptote at x equals negative 2. The graph draws itself with both features.
> You can predict the breaks from a formula. Factor the top and bottom. [pause]
> A factor that cancels, like x minus two here, leaves a hole. Its height is what the simplified function gives: 3 over 4. [pause]
> A factor that stays in the denominator, like x plus two, makes the function blow up. That's a vertical asymptote. [long pause]

## Piecewise functions
**On screen:** A piecewise function with pieces x plus 1 and x squared meeting at x equals 1. The first version leaves a gap: left heads to 2, right to 1. The graph shows a jump.
> Piecewise functions can jump where the pieces meet. Compare the two pieces at the boundary.
> Here the left piece heads to two and the right piece heads to one, so there's a jump at x equals one. [long pause]

## Where the pieces meet
**On screen:** The two pieces, the left-hand limit 2 and the right-hand limit 1, and the verdict 'jump.'
> Is this piecewise function continuous at one? If not, what kind of discontinuity is it? [try it]
> From the left, the piece x plus one heads to two.
> From the right, the piece x squared heads to one. [pause]
> The one-sided limits disagree, so the graph jumps: a jump discontinuity. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The three zoomed breaks sit side by side with their names: removable, jump, infinite. Under each: "limit exists", "one-sided limits disagree", "unbounded".
> Three kinds of breaks. A removable discontinuity has a limit. A jump has two different one-sided limits. An infinite discontinuity has no finite limit at all. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: Classify from a formula
**On screen:** "Find and classify the discontinuities of f(x) = (x² + 2x − 15)/(x² − 9)." The factoring appears: (x + 5)(x − 3) over (x − 3)(x + 3). The (x − 3) factors cancel with a flash; a hole is drawn at (3, 4/3). The factor (x + 3) stays; a dashed asymptote appears at x = −3.
> Find and classify the discontinuities of x squared plus two x minus fifteen, over x squared minus nine. [pause]
> The bottom is zero at three and at negative three, so those are the only candidates.
> Factor. The top is x plus five times x minus three. The bottom is x minus three times x plus three. [pause]
> The x minus three cancels, so x equals three is removable. The hole's height comes from what's left: eight over six, or four thirds.
> The x plus three stays in the denominator, so x equals negative three is an infinite discontinuity. [pause]

## Example 2: A piecewise function with a misplaced point
**On screen:** "f(x) = 2x − 1 for x < 2; f(2) = 5; f(x) = x² − 1 for x > 2." The left piece heads to 3, the right piece heads to 3, and the lone dot sits at (2, 5).
> Here's a piecewise function: two x minus one on the left of two, x squared minus one on the right, and f of two is five. Find and classify the discontinuity at two. [pause]
> From the left, four minus one is three. From the right, four minus one is also three. The limit is three.
> But f of two is five. [pause] The limit exists and the value is in the wrong place. That's a removable discontinuity.

## Example 3: A jump from an absolute value
**On screen:** "Classify the discontinuity of f(x) = |x + 1|/(x + 1) at x = −1." A number line splits at −1: on the left, |x + 1| = −(x + 1) and f = −1; on the right, f = 1. The graph shows two horizontal rays with open circles.
> Classify the discontinuity of the absolute value of x plus one, over x plus one, at negative one. [pause]
> Split at negative one. To the left, x plus one is negative, so the absolute value flips its sign, and the quotient is negative one.
> To the right, x plus one is positive, and the quotient is one. [pause]
> The one-sided limits are negative one and one. They disagree, so it's a jump discontinuity. [long pause]
