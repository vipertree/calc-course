# 1.11 Defining Continuity at a Point

## From pencils to precision
**On screen:** The pencil from the last lesson hovers over a graph, then fades. In its place, three empty checkboxes appear.
> The pencil test is a good picture, but it isn't a definition. On an exam you'll need to justify continuity, and "I didn't lift my pencil" won't earn the point. [pause]
> Continuity at a point comes down to three checks. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.11 · Defining continuity at a point."

## The three conditions
**On screen:** The three checkboxes fill with text, one at a time: "f of c is defined"; "the limit as x approaches c of f of x exists"; "the limit equals f of c". Each line is paired with a tiny graph showing what failure looks like: a missing point, a jump, a hole with a stray dot.
> A function f is continuous at x equals c when three things are true. [pause]
> First, f of c exists. There's a point there. [pause]
> Second, the limit as x approaches c of f of x exists. Both sides head to the same height. [pause]
> Third, the limit equals f of c. The point sits exactly where the graph is heading. [long pause]

## Each discontinuity fails a condition
**On screen:** The three discontinuities from Topic 1.10 line up under the checklist. For each, the condition that fails turns red: the removable one with a missing point fails the first check (or the third, if a stray dot is present); the jump fails the second; the asymptote fails the second as well.
> Every discontinuity breaks at least one of these conditions.
> A hole with no point fails the first check. A hole with a misplaced point passes the first two but fails the third. [pause]
> A jump fails the second, because the one-sided limits disagree. An asymptote fails the second too. [long pause]

## A justification, written out
**On screen:** A piecewise function: x squared plus 1 for x less than 2, and 4x minus 3 for x at least 2. The work appears in three lines: f of 2 equals 5; the left and right limits are both 5; so the limit equals f of 2, and f is continuous at 2.
> A full justification checks all three. f of two is four times two minus three, which is five. [pause]
> From the left, x squared plus one heads to five. From the right, four x minus three heads to five. So the limit is five. [pause]
> The limit equals f of two, so f is continuous at x equals two. [long pause]

## Justify continuity
**On screen:** The three conditions checked one at a time, each with its value, ending with lim f(x) = f(2).
> Is this function continuous at two? Justify your answer using the definition. [try it]
> Condition one: f of two exists. It's four times two minus three, which is five.
> Condition two: the limit exists. From the left it's two squared plus one, five, and from the right it's five too. [pause]
> Condition three: the limit equals the value. Five equals five, so f is continuous at two. [long pause]

## Justify discontinuity
**On screen:** g(3) = 5, the fraction factored with x − 3 struck out, the limit 6, and 6 ≠ 5.
> Now g: x squared minus nine over x minus three, except that g of three is five. Is g continuous at three? [try it]
> g of three is five.
> Away from three, the fraction simplifies: x minus three cancels, leaving x plus three. [pause]
> So the limit as x approaches three is six. Six isn't five, so g is not continuous at three. [long pause]

## One-sided continuity
**On screen:** The graph of the square root of x, starting at the origin. A small arrow approaches 0 from the right only.
> At the edge of a domain, only one side exists. The square root function starts at zero.
> We say it's continuous from the right at zero, because the right-hand limit equals the function value there. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The completed checklist, with the phrase "the limit equals the function value" highlighted.
> So continuity at a point means one thing, packaged in three checks: the limit equals the function value. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: Is it continuous?
**On screen:** "Is f continuous at x = 3? f(x) = x² − 2 for x < 3, and 2x + 1 for x ≥ 3." Three checkboxes fill in: f(3) = 7; left limit 7 and right limit 7, so the limit is 7; the limit equals f(3). All three turn green.
> Is this function continuous at three? It's x squared minus two on the left, and two x plus one from three on. [pause]
> Check one: f of three uses the second piece, two times three plus one, which is seven.
> Check two: from the left, nine minus two is seven. From the right, six plus one is seven. The limit is seven. [pause]
> Check three: the limit, seven, equals f of three, seven. So f is continuous at three.

## Example 2: Which condition fails?
**On screen:** "g(x) = (x² − 16)/(x − 4) for x ≠ 4, and g(4) = 5." The checklist: g(4) = 5 passes; the limit as x → 4 is 8, passes; 8 ≠ 5, fails in red.
> Here g is x squared minus sixteen over x minus four, except at four, where g is five. Is it continuous at four? [pause]
> Check one: g of four is five. It exists.
> Check two: factor, cancel, and the limit as x approaches four of x plus four is eight. It exists. [pause]
> Check three: eight is not five. The third condition fails, so g is not continuous at four. It's removable: redefining g of four as eight would fix it.

## Example 3: Finding the constant
**On screen:** "Find k so that h(x) = kx − 1 for x < 2, and x² + k for x ≥ 2, is continuous at x = 2." The equation 2k − 1 = 4 + k appears and solves to k = 5.
> Find k so that h is continuous at two: k x minus one on the left, x squared plus k from two on. [pause]
> For continuity, the two pieces have to meet. The left piece heads to two k minus one. The right piece gives four plus k, and that's also h of two.
> Set them equal: two k minus one equals four plus k. [pause]
> So k is five. [long pause]
