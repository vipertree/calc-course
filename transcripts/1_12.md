# 1.12 Confirming Continuity over an Interval

## From one point to many
**On screen:** A graph with a single point under a magnifying glass. The glass slides along the curve, checking point after point, leaving a trail of small check marks.
> Last lesson we checked continuity at a single point. Checking an interval one point at a time would take forever. [pause]
> Fortunately, most functions come with continuity already built in. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.12 · Confirming continuity over an interval."

## The definition
**On screen:** An open interval (a, b) on the x-axis, shaded. The graph above it is unbroken. Text: "continuous at every point of the interval." Then the interval closes to [a, b] and arrows at each end point inward: "one-sided at the endpoints."
> A function is continuous on an interval if it's continuous at every point in that interval. [pause]
> At an endpoint of a closed interval, the function only has to behave from the inside: from the right at the left end, and from the left at the right end. [long pause]

## The library of continuous functions
**On screen:** A shelf of function families, each with a small icon graph: polynomials, rational functions, roots and powers, exponentials, logarithms, sine, cosine and tangent. A banner over the shelf: "continuous on their domains."
> Here's the shortcut. Polynomials, rational functions, powers and roots, exponentials, logarithms, and the trig functions are all continuous at every point in their domains. [pause]
> So the question "where is this function continuous?" usually becomes "where is it defined?" [long pause]

## Reading a domain
**On screen:** The function square root of (x minus 1), over x minus 4. The square root is highlighted with the condition x at least 1; the denominator with x not equal to 4. A number line shades from 1 on, with an open circle at 4. The answer: continuous on [1, 4) and (4, infinity).
> For the square root of x minus one, over x minus four, the root needs x at least one, and the denominator can't be zero, so x can't be four. [pause]
> That gives the answer: continuous from one up to four, and from four on, with four itself left out. [long pause]

## Read the domain
**On screen:** The root's condition, the denominator's condition, and the domain written as intervals.
> Where is the square root of x minus one, over x minus four, continuous? [try it]
> The square root needs x minus one to be at least zero, so x is at least one.
> The denominator can't be zero, so x isn't four. [pause]
> So f is continuous on one to four, including one, and on four to infinity. [long pause]

## Logs and trig
**On screen:** The log's condition giving (−3, 3); tan x as sin x / cos x, and the excluded points π/2 + kπ.
> Where are the natural log of nine minus x squared, and tangent x, continuous? [try it]
> A log needs a positive input: nine minus x squared greater than zero, so x is between negative three and three.
> Tangent is sine over cosine, so it's continuous wherever cosine isn't zero. [pause]
> Cosine is zero at pi over two plus any multiple of pi. Everywhere else, tangent is continuous. [long pause]

## Building bigger functions
**On screen:** Two continuous functions combine with plus, minus, times, a division (with a warning where the denominator is zero), and a composition. Each result keeps a solid graph.
> Sums, differences, products and compositions of continuous functions are continuous too. So are quotients, except where the denominator is zero. [long pause]

## Piecewise functions need one extra check
**On screen:** A piecewise function: x squared for x less than 1, and 2 minus x for x at least 1. Each piece is marked continuous on its own part. Only the boundary x equals 1 gets a magnifying glass: left heads to 1, right heads to 1, value is 1.
> Piecewise functions are the exception. Each piece is continuous on its own part, so the only places to check are the boundaries. [pause]
> At x equals one, both sides head to one, and the value is one. So this function is continuous everywhere. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The shelf of function families, with the note "check the domain; check the seams."
> To confirm continuity on an interval: check the domain, and check the seams where the formula changes. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A log over a line
**On screen:** "Where is f(x) = ln(x + 2)/(x − 1) continuous?" Two conditions appear: x + 2 > 0 and x − 1 ≠ 0. A number line shades from −2 (open) onward, with a hole at 1. Answer: (−2, 1) ∪ (1, ∞).
> Where is the natural log of x plus two, over x minus one, continuous? [pause]
> It's built from continuous pieces, so we just need its domain.
> The log needs a positive input: x plus two greater than zero, so x greater than negative two. The denominator can't be zero, so x isn't one. [pause]
> Continuous from negative two to one, and from one on, with both negative two and one left out.

## Example 2: Factor the denominator
**On screen:** "Where is g(x) = (x + 3)/(x² − x − 6) discontinuous?" The denominator factors as (x − 3)(x + 2). Two red marks on the number line at −2 and 3.
> Where is x plus three, over x squared minus x minus six, not continuous? [pause]
> A rational function is continuous everywhere except where its denominator is zero.
> Factor: x minus three, times x plus two. That's zero at three and at negative two. [pause]
> So g is discontinuous at exactly those two points, and continuous everywhere else.

## Example 3: Two seams
**On screen:** "h(x) = x + 2 for x < 0; 2cos x for 0 ≤ x ≤ π; x − π − 2 for x > π. Where is h continuous?" Magnifying glasses visit x = 0 (values 2, 2, 2) and x = π (values −2, −2, −2).
> Is this three-piece function continuous everywhere? It has two seams to check: at zero and at pi. [pause]
> Each piece is continuous on its own. At zero, the left piece heads to two, and two cosine zero is two. They match.
> At pi, two cosine pi is negative two, and pi minus pi minus two is negative two. They match too. [pause]
> Both seams check out, so h is continuous everywhere. [long pause]
