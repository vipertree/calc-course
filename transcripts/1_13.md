# 1.13 Removing Discontinuities

## Patching a hole
**On screen:** The graph of x squared minus 16, over x minus 4: a line with a hole at (4, 8). A small filled dot drops from above and snaps into the hole. The graph becomes solid.
> This graph has a hole at x equals four. The limit there is eight: for every x except four, the function matches x plus four, and x plus four heads to eight. [pause]
> If we define the function to equal eight at x equals four, the hole is filled, and the function becomes continuous. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.13 · Removing discontinuities."

## Which breaks can be removed
**On screen:** The three kinds of discontinuity from Topic 1.10 appear. A dot tries to patch each one. It fills the hole; it can't bridge the jump; it can't reach the top of the asymptote.
> Only one kind of break can be patched this way. A removable discontinuity has a limit, so you fill the hole with the limit's value. [pause]
> A jump has no single limit to use, and an infinite discontinuity has no finite limit at all. Those can't be fixed by changing one point. [long pause]

## Writing the repaired function
**On screen:** The repaired function written as a piecewise definition: (x squared minus 16) over (x minus 4) when x is not 4, and 8 when x equals 4.
> To write the repaired function, keep the formula everywhere else, and set the value at the hole equal to the limit. [long pause]

## Choosing a parameter
**On screen:** A piecewise function with an unknown: k x plus 1 for x less than 2, and x squared minus 1 for x at least 2. The left piece swings as k changes, like a hinge at the y-intercept. When k equals 1, the two pieces meet at (2, 3).
> The same idea can design a function. Here, the left piece has an unknown slope k.
> As k changes, the left piece swings. We want it to land exactly where the right piece starts. [pause]
> The right piece gives three at x equals two. The left piece gives two k plus one. Set them equal: k equals one. [long pause]

## Two unknowns, two seams
**On screen:** A three-piece function with unknowns a and b and seams at x equals negative 1 and x equals 2. Each seam produces an equation. The two equations are solved together: a equals 3 and b equals 1.
> With two seams, you get two equations. At each seam, set the left piece equal to the right piece. [pause]
> Then solve the system. Here, a is three and b is one. [long pause]

## Two seams
**On screen:** The three-piece function, the seam at x = 2 solved first for b, then the seam at x = −1 for a.
> Find a and b so that this three-piece function is continuous everywhere. [try it]
> Start at the seam with only one unknown, x equals two. The middle piece gives four b plus one, and the right piece gives five. So b is one.
> Now the seam at negative one. The left piece gives negative one plus a, and the middle piece gives b plus one, which is two. [pause]
> So a is three. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The patched line from the opening and the hinged piecewise function, both solid.
> Fill a hole with the limit. Match the pieces at every seam. That's how to remove a discontinuity. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: Fill the hole
**On screen:** "f(x) = (x² − 5x + 6)/(x − 3) has a removable discontinuity at x = 3. What value of f(3) makes f continuous?" Each line keeps the limit: the limit of (x − 2)(x − 3)/(x − 3); = the limit of (x − 2); = 1. Then the repaired piecewise definition with f(3) = 1.
> This function has a removable discontinuity at three. What should f of three be? [pause]
> Whatever makes the value match the limit. So find the limit.
> Factor the top: x minus two, times x minus three. Cancel, and the limit as x approaches three of x minus two is one. [pause]
> Define f of three to be one, and the hole is filled.

## Example 2: A hole hiding behind a root
**On screen:** "g(x) = (√(x + 1) − 2)/(x − 3). What value of g(3) removes the discontinuity?" Multiply by the conjugate: the limit of 1/(√(x + 1) + 2) = 1/4.
> Same question, harder algebra. The square root of x plus one, minus two, over x minus three. [pause]
> Substitution gives zero over zero. With a root on top, use the conjugate.
> The top becomes x plus one minus four, which is x minus three, and that cancels with the bottom. [pause]
> What's left is one over the square root of x plus one, plus two. At three, that's one over four. So define g of three as one fourth.

## Example 3: Two seams, two unknowns
**On screen:** "f(x) = ax + 2 for x < 1; x² + b for 1 ≤ x ≤ 3; 4x − 1 for x > 3. Find a and b so f is continuous." Seam x = 3: 9 + b = 11, so b = 2. Seam x = 1: a + 2 = 1 + b = 3, so a = 1.
> Find a and b so this three-piece function is continuous everywhere. [pause]
> Start with the seam that has only one unknown. At three, the middle piece gives nine plus b, and the right piece gives twelve minus one, eleven. So b is two.
> Now the seam at one. The left piece gives a plus two. The middle gives one plus b, which is three. [pause]
> So a plus two is three, and a is one. [long pause]
