# 1.14 Connecting Infinite Limits and Vertical Asymptotes

## Dividing by almost nothing
**On screen:** A column of divisions: 1 divided by 0.1 is 10, by 0.01 is 100, by 0.001 is 1000. The results grow taller as bars.
> One divided by a tenth is ten. One divided by a hundredth is a hundred. Divide by smaller and smaller numbers, and the results grow without bound. [pause]
> That's the engine behind every vertical asymptote. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.14 · Connecting infinite limits and vertical asymptotes."

## Infinite limits
**On screen:** The graph of 1 over (x minus 2) squared. Both sides rise without bound along a dashed line at x equals 2. The notation "limit as x approaches 2 equals infinity" appears.
> When a function's outputs grow without bound as x approaches c, we write that the limit equals infinity. [pause]
> Remember what that means. The limit still doesn't exist as a number. [pause] The infinity symbol tells you how it fails: the outputs are getting larger than any number you name. [long pause]

## Vertical asymptotes
**On screen:** The dashed line x equals 2 is labeled "vertical asymptote." Then a second graph, 1 over (x minus 2), with one side going up and the other going down, also gets the label.
> If either one-sided limit at c is infinite, positive or negative, the line x equals c is a vertical asymptote. [pause]
> One side is enough. For one over x minus two, the left side falls to negative infinity and the right side rises to infinity. It's still an asymptote. [long pause]

## Finding the sign
**On screen:** The function (x plus 3) over (x minus 1)(x plus 2). A sign chart on a number line near x equals 1: numerator positive, factor (x minus 1) negative on the left and positive on the right, factor (x plus 2) positive. The signs are written as fractions, plus over (minus)(plus) on the left and plus over (plus)(plus) on the right, which come out minus and plus.
> To find which way each side goes, check signs. Near x equals one, the numerator is about four, which is positive, and x plus two is about three, also positive. [pause]
> The factor x minus one is a small negative number on the left and a small positive number on the right. [pause]
> Write the signs as a fraction. On the left: plus, over minus times plus. That's negative. On the right: plus, over plus times plus. That's positive. [pause]
> So the left side heads to negative infinity and the right side to positive infinity. [long pause]

## A sign chart
**On screen:** The sign of each factor near x = 1, written as a fraction of signs for each side, and the two infinite limits.
> Find the one-sided limits of x plus three, over x minus one times x plus two, as x approaches one. [pause]
> Near one, x plus three is about four, and x plus two is about three. Both positive.
> On the left, x minus one is a small negative number. Plus over negative times plus is negative, so the limit is negative infinity. [pause]
> On the right, x minus one is a small positive number, everything is positive, and the limit is infinity. [long pause]

## Not every zero of the denominator
**On screen:** The function (x squared minus 1) over (x minus 1). The denominator is zero at x equals 1, but the factor cancels, leaving a hole instead of an asymptote.
> Be careful. A zero in the denominator only makes an asymptote if it survives after canceling. [pause] Here, x minus one cancels, and x equals one is a hole, not an asymptote. [long pause]

## Beyond rational functions
**On screen:** Three graphs: natural log of x with its asymptote at x equals 0; tangent of x with asymptotes at plus and minus pi over 2; and e to the 1 over x, which shoots up only from the right of zero.
> Other functions have vertical asymptotes too. The natural log dives to negative infinity as x approaches zero from the right.
> Tangent has asymptotes wherever cosine is zero. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The division bars from the opening return, now beside a graph approaching a dashed vertical line.
> Nonzero divided by almost zero means unbounded. Check the sign on each side, and you know exactly how the graph behaves near the asymptote. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: One side at a time
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 4 from the left, and from the right, of (2x + 1)/(x − 4)." Substitution gives 9/0. A sign line: numerator +, denominator − on the left and + on the right. Results: −∞ and ∞.
> Find both one-sided limits at four of two x plus one, over x minus four. [pause]
> Substitution yields nine over zero. Nonzero over zero, so the function is unbounded. Now the signs.
> The top is about nine, positive on both sides. [pause]
> Just left of four, x minus four is a tiny negative number. Positive over tiny negative: negative infinity.
> Just right of four, it's tiny and positive. So the right-hand limit is infinity. [pause]

## Example 2: Which zeros make asymptotes?
**On screen:** "Find the vertical asymptotes of f(x) = (x² − 9)/(x² − 2x − 3)." Factor: (x − 3)(x + 3) over (x − 3)(x + 1). The (x − 3) factors cancel and a hole appears at x = 3; a dashed line appears at x = −1.
> Find the vertical asymptotes of x squared minus nine, over x squared minus two x minus three. [pause]
> The denominator is zero at three and at negative one. But don't stop there. Factor.
> The top is x minus three times x plus three. The bottom is x minus three times x plus one. [pause]
> The x minus three cancels, so x equals three is a hole. The x plus one survives, so the only vertical asymptote is x equals negative one.

## Example 3: Same sign on both sides
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 0 of (x − 2)/x²." Substitution gives −2/0. The sign line: numerator − on both sides; x² + on both sides. Both sides dive to −∞; the two-sided limit is written as −∞.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of x minus two, over x squared. [pause]
> Substitution yields negative two over zero.
> The top is about negative two on both sides. The bottom, x squared, is positive on both sides. [pause]
> As a sign fraction, that's minus over plus, on both sides: negative. Both sides head to negative infinity. Since they agree, we write the limit as negative infinity. [long pause]
