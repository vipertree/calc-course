# 1.15 Connecting Limits at Infinity and Horizontal Asymptotes

## Zooming out
**On screen:** The graph of (3x squared plus x) over (x squared plus 1) near the origin, with some wiggle. The camera zooms out, and out again. The curve flattens toward a dashed horizontal line at height 3.
> So far, x has been approaching a number. Now let x grow without bound, and ask what the function does in the long run. [pause]
> Zoom out on this graph, and the curve settles down toward the height 3. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.15 · Connecting limits at infinity and horizontal asymptotes."

## Limits at infinity
**On screen:** The notation "limit as x approaches infinity of f of x equals 3" appears beneath the graph. A quick reminder card: a horizontal line is y = a constant; the line y = 3 is every point at height 3. Then the dashed line is labeled "horizontal asymptote y = 3."
> We write that the limit as x approaches infinity of f of x is three. [pause]
> A quick reminder from algebra: a horizontal line has an equation like y equals three. Every point on it has height three, no matter what x is. [pause]
> When a limit at infinity, or at negative infinity, is a number L, the line y equals L is a horizontal asymptote. [pause]

## The biggest term wins
**On screen:** The numerator 3x squared plus x and denominator x squared plus 1, evaluated at x equals 1000. The terms appear as bars: 3,000,000 dwarfs 1000; 1,000,000 dwarfs 1. The small bars fade away. A boxed big idea: "For large x, only the fastest-growing terms matter." Then the chain: the limit of the original equals the limit of 3x² over x², equals the limit of 3, equals 3.
> Why three? For large x, the highest power in each part dominates. [pause]
> At x equals a thousand, three x squared is three million, and x is only a thousand. The small terms barely matter. [pause]
> That's the big idea of limits at infinity. For large enough x, only the fastest-growing terms matter. They dominate everything else. [long pause]
> So write a chain of limits. The limit of the original function equals the limit of just the leading terms, three x squared over x squared. [pause]
> That simplifies to the limit of three, which is three. [long pause]

## Dividing by the highest power
**On screen:** The algebra: divide the numerator and denominator by x squared. The expression becomes (3 plus 1 over x) over (1 plus 1 over x squared). The terms 1 over x and 1 over x squared shrink to 0, leaving 3 over 1.
> To make that exact, divide the top and bottom by the highest power of x in the denominator.
> Every term like one over x heads to zero, and what's left is three over one. [long pause]

## Three cases for rational functions
**On screen:** Three examples side by side, comparing degrees: top degree less than bottom (limit 0); equal degrees (ratio of leading coefficients); top degree greater (unbounded, no horizontal asymptote).
> For rational functions, compare degrees. [pause]
> If the bottom's degree is bigger, the limit is zero. If the degrees match, the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients. [pause]
> If the top's degree is bigger, the function grows without bound, and there's no horizontal asymptote. [long pause]

## Other functions
**On screen:** Three graphs: e to the negative x settling to 0 on the right; a logistic-style curve, 5 over (1 plus e to the negative x), with asymptotes at 0 and 5; and sine x over x oscillating inside a shrinking envelope toward 0.
> Other functions have long-run behavior too. e to the negative x shrinks to zero.
> A function can have two different horizontal asymptotes, one on each end, like this S-shaped curve. [pause]
> And sine x over x keeps wiggling, but the squeeze theorem pins it down to zero. [long pause]

## Roots at negative infinity
**On screen:** The leading terms, the absolute value rewritten as −x for negative x, and the limit −2.
> Find the limit of the square root of four x squared plus one, over x, as x goes to negative infinity. [pause]
> The fastest-growing terms dominate: the root of four x squared on top, which is two times the absolute value of x, and x on the bottom.
> Watch the sign. For negative x, the absolute value of x is negative x. [pause]
> So the ratio is two times negative x, over x: negative two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** Back to the zoomed-out graph from the opening, settling onto y equals 3.
> Limits at infinity describe the long run. When they're finite, they give you the horizontal asymptotes. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: Equal degrees
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → ∞ of (4x² − 3x)/(2x² + 5)." The chain, each step with its limit: the limit of (4x² − 3x)/(2x² + 5) = the limit of 4x²/(2x²) = the limit of 2 = 2. A dashed line y = 2 appears on a sketch.
> Find the limit as x approaches infinity of four x squared minus three x, over two x squared plus five. [pause]
> For large x, only the leading terms matter: four x squared on top, two x squared on the bottom.
> So the limit equals the limit of four x squared over two x squared. [pause]
> That simplifies to the limit of two, which is two.
> So y equals two is a horizontal asymptote.

## Example 2: A square root at negative infinity
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → −∞ of (5x + 1)/√(x² + 4)." A warning flag on √(x²) = |x| = −x for x < 0. The chain: the limit of (5x + 1)/√(x² + 4) = the limit of 5x/√(x²) = the limit of 5x/(−x) = the limit of −5 = −5.
> Find the limit as x approaches negative infinity of five x plus one, over the square root of x squared plus four. [pause]
> The leading terms are five x on top and the square root of x squared on the bottom. So the limit equals the limit of five x over the square root of x squared.
> Careful here. The square root of x squared is the absolute value of x, and when x is negative, that's negative x. [pause]
> So we have the limit of five x over negative x, which is the limit of negative five: negative five.
> At positive infinity, the same function heads to positive five. Two different horizontal asymptotes, one on each end. [long pause]

## Example 3: Compare the degrees
**On screen:** Part (a) appears alone: "the limit as x → ∞ of (x² + 1)/(x − 3)." Its chain: = the limit of x²/x = the limit of x = ∞, no horizontal asymptote. Then part (b): "the limit as x → ∞ of 7x/(x³ − 1)." Its chain: = the limit of 7x/x³ = the limit of 7/x² = 0.
> Two quick ones. [pause]
> Part a: x squared plus one, over x minus three, as x goes to infinity. [try it]
> Leading terms: x squared over x. That's the limit of x, which grows without bound. The limit is infinity, and there's no horizontal asymptote on that end. [pause]
> Part b: seven x over x cubed minus one, as x goes to infinity. [try it]
> Leading terms: seven x over x cubed, which is seven over x squared. That shrinks to zero. [long pause]
