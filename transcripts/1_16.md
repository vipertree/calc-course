# 1.16 Working with the Intermediate Value Theorem

## A hike up a mountain
**On screen:** A hiker climbs a trail from a trailhead at 2000 feet to a summit at 5000 feet. An altimeter reads the elevation. A dashed line at 3500 feet crosses the trail.
> A hiker starts at 2000 feet and reaches the summit at 5000 feet. Was there a moment when the hiker was at exactly 3500 feet? [pause]
> Of course. You can't get from 2000 to 5000 without passing through every elevation in between. Elevation changes continuously. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.16 · Working with the Intermediate Value Theorem."

## The theorem
**On screen:** A continuous graph on a closed interval from a to b, with f of a below and f of b above. A horizontal line at height k, between them, crosses the graph at a point c. The statement builds: "If f is continuous on [a, b], and k is between f of a and f of b, then there is a c in (a, b) with f of c equals k."
> Here's the theorem behind that common sense. If f is continuous on a closed interval from a to b, and k is any value between f of a and f of b, then somewhere between a and b, f takes the value k. [pause]
> The theorem promises the point exists. It doesn't tell you where it is. [pause]
> It may sound too obvious to need a theorem. But every condition matters. Drop continuity, or use an open interval, and the promise can fail. [long pause]

## Why continuity matters
**On screen:** A graph with a jump from below k to above k. The horizontal line at height k passes through the gap without touching the graph.
> Take away continuity and the promise breaks. [pause] This function jumps right over the height k. It starts below k and ends above k, but it never equals k. [long pause]

## Finding a root
**On screen:** The function x cubed plus x minus 1. At x equals 0 it's negative 1; at x equals 1 it's 1. The graph crosses the x-axis between them.
> The theorem's most common job is proving that an equation has a solution. [pause]
> Take x cubed plus x minus one. It's a polynomial, so it's continuous. At zero it's negative one, and at one it's one. [pause]
> Zero is between negative one and one, so there's a c between zero and one where the function equals zero. The equation has a root there. [long pause]

## A root exists
**On screen:** The continuity statement, the two endpoint values with 0 between them, and the IVT conclusion.
> Show that x cubed plus x minus one equals zero has a solution between zero and one. [try it]
> Call the left side f. It's a polynomial, so it's continuous on zero to one. That's the condition the theorem needs.
> f of zero is negative one, and f of one is one. Zero is between them. [pause]
> So by the Intermediate Value Theorem, f of c equals zero for some c between zero and one. [long pause]

## A given value
**On screen:** g(x) = cos x − x defined and stated continuous, its values at 0 and π/2 with opposite signs, and the IVT conclusion.
> Show that cosine x equals x for some x between zero and pi over two. [try it]
> Turn it into a root problem: let g of x be cosine x minus x. It's continuous on the interval.
> g of zero is one, which is positive. g of pi over two is negative pi over two, which is negative. [pause]
> By the Intermediate Value Theorem, g of c is zero somewhere in between, and there cosine c equals c. [long pause]

## Reading a table
**On screen:** An AP-style table of a continuous function g at x equals 1, 3, 4, 7: values 5, negative 2, 1, 6. Arrows mark sign changes between 1 and 3, and between 3 and 4.
> On the AP exam, you'll often see a table. Here g is continuous, and its values go from five down to negative two, then up to one. [pause]
> Each time the values cross zero, the theorem guarantees a root in that interval. So g has at least two zeros between x equals one and x equals four. [long pause]

## Writing the justification
**On screen:** A banner: "On the AP exam: expect an IVT or MVT justification." A model sentence appears with its key phrases highlighted: "Since g is continuous on [1, 3] and g of 1 is greater than 0, which is greater than g of 3, by the Intermediate Value Theorem there is a value c in (1, 3) with g of c equals 0."
> When you use the theorem, say three things. [pause] The function is continuous on the closed interval. The target value is between the endpoint values. Then name the theorem and state its conclusion. [pause]
> Learn this sentence. Justifications like this are one of the most important skills on the AP exam, and you will almost certainly be asked for one: either the Intermediate Value Theorem, or its cousin, the Mean Value Theorem, which comes in Unit 5. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The hiker at the summit, looking back at the dashed 3500-foot line on the trail.
> A continuous function can't skip values. That's the Intermediate Value Theorem, and it closes out our work on limits and continuity. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A root in an interval
**On screen:** "Show that x⁴ − 2x − 5 = 0 has a solution between x = 1 and x = 2." The work: f(1) = −6, f(2) = 7, a number line with 0 between them. The full justification sentence builds with its key phrases highlighted.
> Show that x to the fourth minus two x minus five equals zero has a solution between one and two. [pause]
> Call the left side f. It's a polynomial, so it's continuous on the closed interval from one to two.
> f of one is one minus two minus five, negative six. f of two is sixteen minus four minus five, seven. [pause]
> Zero is between negative six and seven. So by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there's a c between one and two where f of c is zero.

## Example 2: Counting from a table
**On screen:** A table for a continuous function h: x = 0, 2, 5, 8 with h(x) = 3, −1, 4, 2. The question: "What is the least number of solutions of h(x) = 1 on [0, 8]?" A dashed line at height 1 over a dot plot. Crossings are marked on [0, 2] and [2, 5]; [5, 8] gets a gray "no guarantee."
> h is continuous. What's the least number of solutions of h of x equals one on the interval from zero to eight? [pause]
> Go interval by interval. From zero to two, h goes from three to negative one. One is in between, so there's a solution.
> From two to five, h goes from negative one to four. One is in between again: another solution. [pause]
> From five to eight, h goes from four to two. One is not between them, so the theorem promises nothing there.
> At least two solutions. [pause]

## Example 3: When the theorem doesn't apply
**On screen:** "f(x) = 1/(x − 1) has f(0) = −1 and f(2) = 1. Must f(c) = 0 for some c in (0, 2)?" The graph shows the two branches split by a dashed asymptote at x = 1, and neither branch touches the x-axis.
> f of x is one over x minus one. f of zero is negative one and f of two is one. Must f equal zero somewhere in between? [pause]
> It's tempting to say yes. But check the first condition: is f continuous on the closed interval from zero to two?
> No. At x equals one, there's a vertical asymptote. [pause]
> The theorem doesn't apply, and in fact, one over x minus one is never zero. Always check continuity before you use the theorem. [long pause]
