# 1.2 Defining Limits and Using Limit Notation

## Inputs and outputs
**On screen:** A function drawn as a machine: a number goes in the top, a number comes out the bottom. The machine is labeled f(x) = 2x + 1. Input 3 drops in slowly and 7 comes out; then 0 goes in and 1 comes out. Each pair stays on screen as a little input-output record.
> Start with the most basic idea in this course: a function. [pause]
> A function takes an input and gives back exactly one output. [pause]
> Put in three, and this one gives back seven. [pause]
> Put in zero, and it gives back one.
> Everything this year is about how outputs respond to inputs. [pause]

## Approaching
**On screen:** A number line for inputs above a number line for outputs. A dot on the input line creeps toward 3: 2.9, 2.99, 2.999. Below, the matching output dot creeps toward 7: 6.8, 6.98, 6.998.
> Now watch the inputs move. [pause]
> Feed in two point nine, then two point nine nine, then two point nine nine nine. The inputs are approaching three.
> The outputs follow: six point eight, six point nine eight, six point nine nine eight. They're approaching seven. [pause]
> For this function that's no surprise. Put in three itself, and you get seven.
> But what if you can't put in the number you're approaching? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.2 · Defining limits and using limit notation."

## A function with a hole
**On screen:** The formula g of x equals x squared minus 1, over x minus 1. Below it, the substitution x equals 1 turns into 0 over 0.
> Here is a function that causes trouble at one input. g of x equals x squared minus one, over x minus one.
> Try x equals one, and you get zero over zero. The function simply isn't defined there.

## What the graph does near the hole
**On screen:** The graph appears: the line y equals x plus 1 with an open circle at the point (1, 2). A dot slides along the line toward the hole from the left, then from the right, with its coordinates displayed.
> Everywhere else, the graph is the line y equals x plus one, with a single point missing.
> Slide toward x equals one from the left: 0.9, 0.99, 0.999. The outputs are 1.9, 1.99, 1.999.
> From the right, the outputs are 2.1, 2.01, 2.001.
> The outputs close in on 2, even though g of 1 doesn't exist.

## The closeness game
**On screen:** A horizontal band of height 0.5 appears around y equals 2. A vertical window around x equals 1 appears, narrow enough that the graph inside the window stays inside the band. The band shrinks to 0.1, then 0.01; each time, a narrower window answers it.
> Here is a way to make "closing in" exact. Someone draws a band around the height 2. It can be as thin as they like.
> Your job is to find a window around x equals one where the graph stays inside the band.
> Make the band thinner, and a thinner window still works. There is always a window that works.
> When you can win this game for every band, no matter how thin, the limit is 2. [pause]
> This game is the formal definition of a limit, and it's good to know the idea is airtight. But the AP exam won't test it. For the exam, a clear intuitive sense of "approaching" is what you need. [pause]

## The definition in words
**On screen:** The definition builds line by line: "The limit of f of x as x approaches c is L if f of x can be made as close to L as we like by taking x close enough to c, but not equal to c."
> That's the definition. The limit of f of x as x approaches c is L if we can make f of x as close to L as we like, just by taking x close enough to c.
> Notice the last part: x gets close to c, but never equals c. The limit never looks at the point itself.

## The notation
**On screen:** The expression "limit as x approaches 1 of g of x equals 2" is written in symbols. Each piece is labeled: "lim", "x approaches 1", "g of x", "equals 2".
> In symbols, we write the limit as x approaches one of g of x equals two.
> The arrow says what x is doing. The function is what we watch. The number on the right is where the outputs are heading.

## Symbols to words
**On screen:** The limit statement, with the input part and the output part each translated into words, then the full sentence.
> T of t is the temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, t hours after noon. What does the limit of T of t, as t approaches three, equals seventy-two mean? [try it]
> Read the input, then the output. t approaching three means the time approaches three PM. T approaching seventy-two means the temperature approaches seventy-two degrees.
> So: as the time gets closer and closer to three PM, the temperature gets closer and closer to seventy-two degrees. [long pause]

## Words to symbols
**On screen:** The sentence, then x → −2⁻ with the superscript minus circled, then the full limit statement.
> Now the other direction. Write this in limit notation: as x approaches negative two from the left, h of x approaches seven. [try it]
> From the left means a small minus sign up and to the right of the negative two.
> So it's the limit, as x approaches negative two from the left, of h of x, equals seven. [long pause]

## The point doesn't matter
**On screen:** A second function is drawn: the same line, but with a filled dot floating at (1, 5) above the open circle. The dot slides in again from both sides and still heads to 2.
> Here's the same line with the point at x equals one moved up to 5. The limit is still 2.
> The value at c, the limit as x approaches c: these are two different questions, and they can have different answers.

## Reading a graph
**On screen:** A parabola-shaped graph with a hole at (2, 3) and a filled dot at (2, 1). f(2) = 1 is marked at the dot; the limit 3 is marked at the hole.
> Use the graph of f to find f of two, and the limit of f of x as x approaches two. [try it]
> f of two is the filled dot: one.
> As x approaches two from both sides, the graph heads to the hole at height three. So the limit is three, even though f of two is one. [long pause]

## Three ways to see a limit
**On screen:** Three panels side by side: the graph with its hole, a table of x values approaching 1 with outputs approaching 2, and the algebra (x squared minus 1 factors to x minus 1 times x plus 1; cancel; get x plus 1; the outputs head to 2).
> You can find limits from a graph, from a table, or from the formula. The next few lessons take each of these in turn.

## Close
**On screen:** The graph of g with its open circle at (1, 2). The limit statement sits beside it. The open circle pulses.
> A limit is about where the outputs are heading, not where they arrive. [pause]
> Even when a function has a hole, it can still have a limit there. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A limit from a table
**On screen:** "Estimate the limit as x approaches 2 of (x² + x − 6)/(x − 2)." A table fills in: x = 1.9, 1.99, 2.01, 2.1 with outputs 4.9, 4.99, 5.01, 5.1. At x = 2 the cell reads "0/0, undefined."
> Estimate the limit as x approaches two of x squared plus x minus six, over x minus two. [pause]
> At x equals two itself, we get zero over zero. So we look near two instead.
> At one point nine, the output is four point nine. At one point nine nine, four point nine nine. [pause]
> From the other side: two point zero one gives five point zero one, and two point one gives five point one.
> From both sides, the outputs are heading to five. [pause] So the limit is five, even though the function has no value at two.

## Example 2: Value versus limit
**On screen:** A graph: a curve heading into an open circle at (2, 3) from both sides, with a filled dot at (2, 1) below it. The question: "Find f(2) and the limit as x approaches 2 of f(x)."
> Here's a graph. Find f of two, and the limit of f of x as x approaches two. [pause]
> f of two asks for the actual point. The filled dot is at height one, so f of two is one.
> The limit asks where the graph is heading as x gets close to two. From both sides the curve runs into the open circle at height three. [pause]
> So the limit is three. The value and the limit are different numbers, and that's allowed. [pause]

## Example 3: Words and symbols
**On screen:** Top: "As t approaches 4, P(t) approaches 90." Below it, the symbols assemble: lim, t → 4, P(t), = 90. Then the reverse: the symbols "lim as x → −1 of h(x) = 6" translate into the sentence "As x approaches −1, h(x) approaches 6."
> Write this in limit notation: as t approaches four, P of t approaches ninety. [pause]
> The variable and where it's going go under the lim: t approaching four. Then the function, P of t. Then where the outputs are heading: equals ninety.
> Now go the other way. The limit as x approaches negative one of h of x equals six. [pause]
> In words: as x approaches negative one, h of x approaches six.
> Notice that neither statement tells you P of four or h of negative one. [long pause]
