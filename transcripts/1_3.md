# 1.3 Estimating Limit Values from Graphs

## Two roads to one point
**On screen:** A graph with a jump at x equals 2: the left piece rises to an open circle at height 3; the right piece starts at a filled dot at height 4 and continues. Two small cars drive toward x equals 2, one on each piece.
> Picture two cars driving toward the same spot on the x-axis, x equals 2. One comes from the left along this piece of the graph. The other comes from the right along that piece.
> Watch the heights, not the positions. [pause]
> The left car's height heads toward 3. [pause] The right car's height heads toward 4.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.3 · Estimating limit values from graphs."

## One-sided limits
**On screen:** Under the left car: "limit as x approaches 2 from the left of f of x equals 3", with the small minus sign highlighted. Under the right car: the same with a plus sign, equals 4.
> Each car gives a one-sided limit. From the left, we write x approaches 2 with a small minus sign, and the limit is 3. [pause]
> From the right, a small plus sign, and the limit is 4. [pause]

## When the two sides disagree
**On screen:** The two one-sided results sit next to each other, 3 and 4, with a "not equal" sign. The two-sided limit notation appears with "does not exist" beside it.
> For the ordinary two-sided limit to exist, both cars have to be heading to the same height.
> Here they aren't. [pause] So the limit as x approaches 2 of f of x does not exist.
> Notice that the filled dot at height 4 changes nothing. The limit only cares about the approach. [long pause]

## The rule
**On screen:** A boxed statement: "If f is defined on both sides of c: the limit as x approaches c of f of x equals L exactly when both one-sided limits equal L." Below it, in red: "Different one-sided limits: the limit does not exist."
> That gives us the rule. When the function is defined on both sides of c, the limit exists and equals L exactly when the left-hand and right-hand limits both equal L. [pause]
> And if the two one-sided limits are different, the limit does not exist. That part has no exceptions. [pause]

## At the edge of the domain
**On screen:** The graph of the square root of x, starting at the origin. The region left of the y-axis is shaded gray and labeled "no function here." A single car drives in from the right toward the origin.
> Why did the rule say "defined on both sides"? [pause]
> Look at the square root of x near zero. To the left of zero, there's no function at all. Only one car can drive in.
> At an edge like this, the limit is just the one-sided limit from the side where the function lives. As x approaches zero, the square root of x approaches zero. [pause]
> You'll usually see this written as a right-hand limit, x approaching zero from the right. That version is always safe. [pause]

## Three ways a limit can fail
**On screen:** Three small graphs appear side by side and are labeled one at a time: absolute value of x over x (a jump from negative 1 to 1), one over x squared (both sides shooting up), and sine of one over x (wiggles packing tighter near zero).
> Limits can fail to exist in three main ways. [pause]
> First, a jump, like the absolute value of x, over x. The left side heads to negative one, and the right side heads to one. [pause]
> Second, unbounded growth, like one over x squared. The outputs don't head toward any number. They just grow without bound.
> We write that this limit equals infinity, but infinity isn't a number, so the limit still doesn't exist. [pause]

## Oscillation
**On screen:** The camera zooms toward the origin on the graph of sine of one over x. At every zoom level the curve still swings between negative 1 and 1, faster and faster.
> The third way is the strangest. Sine of one over x swings between negative one and one.
> Zoom in toward zero, and it swings faster. Zoom in again, and it swings faster still.
> It never settles on a single height, so there is no limit. [long pause]

## Graphs can hide things
**On screen:** A graphing calculator screen shows x squared minus 1, over x minus 1: it looks like an unbroken line. A zoomed inset reveals the missing point at (1, 2).
> One warning. A graph on a screen is drawn from a finite number of points.
> This calculator graph looks like a solid line, but the function has a hole at x equals one. The picture is too coarse to show it. [pause]
> Graphs are great for estimating limits, but be ready to check with a table or with algebra.

## Reading a full graph
**On screen:** A graph with several features: a removable hole at x equals negative 1 (with a stray dot below it), and a jump at x equals 2. A cursor visits each point, and the corresponding one-sided and two-sided limits are written beside the graph.
> Put it together on one graph. At x equals negative one, both sides head to 2, so the limit is 2, even though the function's value there is 0.
> At x equals 2, the left side heads to negative one and the right side to one. The one-sided limits exist, but the two-sided limit does not. [pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The two cars from the opening return, both heading to the same height this time, meeting at an open circle: the limit.
> So the key here: to read a limit from a graph, look at the height the graph heads toward from the left and from the right. If both sides head to the same height, that height is the limit. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A hole with a stray dot
**On screen:** A graph near x = −2: from the left, the line y = x + 3 runs into an open circle at (−2, 1); from the right, the line y = −x − 1 leaves the same open circle. A filled dot sits at (−2, 4). The questions: the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit, the limit, and g(−2).
> Here's a graph of g near x equals negative two. Find the one-sided limits, the limit, and g of negative two. [pause]
> From the left, the graph climbs into the open circle at height one. So the left-hand limit is one.
> From the right, the graph comes down into the same open circle. The right-hand limit is also one. [pause]
> Both sides agree, so the limit as x approaches negative two is one.
> And g of negative two? That's the filled dot, up at four. [pause] The value and the limit are different, and that's fine.

## Example 2: A jump
**On screen:** A graph near x = 1: from the left, the parabola y = x² runs into an open circle at (1, 1); from the right, the line y = 3 − x starts at a filled dot at (1, 2). The same four questions.
> Now x equals one on a different graph. [pause]
> From the left, the parabola heads to height one. From the right, the line starts at height two.
> The left-hand limit is one and the right-hand limit is two. [pause]
> They disagree, so the limit as x approaches one does not exist.
> The filled dot tells us g of one is two. It belongs to the right piece, but it doesn't rescue the limit. [pause]

## Example 3: Running off to infinity
**On screen:** The graph of h(x) = 1/(x − 3) with a dashed vertical asymptote at x = 3. Arrows show the left branch diving down and the right branch shooting up. The notation appears: the limit from the left is −∞, the limit from the right is ∞, the limit does not exist.
> Last one. Find the one-sided limits and the limit of h of x equals one over x minus three, at x equals three. [pause]
> From the left, the graph dives down without bound. We write that the left-hand limit is negative infinity.
> From the right, it shoots up. The right-hand limit is infinity. [pause]
> Infinity isn't a number, and the two sides don't even agree, so the limit as x approaches three does not exist. [long pause]
