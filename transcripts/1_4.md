# 1.4 Estimating Limit Values from Tables

## A limit with no graph handy
**On screen:** The expression sine of x over x. Substituting x equals 0 gives 0 over 0, which fades.
> Here's a limit we'll need later in the course: sine of x, over x, as x approaches zero.
> Plug in zero and you get zero over zero. [pause] That doesn't tell us the limit. So instead of plugging in, we'll collect evidence.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.4 · Estimating limit values from tables."

## Building the table
**On screen:** A two-row table fills in from the outside toward the middle. Left of zero: x equals negative 0.1, negative 0.01, negative 0.001. Right of zero: 0.001, 0.01, 0.1. The outputs appear: 0.998334, 0.999983, 0.99999983 on both sides. The digits the outputs agree on turn bright.
> Pick inputs close to zero from both sides, and let them get closer by a factor of ten each time.
> At plus or minus 0.1, the output is 0.998. At 0.01, it's 0.99998. At 0.001, it's 0.9999998.
> The nines keep piling up. [pause] The outputs are closing in on one.
> So we estimate that the limit as x approaches zero of sine x over x is one. [long pause]

## What makes a good table
**On screen:** A checklist builds: both sides; inputs shrinking by factors of ten; outputs that converge on one value.
> A good table has three things. Inputs from both sides. Inputs that keep getting closer, not just one or two. And outputs that converge on one value.

## Your own table
**On screen:** A table with x = 0.9, 0.99, 0.999 on the left of 1 and 1.001, 1.01, 1.1 on the right, outputs filling in toward 3 from both sides.
> Use a table to estimate the limit of x cubed minus one, over x minus one, as x approaches one. [pause]
> From the left, try point nine, point nine nine, and point nine nine nine. The outputs are two point seven one, two point nine seven, two point nine nine seven.
> From the right, one point one, one point zero one, one point zero zero one: three point three one, three point zero three, three point zero zero three. [pause]
> Both sides close in on three. [long pause]

## Trap one: unlucky inputs
**On screen:** A new table for sine of pi over x at x equals 0.1, 0.01, 0.001. Every output is 0. Then the graph of sine of pi over x appears, oscillating wildly near zero, with the three sampled points sitting exactly on the axis.
> Tables can fool you. [pause] Try sine of pi over x at 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001. Every output is exactly zero.
> It looks like the limit is zero. But the graph tells a different story. The function oscillates between negative one and one, faster and faster.
> Our inputs just happened to land where the wave crosses the axis. This limit does not exist. [long pause]

## Trap two: too close for the calculator
**On screen:** A calculator screen evaluates one minus cosine x, over x squared. At x equals 0.001 it shows 0.49999996. At 10 to the negative 9 it shows 0.
> Here's a different trap. One minus cosine of x, over x squared, heads to one half.
> But at x equals ten to the negative nine, a calculator reports zero. Cosine of x rounds to exactly one, and the true answer gets lost in rounding. [pause]
> When outputs suddenly jump or collapse at tiny inputs, suspect the calculator, not the math.

## Reading a given table
**On screen:** An AP-style table: x values 1.9, 1.99, 1.999 with outputs 3.9, 3.99, 3.999; then 2.001, 2.01, 2.1 with outputs 5.001, 5.01, 5.1. The left side gets a bracket labeled "heading to 4", the right side "heading to 5".
> On a test, the table is often handed to you. Read each side separately.
> From the left, the outputs head to 4. From the right, they head to 5.
> The one-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided limit doesn't exist. [pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The sine x over x table returns, beside a small graph of the function with a hole at (0, 1).
> A table gives evidence, not proof. For that, we'll turn to algebra in the next two lessons, and to a clever squeeze in Topic one point eight. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A famous number
**On screen:** "Estimate the limit as x approaches 0 of (1 + x)^(1/x)." A table fills from the outside in. Right side: x = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 with outputs 2.5937, 2.7048, 2.7169. Left side: x = −0.1, −0.01, −0.001 with outputs 2.8680, 2.7320, 2.7196. The digits 2.7 turn bright on both sides.
> Estimate the limit as x approaches zero of one plus x, raised to the one over x. [pause]
> At zero, the exponent one over x makes no sense, so we build a table.
> From the right: two point five nine, then two point seven zero, then two point seven one seven. [pause]
> From the left: two point eight seven, then two point seven three, then two point seven one nine six.
> The left side comes down, the right side comes up, and they're squeezing in on about two point seven one eight. [pause]
> That number is so important it has its own letter, e. You'll meet it again in Unit 2.

## Example 2: The value in the middle doesn't count
**On screen:** A given table. x: 4.9, 4.99, 4.999, 5, 5.001, 5.01, 5.1. f(x): 7.2, 7.02, 7.002, 2, 6.998, 6.98, 6.8. The column for x = 5 is dimmed.
> This table even gives a value at x equals five. Find the limit as x approaches five. [pause]
> Ignore the middle column for now. From the left: seven point two, seven point zero two, seven point zero zero two. Heading to seven.
> From the right: six point nine nine eight, six point nine eight, six point eight. Also heading to seven. [pause]
> Both sides agree, so the limit is seven.
> The table says f of five is two. That's the value at the point, and the limit never looks at it. [pause]

## Example 3: Outputs that run away
**On screen:** "Estimate the limit as x approaches 2 of 1/(x − 2)²." A table: x = 1.9, 1.99, 1.999 and 2.001, 2.01, 2.1, with outputs 100, 10,000, 1,000,000 on both sides, growing.
> Estimate the limit as x approaches two of one over x minus two, squared. [pause]
> At one point nine and two point one, the output is a hundred.
> At one point nine nine and two point zero one, ten thousand. One step closer, a million. [pause]
> The outputs aren't settling on anything. They grow without bound on both sides.
> We write that the limit is infinity, which is a way of saying how it fails to exist. [long pause]
