# 1.5 Determining Limits Using Algebraic Properties of Limits

## From estimating to computing
**On screen:** Three thumbnails from the last lessons (a graph, a table, a formula) slide aside. The formula one grows to fill the screen.
> Graphs and tables let us estimate limits. Starting now, we compute them exactly. [pause]
> The key fact is that limits follow the same rules as ordinary arithmetic.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.5 · Determining limits using algebraic properties of limits."

## Adding two functions
**On screen:** Two graphs near x equals c: f heading to height 3, g heading to height 2. Vertical bars show each height at a sliding x. A third graph, f plus g, is drawn by stacking the bars. As x slides toward c, the stacked bar heads to 5.
> Here are two functions near x equals c. f of x heads to 3, and g of x heads to 2.
> Add them point by point. As x gets close to c, the sum gets close to 3 plus 2.
> So the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. [pause]

## The limit laws
**On screen:** A formula card fills in line by line: sum, difference, constant multiple, product, quotient, power. Each line flashes the example numbers 3 and 2: 5, 1, 6 (for 2 times f), 6, 1.5, 9 (for f squared).
> The same goes for differences, constant multiples, products and powers.
> Quotients work too, with one condition: the limit of the denominator can't be zero. [pause]
> Every one of these laws assumes the individual limits exist.

## Direct substitution
**On screen:** The polynomial x cubed minus 4x plus 1 as x approaches 2. The laws break it into pieces, each piece evaluated, then everything collapses into "plug in 2": 8 minus 8 plus 1 equals 1.
> Chain the laws together, and something convenient happens. For a polynomial, the limit as x approaches c is just the value at c. [pause]
> The same holds for a rational function, as long as the denominator isn't zero at c.
> This is direct substitution. Always try it first. [pause]

## Plug in
**On screen:** Each limit evaluated by direct substitution, with the denominator checked first in part (b).
> Find the limit of x cubed minus four x plus one as x approaches two, and the limit of x squared plus three over x minus two, as x approaches negative one. [try it]
> The first is a polynomial, so plug in: eight minus eight plus one. One.
> The second is a fraction. Check the bottom first: negative one minus two is negative three, not zero. [pause]
> So we can substitute: four over negative three. Negative four thirds. [long pause]

## Composite functions
**On screen:** The expression square root of (g of x). g of x heads to 9. The square root machine takes the incoming values and returns values heading to 3.
> For a composition, work from the inside out. If g of x heads to 9, then the square root of g of x heads to the square root of 9, which is 3. [pause]
> This works when the outer function behaves nicely at 9, meaning it's continuous there. Topic one point eleven makes that word precise.

## Using given limits
**On screen:** The two given limits, then each of the five parts worked on its own line with limit notation.
> The limit of f is three, and the limit of g is negative two, as x approaches four. Find five limits built from them. [try it]
> The laws let us work with the limits like numbers. Two f minus g: two times three, minus negative two. Eight.
> f times g: three times negative two. Negative six. [pause]
> f over g: three over negative two, since the bottom limit isn't zero. Negative three halves.
> f squared plus g: nine plus negative two. Seven. [pause]
> And the square root of f plus six uses the composite rule: the square root of three plus six, which is three. [long pause]

## Pieces and sides
**On screen:** A piecewise function: x squared plus 1 for x less than 2, and 3x minus 1 for x at least 2. The left piece is highlighted as x approaches 2 from the left; its value 5 appears. Then the right piece, also 5.
> For a piecewise function, use the piece that lives on each side.
> From the left, x squared plus one heads to 5. From the right, three x minus one also heads to 5.
> The sides agree, so the limit is 5. [pause]

## A limit that exists anyway
**On screen:** One function at a time. First f, written as cases (2 for x < 1, −1 for x ≥ 1) and graphed: two flat pieces with a jump at x = 1. Then g(x) = x − 1, written and graphed as a line through (1, 0). Then a new function h(x) = f(x) · g(x) is built by multiplying piece by piece: 2(x − 1) on the left, −(x − 1) on the right. f and g fade, and h's graph is drawn alone: a V-like shape whose two pieces both meet at (1, 0). Finally the one-sided limits of h, each written in full: both are 0, so the limit of h at 1 is 0.
> Here's a surprise. [pause] Meet the first function, f. It's two to the left of one, and negative one from one on. [pause]
> At one, f jumps, so its limit at one does not exist. [pause]
> Now a second function: g of x equals x minus one. It's a line, and at one it passes through zero. So the limit of g at one is zero. [pause]
> Build a new function by multiplying them. Call it h: h of x is f of x times g of x. [pause]
> On the left, f is two, so h is two times x minus one. On the right, f is negative one, so h is negative x minus one. [pause]
> Here's the graph of h by itself. The jump is gone. Both pieces are being multiplied by something shrinking to zero, so both pieces head to zero at one. [pause]
> From the left, two times x minus one heads to zero. From the right, negative x minus one heads to zero too. So the limit of h at one is zero. [pause]
> The product law can't be used here, because the limit of f doesn't exist. But checking each side still works. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The formula card of limit laws shrinks into the corner, next to the words "try direct substitution first."
> Direct substitution handles most limits in one step. The interesting cases are the ones where it yields zero over zero. That's the next lesson. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Using the laws
**On screen:** "The limit as x → 3 of f(x) is 4, and of g(x) is −2. Find (a) the limit of 3f(x) − f(x)g(x), and (b) the limit of f(x) / (g(x) + 5)." Each law is named as it's used: constant multiple, product, difference, quotient.
> Suppose the limit of f at three is four, and the limit of g at three is negative two. [pause]
> Part a: find the limit of three f minus f times g. [try it]
> Split it with the laws. Three times four is twelve. Four times negative two is negative eight.
> Twelve minus negative eight is twenty. [pause]
> Part b: find the limit of f over g plus five. [try it]
> The denominator heads to negative two plus five, which is three. That's not zero, so the quotient law applies.
> Four over three. [pause]

## Example 2: Direct substitution
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → −1 of (x² + 2x + 5)/(x + 3)." Substitution: (1 − 2 + 5)/(−1 + 3) = 4/2 = 2. A green check next to "denominator ≠ 0."
> Find the limit as x approaches negative one of x squared plus two x plus five, over x plus three. [pause]
> It's a rational function, so try direct substitution. The denominator is negative one plus three, which is two. Not zero, so we're allowed.
> The top is one minus two plus five, which is four. Four over two is two. [pause]

## Example 3: A composite and a piecewise
**On screen:** Left: "the limit as x → 2 of √(x + 7)" becomes √9 = 3. Right: f(x) = x² − 3 for x < 2 and 5 − x for x ≥ 2; the left piece gives 1, the right piece gives 3, and "DNE" appears.
> Two quick ones, both at x equals two. [pause]
> Part a: the limit as x approaches two of the square root of x plus seven. [try it]
> Inside first: x plus seven heads to nine. The square root of nine is three.
> Part b: a piecewise function, x squared minus three on the left of two, and five minus x from two on. Find its limit at two. [try it]
> From the left, four minus three is one. From the right, five minus two is three.
> The sides disagree, so the limit at two does not exist. [long pause]
