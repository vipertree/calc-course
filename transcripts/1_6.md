# 1.6 Determining Limits Using Algebraic Manipulation

## Zero over zero is a message
**On screen:** Direct substitution into x squared minus 9, over x minus 3, at x equals 3. The result 0 over 0 appears, then turns into a signpost that reads "more work needed."
> Try direct substitution on x squared minus nine, over x minus three, at x equals three. You get zero over zero.
> That result doesn't mean the limit is zero, and it doesn't mean the limit fails to exist. [pause] It means you need to do more work.
> The more work can go either way. [pause] Sometimes it turns up a number. Sometimes, even after all the algebra, the limit still doesn't exist.
> So zero over zero is a signpost that says "keep going." It doesn't promise what you'll find. [pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.6 · Determining limits using algebraic manipulation."

## Two functions, one missing point
**On screen:** Two graphs overlap: y equals x plus 3 (a full line) and x squared minus 9 over x minus 3 (the same line with a hole at (3, 6)). They coincide everywhere except the single point.
> Factor the top: x squared minus nine is x minus three times x plus three. Cancel the common factor, and you're left with x plus three. [pause]
> Careful, though. The two functions aren't identical. The original is undefined at three. The simplified one isn't. [pause]
> They agree at every other input, and a limit only looks at other inputs. So they have the same limit at three. [long pause]

## The key fact
**On screen:** A boxed statement: "If f of x equals g of x for all x near c, except possibly at c, then their limits at c are equal." Then the substitution: 3 plus 3 equals 6.
> That's the key fact. If two functions agree everywhere near c, except possibly at c itself, their limits at c are the same.
> So the limit is three plus three, which is six.

## Tool one: factor and cancel
**On screen:** x cubed minus 8, over x minus 2. The numerator factors as a difference of cubes; the common factor cancels; substitution gives 4 plus 4 plus 4 equals 12.
> Factoring is the first tool. [pause] For x cubed minus eight, over x minus two, use the difference of cubes: x minus two, times x squared plus two x plus four.
> The x minus two cancels. Substitute two into what's left: four plus four plus four, which is twelve. [pause]

## Factor
**On screen:** The limit, the 0/0 check, the difference-of-cubes factoring with x − 2 struck out top and bottom, and the result 12.
> Find the limit of x cubed minus eight, over x minus two, as x approaches two. [try it]
> Plugging in gives zero over zero, so there's a common factor to find.
> x cubed minus eight is a difference of cubes: x minus two, times x squared plus two x plus four. The x minus two cancels. [pause]
> Now plug in: four plus four plus four. Twelve. [long pause]

## Conjugates, a quick review
**On screen:** An algebra reminder before any limits: (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b². Then 1 over (√5 − 2) is multiplied top and bottom by (√5 + 2); the bottom becomes 5 − 4 = 1, leaving √5 + 2.
> Before the next tool, a quick review from algebra. [pause]
> The conjugate of a plus b is a minus b. Multiply them, and the middle terms cancel: you get a squared minus b squared. [pause]
> That's how you clear a square root out of a denominator. Take one over the square root of five, minus two. [pause]
> Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate, the square root of five plus two. The bottom becomes five minus four, which is one. [pause]
> So the fraction is just the square root of five plus two. The same move works inside a limit. [long pause]

## Tool two: the conjugate
**On screen:** Square root of x plus 4, minus 2, all over x. The top and bottom are multiplied by the conjugate. The numerator collapses to x; the x's cancel; substitution gives 1 over 4.
> Square roots call for the conjugate. [pause] Multiply the top and bottom by the square root of x plus four, plus two.
> The top becomes x plus four, minus four, which is just x. Cancel, substitute zero, and the limit is one over four. [pause]

## Conjugate
**On screen:** The limit, multiplied by the conjugate over itself, the top simplifying to x, the x's struck out, and 1/4.
> Find the limit of the square root of x plus four, minus two, all over x, as x approaches zero. [try it]
> Zero over zero, and there's a root minus a number on top. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate, root x plus four, plus two.
> On top, the square root squared is x plus four, minus four. That's just x. [pause]
> The x on top cancels the x on the bottom.
> Now plug in: one over two plus two. One fourth. [long pause]

## Tool three: clear the fractions
**On screen:** One over x, minus one third, all over x minus 3. Both small fractions combine over the common denominator 3x; the result simplifies to negative 1 over 3x; substitution gives negative 1 over 9.
> Fractions inside fractions? Combine them over a common denominator first.
> This one simplifies to negative one over three x, and at x equals three that's negative one ninth. [pause]

## Combine fractions
**On screen:** The complex fraction combined into one fraction, the x − 3 factors struck out, and −1/9.
> Find the limit of one over x minus one third, all over x minus three, as x approaches three. [try it]
> Combine the fractions on top over the common denominator three x: three minus x, over three x.
> Three minus x is the negative of x minus three, so the x minus three cancels. [pause]
> What's left is negative one over three x. At three, that's negative one ninth. [long pause]

## Tool four: trig identities
**On screen:** A unit circle with a point at angle x: the horizontal leg is cos x, the vertical leg is sin x, the radius is 1. The Pythagorean theorem turns into sin² x + cos² x = 1, boxed, with its two rearrangements beneath.
> Trig limits often yield to identities, and one identity matters more than all the others: sine squared plus cosine squared equals one. [pause]
> It's the Pythagorean theorem in disguise. On the unit circle, the point at angle x makes a right triangle with legs cosine x and sine x, and a hypotenuse of one. Leg squared plus leg squared equals hypotenuse squared. [pause]
> This is the one trig identity you're really expected to know for AP Calculus. Know it in every form: one minus cosine squared is sine squared, and one minus sine squared is cosine squared. [long pause]

## Tool four, in a limit
**On screen:** The limit of sine squared x over 1 minus cosine x as x approaches 0. Sine squared becomes 1 minus cosine squared, the difference of squares factors, the common factor cancels, and 1 plus cosine x heads to 2.
> Now the limit of sine squared x over one minus cosine x, at zero. Rewrite sine squared as one minus cosine squared, factor, and cancel. [pause]
> What's left, one plus cosine x, heads to two. [pause]

## Identity
**On screen:** The Pythagorean identity rewriting sin² x, the difference of squares factored, the common factor struck out, and 2.
> Find the limit of sine squared x, over one minus cosine x, as x approaches zero. [try it]
> Zero over zero. Use the Pythagorean identity to write sine squared as one minus cosine squared.
> That's a difference of squares: one minus cosine, times one plus cosine. The one minus cosine cancels. [pause]
> Plug in: one plus one. Two. [long pause]

## A different message
**On screen:** x plus 1 over x minus 3, at x equals 3: substitution gives 4 over 0. A small graph shows a vertical asymptote at x equals 3, with the two sides heading off in opposite directions.
> Not every stuck substitution is zero over zero. Four over zero is a different message. [pause]
> A nonzero number divided by something shrinking to zero grows without bound. There's no hole to fix here. There's an asymptote, and the limit does not exist.

## Close
**On screen:** A two-branch flowchart: substitute. If you get a number, done. If you get 0 over 0, rewrite and try again. If you get nonzero over 0, look for unbounded behavior.
> So substitute first, and read the result. A number means you're done. Zero over zero means rewrite. Nonzero over zero means something is heading to infinity. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Factor and cancel
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → −4 of (x² + x − 12)/(x + 4)." Substitution gives 0/0. Each line keeps the limit: the limit of (x + 4)(x − 3)/(x + 4); = the limit of (x − 3); = −7.
> Find the limit as x approaches negative four of x squared plus x minus twelve, over x plus four. [pause]
> Substitute first: sixteen minus four minus twelve is zero on top, and zero on the bottom. Zero over zero, so we rewrite.
> The top factors as x plus four times x minus three. [pause]
> Cancel the x plus four. We're allowed, because the limit never uses x equals negative four itself.
> Now substitute into x minus three: negative seven. [pause]

## Example 2: A conjugate in the denominator
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 9 of (x − 9)/(√x − 3)." Multiply top and bottom by √x + 3. The bottom becomes x − 9, which cancels. The limit of (√x + 3) is 6.
> Find the limit as x approaches nine of x minus nine, over the square root of x minus three. [pause]
> Substitution gives zero over zero. There's a square root, so reach for the conjugate: the square root of x, plus three.
> The bottom becomes x minus nine. [pause] That cancels with the top, leaving the square root of x plus three.
> Substitute nine: three plus three is six. [pause]

## Example 3: Zero over zero, and still no limit
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 1 of (x − 1)/(x² − 2x + 1)." Substitution gives 0/0. Factor: (x − 1)/(x − 1)². Cancel: 1/(x − 1). Substitution now gives 1/0. A small graph shows the two sides heading to −∞ and ∞, and "DNE" appears.
> Find the limit as x approaches one of x minus one, over x squared minus two x plus one. [pause]
> Substitution gives zero over zero, so we rewrite. The bottom is x minus one, squared.
> Cancel one factor of x minus one. What's left is one over x minus one. [pause]
> Substitute again: one over zero. The signpost has changed. [pause]
> Just left of one, the bottom is a tiny negative number, so the quotient heads to negative infinity. Just right of one, it heads to infinity.
> The limit does not exist. Zero over zero told us to keep going. It never promised a number. [long pause]
