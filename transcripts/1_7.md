# 1.7 Selecting Procedures for Determining Limits

## A pile of limits
**On screen:** Six limit problems tumble onto the screen like cards: a polynomial, a factorable rational, a square root difference, an absolute value quotient, a piecewise function, and a complex fraction.
> You now have several tools for limits. The hard part on a test is choosing the right one quickly.
> This lesson is about the choosing.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.7 · Selecting procedures for determining limits."

## Tool one: direct substitution
**On screen:** A toolkit tray slides up from the bottom with four empty slots. The first tool card drops in: "Direct substitution." Beside it, three possible results: "a number", "0 over 0", "nonzero over 0".
> Think of this lesson as a toolkit. The first tool is always the same: direct substitution. [pause]
> If you get a number, and the function is a nice formula near that point, you're finished. [pause]
> If you don't, the result still helps. It tells you which tool to reach for next. [long pause]

## Tool two: rewrite and cancel
**On screen:** The second card drops into the tray: "Rewrite and cancel." Three small examples appear under it: a factored polynomial, a square root times its conjugate, a fraction inside a fraction over a common denominator.
> Zero over zero means the expression is hiding a factor that can cancel. The second tool is to rewrite and cancel. [pause]
> Polynomials: factor. Square roots: multiply by the conjugate. Fractions inside fractions: clear them with a common denominator. [pause]
> Then substitute again. The algebra can uncover a number, or a nonzero over zero. [pause]
> Infinity over infinity is just as undecided as zero over zero. It shows up when x heads off to infinity, and topic one point fifteen rewrites it the same way. [pause]

## Tool three: special identities
**On screen:** The third card drops in: "Special identities." Under it, sine squared x plus cosine squared x equals one, and sine squared x rewritten as (1 minus cosine x)(1 plus cosine x).
> The third tool is special identities, and the trig ones are the stars. [pause]
> Sine squared plus cosine squared equals one lets you trade sine squared for one minus cosine squared, which factors. Then something cancels. [long pause]

## Tool four: split into sides
**On screen:** The fourth card drops in: "Split into sides." A number line near c, with one formula on the left side and another on the right. Under it: "sides agree: that's the limit · sides disagree: does not exist".
> The fourth tool is to split into sides. Use it when the formula changes at the point, such as a piecewise function or an absolute value. [pause]
> On each side, use the formula that applies there, and find that one-sided limit. [pause]
> If the two sides agree, that's the limit. If they disagree, the limit does not exist. [long pause]
## A number over zero
**On screen:** The first card's third result, "nonzero over 0", lights up. Beside it: "3/0: no algebra fixes it · the function heads off toward infinity · which way: topic 1.14". A small graph of 1/(x − 2) shows both sides heading off along a dashed line at x = 2.
> One more message from substitution: a nonzero number over zero, such as three over zero. [pause]
> No algebra will fix that. Near the point, the bottom is tiny, so the function heads off toward infinity, and the limit isn't a number. [pause]
> Topic one point fourteen slows down on what's happening there, and how to tell which way each side goes. For now, recognize the message. [long pause]
## Absolute value
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 2 of |x − 2|/(x − 2)." The number line splits at 2: on the left, |x − 2| becomes −(x − 2) and the quotient is −1; on the right it's x − 2 and the quotient is 1. Then "does not exist."
> Here's splitting into sides for an absolute value. Find the limit of the absolute value of x minus two, over x minus two, as x approaches two. [pause]
> The formula changes at two. From the left, x minus two is negative, so its absolute value is the negative of x minus two. The ratio is negative one.
> From the right, x minus two is positive, and the ratio is one. [pause]
> Negative one and one disagree, so the limit does not exist. [long pause]
## Two tools in one
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → −4 of (x² + 2x − 8)/(√(x + 5) − 1)." Substitution yields 0/0. The top factors as (x + 4)(x − 2) and the bottom is multiplied by its conjugate; the bottom becomes x + 4; the x + 4 factors are struck out; substitution gives (−6)(2) = −12.
> Here's one that needs two tools. Find the limit of x squared plus two x minus eight, over the square root of x plus five, minus one, as x approaches negative four. [pause]
> Substituting yields zero over zero. Factor the top into x plus four times x minus two. The square root on the bottom says conjugate: multiply top and bottom by the square root of x plus five, plus one.
> The bottom becomes x plus five minus one, which is x plus four. It cancels with the top. [pause]
> Plug in: negative six times two. Negative twelve. [long pause]
## Close
**On screen:** The full toolkit tray: direct substitution, rewrite and cancel, special identities, split into sides. A fifth slot, outlined but empty, is labeled "coming later: L'Hospital's Rule."
> Four tools: substitute, rewrite and cancel, special identities, and split into sides. Let the form of the expression pick the tool. [pause]
> There's one more slot in the toolkit. Later in the year, once we can take derivatives, a cool result called L'Hospital's Rule will handle zero over zero and infinity over infinity in a new way. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Nothing to fix
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 2 of (x² + 1)/(x + 3)." Substitution: 5/5 = 1. The toolkit lights "direct substitution."
> Find the limit as x approaches two of x squared plus one, over x plus three. [pause]
> Substitute first. The top is five, the bottom is five. We got a number, and the denominator isn't zero.
> The limit is one. No algebra needed. [pause] Don't do more work than the problem asks for.

## Example 2: Fractions inside a fraction
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 0 of [1/(x + 2) − 1/2] / x." Substitution yields 0/0; the toolkit lights "rewrite and cancel." Each line keeps the limit: the top becomes (2 − (x + 2)) / (2(x + 2)) = −x / (2(x + 2)); dividing by x gives −1/(2(x + 2)); the limit is −1/4.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of one over x plus two, minus one half, all over x. [pause]
> Substitution yields one half minus one half, over zero. Zero over zero.
> The form is a fraction inside a fraction, so combine the top over the common denominator two times x plus two. [pause]
> The top becomes negative x, over two times x plus two. Dividing by x cancels the x, leaving negative one over two times x plus two.
> Now substitute zero: negative one over four. [pause]

## Example 3: A piecewise function
**On screen:** "f(x) = x² + 1 for x < 1, 3x − 1 for x ≥ 1. Find the limit of f(x) as x → 1." The toolkit lights "split into sides." Left: 1 + 1 = 2. Right: 3 − 1 = 2. Both sides agree: the limit is 2.
> Let f of x be x squared plus one for x less than one, and three x minus one for x at least one. Find the limit of f of x as x approaches one. [pause]
> The formula changes at one, so split into sides. [pause]
> From the left, use x squared plus one: one plus one, two.
> From the right, use three x minus one: three minus one, two. [pause]
> Both sides give two, so the limit is two. [long pause]
