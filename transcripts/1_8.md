# 1.8 Determining Limits Using the Squeeze Theorem

## A function that won't hold still
**On screen:** The graph of x squared times sine of one over x, with an open circle at the origin labeled "undefined at x = 0." The view zooms in twice; at every zoom the curve is still a tangle of wiggles, packed tighter and tighter toward zero, each one smaller than the last.
> Here's x squared times sine of one over x. Near zero, the sine part oscillates wildly, just like it did in topic one point three.
> Notice that it isn't even defined at zero: one over zero is meaningless. [pause]
> Zoom in, and the wiggles keep coming, faster and faster. Zoom in again, and there are still more. [pause]
> But this time the wiggles are shrinking. None of our algebra tools can touch this function. It needs a new idea.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.8 · Determining limits using the squeeze theorem."

## Trapped between two parabolas
**On screen:** The parabolas y equals x squared and y equals negative x squared appear, dashed. The wiggly curve bounces between them, touching one and then the other. Near the origin the two parabolas pinch together at (0, 0).
> Sine of anything stays between negative one and one. Multiply through by x squared, which is never negative.
> So our function is trapped between negative x squared and x squared. [pause]
> Both of those parabolas head to zero as x approaches zero. The function is caught in between, with nowhere to go but zero. [long pause]

## The theorem
**On screen:** A formula card: if g of x is at most f of x, which is at most h of x, near c, and g and h have the same limit L at c, then f also has limit L. A small cartoon of two walls closing in on a ball illustrates it.
> That's the squeeze theorem. If a function is trapped between two others near c, and the two outer functions head to the same limit, the one in the middle has no choice. [pause] It heads there too. [long pause]

## The most important squeeze
**On screen:** A unit circle with an angle x marked in the first quadrant. Three regions are shaded in turn: a small triangle inside the sector, the circular sector itself, and a larger triangle that reaches up to the tangent line at (1, 0).
> Now for the squeeze that matters most in this course. In Topic one point four, a table suggested that sine x over x heads to one. A picture on the unit circle proves it. [pause]
> On a unit circle, draw an angle x. Compare three areas: a small triangle, the slice of the circle, and a larger triangle that reaches the tangent line. [pause]
> The small triangle has area one half sine x. The slice has area one half x. The large triangle has area one half tangent x. [long pause]

## Rearranging the inequality
**On screen:** The inequality of the three areas: one half sine x, at most one half x, at most one half tangent x. The halves cancel; each piece is divided by sine x; everything is flipped. The result: cosine x is at most sine x over x, which is at most 1.
> The areas are in order, so one half sine x is at most one half x, which is at most one half tangent x.
> Clear the halves, and divide everything by sine x. For a small positive angle, sine x is positive, so dividing doesn't change the direction of the inequalities. [pause]
> That gives one, at most x over sine x, at most one over cosine x.
> Now flip every fraction. Flipping positive numbers turns the inequalities around, and we get: cosine x is at most sine x over x, which is at most one. [pause]

## The squeeze closes
**On screen:** A graph with the curve y equals cosine x below, the line y equals 1 above, and the graph of sine x over x in between, with a hole at (0, 1). All three meet at height 1.
> As x approaches zero, cosine x heads to one, and one stays at one. [pause]
> Sine x over x is squeezed in between, so its limit is one. [long pause]
> The same argument works for negative x, since sine x over x has the same value at x and at negative x.

## Match the angle
**On screen:** The limit rewritten as 5 · sin(5x)/(5x), with the matching angles highlighted, then 5 · 1 = 5.
> Find the limit of sine five x over x, as x approaches zero. [try it]
> The special limit needs the same thing inside the sine and on the bottom. So multiply top and bottom by five: five times sine five x over five x.
> As x goes to zero, the angle five x goes to zero too, so sine five x over five x goes to one. [pause]
> The limit is five times one: five. [long pause]

## Rewrite first
**On screen:** tan x rewritten as sin x / cos x, split into two factors, and 1 · 1 = 1.
> Find the limit of tangent x over x, as x approaches zero. [try it]
> Rewrite tangent as sine over cosine: sine x over x, times one over cosine x. [pause]
> The first factor goes to one, and cosine of zero is one. So the limit is one. [long pause]

## Squeeze with a bounded factor
**On screen:** The bound on cosine, the squeeze −|x| ≤ x cos(1/x²) ≤ |x|, both bounds going to 0, and the conclusion.
> Find the limit of x times cosine of one over x squared, as x approaches zero. [try it]
> Cosine of anything is between negative one and one.
> Multiply by x, using absolute values so the inequality works for negative x too: x cosine one over x squared is between negative absolute x and absolute x. [pause]
> Both bounds go to zero as x goes to zero.
> So by the squeeze theorem, the limit is zero. [long pause]

## A second special limit
**On screen:** One minus cosine x, over x. It is multiplied by one plus cosine x over itself; the numerator becomes sine squared x; the expression splits into sine x over x, times sine x over one plus cosine x. The limit becomes 1 times 0 over 2, which is 0.
> With that result, a second limit follows. [pause] Multiply one minus cosine x, over x, by one plus cosine x over itself.
> The top becomes sine squared x. Split it as sine x over x, times sine x over one plus cosine x.
> That's one times zero over two, so the limit is zero. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** Two boxed results side by side: the limit of sine x over x is 1, and the limit of one minus cosine x, over x, is 0. A note: x in radians.
> Keep both of these results. The first one comes back in Unit 2, where it gives us the derivative of sine.

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A squeeze with cosine
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 0 of x cos(3/x)." The graph wiggles between the dashed lines y = |x| and y = −|x|. The inequality builds: −1 ≤ cos(3/x) ≤ 1, then −|x| ≤ x cos(3/x) ≤ |x|.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of x times cosine of three over x. [pause]
> Cosine of three over x swings wildly near zero, so no table or algebra will pin it down. But cosine is always between negative one and one.
> Multiply by x. Since x can be negative, we use absolute values: the function is between negative absolute x and absolute x. [pause]
> Both of those head to zero. So by the squeeze theorem, the limit is zero. [pause]

## Example 2: Squeezed by a line and a parabola
**On screen:** "4x − 9 ≤ f(x) ≤ x² − 4x + 7 for all x. Find the limit as x → 4 of f(x)." The line and parabola are drawn, touching at (4, 7). A shaded channel between them pinches to that point.
> For all x, f of x is between four x minus nine and x squared minus four x plus seven. Find the limit of f at four. [pause]
> Evaluate the bottom function at four: sixteen minus nine is seven.
> Evaluate the top: sixteen minus sixteen plus seven, also seven. [pause]
> Both outer functions head to seven, so f is squeezed to seven as well.

## Example 3: Using the special limit
**On screen:** "Find the limit as x → 0 of sin(5x)/(3x)." The expression is rewritten step by step: (5/3) · sin(5x)/(5x). Under sin(5x)/(5x), a note: "u = 5x → 0." The limit becomes (5/3) · 1 = 5/3.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of sine of five x, over three x. [pause]
> We know the limit of sine u over u is one, but only when the angle and the denominator match. [pause]
> So make them match. Multiply and divide by five: this is five thirds, times sine of five x, over five x.
> As x approaches zero, five x approaches zero too, so that fraction heads to one.
> The limit is five thirds. [long pause]
