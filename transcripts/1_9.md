# 1.9 Connecting Multiple Representations of Limits

## One limit, four views
**On screen:** Four panels around a central question mark: a formula, a graph, a table, and a sentence in words. All four describe the same function near x equals 2.
> A function can reach you as a formula, a graph, a table, or a sentence. The limit is the same no matter how the function is described. [pause]
> AP questions like to mix these views, sometimes in a single problem.

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 1.9 · Connecting multiple representations of limits."

## Each view has a strength
**On screen:** Each panel is labeled with its strength: the formula gives exact values; the graph shows behavior at a glance, including sides and asymptotes; the table gives evidence near a point; the sentence gives meaning and units.
> Each view is good at something. A formula gives exact answers. A graph shows the big picture: jumps, holes and asymptotes. A table gives evidence close to the point. Words tell you what the numbers mean. [pause]
> A strong answer uses the view that fits the question, and checks it against another. [long pause]

## Two functions, two views
**On screen:** On the left, the graph of g, which approaches height 3 from above as x approaches 2. On the right, a formula for f: a piecewise function that equals x plus 1 for x less than 3, and 10 minus x for x at least 3.
> Here's the kind of problem that mixes views. The graph shows g. The formula defines f. What is the limit as x approaches 2 of f of g of x? [long pause]

## Inside out, with direction
**On screen:** As x slides toward 2, a marker on the graph of g shows the outputs 3.25, 3.09, 3.01, all above 3. These values are fed into f, landing on the right-hand piece, 10 minus u, and the results approach 7.
> Work from the inside out. As x approaches 2, g of x approaches 3. [pause]
> But look closely at the graph: g approaches 3 from above. The inputs to f are always a little more than 3. [pause]
> So we need the right-hand limit of f at 3, which uses the piece ten minus u. That heads to seven. [pause]
> The left-hand piece of f would have given four, but g never feeds f any value below three. [long pause]

## Why the direction matters
**On screen:** A split screen: if g approached 3 from below, the left piece u plus 1 would be used, giving 4. The two answers, 7 and 4, sit side by side.
> This is the trap. [pause] The limit of the inside function isn't enough. You also need to know which side it approaches from, whenever the outside function behaves differently on each side. [long pause]

## Checking with a table
**On screen:** A small table of x near 2, g of x, and f of g of x. The last column reads 6.75, 6.91, 6.99, heading to 7.
> A quick table confirms it. The composite's values close in on seven.

## Table meets formula
**On screen:** The table of k values approaching 2 from both sides, then the limit laws giving 3(2) − 1 = 5.
> Values of k near x equals one are in the table. Find the limit of three k of x minus x squared, as x approaches one. [try it]
> From both sides, the table's outputs close in on two. So the limit of k is two. [pause]
> The limit laws do the rest: three times two, minus one squared. Five. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The four panels return, now connected by arrows, with 7 at the center.
> Read each representation for what it's good at, work from the inside out, and keep track of the direction. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A graph and a table together
**On screen:** Left: a graph of f passing through an open circle at (1, 2). Right: a table of g with x = 0.9, 0.99, 1.01, 1.1 and g(x) = −2.8, −2.98, −3.02, −3.2. The question: "Find the limit as x → 1 of [f(x) + g(x)] and of f(x)·g(x)."
> Here f comes as a graph and g as a table. Find the limit at one of f plus g, and of f times g. [pause]
> From the graph, f heads to two as x approaches one. The open circle doesn't matter.
> From the table, g heads to negative three from both sides. [pause]
> Both limits exist, so the limit laws apply. The sum heads to two plus negative three, which is negative one.
> The product heads to two times negative three, negative six. [pause]

## Example 2: A composite with no direction trap
**On screen:** "g(x) = x² − 1. The graph of f is shown, unbroken near u = 3, with f(3) = 5. Find the limit as x → 2 of f(g(x))." An arrow diagram: x → 2 feeds g(x) → 3, which feeds f(u) → 5.
> Now g is a formula, x squared minus one, and f is a graph. Find the limit as x approaches two of f of g of x. [pause]
> Inside out. As x approaches two, g of x approaches four minus one, which is three.
> Does direction matter? Look at the graph of f near three: no break, no jump. It heads to five from either side. [pause]
> So whichever side g comes from, f of g of x heads to five.

## Example 3: From words to a limit
**On screen:** "As the number of items n approaches 500, the cost per item C(n) approaches 12 dollars." The sentence becomes the limit as n → 500 of C(n) = 12. Then: "Find the limit as n → 500 of [3C(n) + 2]": 3·12 + 2 = 38.
> As production approaches five hundred items, the cost per item approaches twelve dollars. Write that as a limit, and find the limit of three C of n plus two. [pause]
> In symbols: the limit as n approaches five hundred of C of n equals twelve.
> Now for three C of n plus two. The laws let us work with the limit directly: three times twelve, plus two. [pause]
> Thirty-eight dollars. [long pause]
