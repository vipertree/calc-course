# 2.1 Defining Average and Instantaneous Rates of Change

## Zeno's arrow, again
**On screen:** Zeno's arrow from Topic 1.1, frozen in midair inside its dashed outline. Its formula from 1.1 appears on the right, s(t) = 60t − 12t², and the table of averages from 1.1 slides in under it: 24, 30, 34.8, 35.88, 35.988, with the "36?" and its question mark.
> At the start of the year, we left a question open. [pause]
> Zeno's arrow, at s of t equals sixty t minus twelve t squared. Its average velocities closed in on thirty-six meters per second, but every one of them was an average over an interval.
> We couldn't say whether thirty-six belonged to the instant itself. [pause]
> Now we have limits. Today we use them to answer Zeno. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.1 · Average and instantaneous rates of change." Unit 2 · Differentiation.

## Amara's drive
**On screen:** A straight road with a small red car (a painted side-view picture) and a house at the left end. The car drives with the graph, turning around while she heads back. Beside it, axes with time t in hours across and distance in miles up. The graph of s(t) = t³ − 9t² + 24t draws in from t = 0 to t = 4.5.
> Amara drives along a straight road.
> Her distance from home, in miles, t hours after she leaves, is s of t. [pause]
> Here is its graph. Up means farther from home. Down means she's heading back.

## The question
**On screen:** A dot appears on the curve at t = 1, height 16. A speedometer icon with a question mark on its needle.
> At t equals one, she is sixteen miles from home.
> Her speedometer could tell us how fast she's going right then. [pause]
> We don't have the speedometer. We only have the graph. So we have to work it out.

## Two moments
**On screen:** A second dot appears farther along the curve. Under the t-axis, a bracket runs from t = 1 to the second dot, labeled h.
> A single point can't tell us how fast she's going. That takes two moments: where she is, and where she is a little later. [pause]
> So pick a second time. We'll call the gap between the two times h.

## What one plus h means
**On screen:** On the t-axis: a tick at 1, and a second tick labeled "1 + h." A slider for h under the graph. As it moves, the second tick and second dot move together. Readouts: "h = 3 → second time = 4," then "h = 0.5 → second time = 1.5."
> If the first time is one, the second time is one plus h. [pause]
> h is just the length of the time interval.
> If h is three, the second time is four hours.
> If h is one half, the second time is one and a half hours. [pause]
> Changing h slides the second moment along the curve. Making h smaller brings the second moment closer to the first.

## Average velocity
**On screen:** h set to 3. The two dots at (1, 16) and (4, 16). A fraction builds: s(1 + h) minus s(1), over h. Beneath it, s(4) minus s(1), over 3. Then a small units card: "output units per input unit: miles per hour, liters per minute, dollars per shirt."
> Now we can compute her average velocity between the two moments.
> In those h hours, her position changed by s of one plus h, minus s of one. [pause]
> Change in position, divided by change in time. That's her average velocity. [pause]
> Its units are output units per input unit: miles per hour here. Liters of water over minutes would give liters per minute, and a cost in dollars for a number of shirts gives dollars per shirt. [pause]

## An average hides things
**On screen:** With h = 3: (16 − 16) / 3 = 0. The car replays those three hours: out to 20 miles at t = 2, then back to 16 at t = 4.
> With h equal to three, that's sixteen minus sixteen, over three. Zero. [pause]
> She was sixteen miles out at hour one, and sixteen miles out at hour four.
> In between she drove farther, turned around, and came back. Her average velocity for those three hours is zero, even though she was moving the whole time. [long pause]
> Let's clarify the difference between velocity and speed. In everyday talk, we use them as roughly the same word. But velocity includes direction, and speed doesn't care about direction. [pause]
> Here she goes out and comes back to the same spot, so her average velocity is zero, the same as if she never left. Her speed was never zero, though. She was moving the whole time. We'll sharpen this later in this unit. [long pause]

## Secant line
**On screen:** A yellow line through the two dots. A dashed rise-over-run triangle: run h, rise s(1 + h) − s(1).
> On the graph, that same ratio is rise over run.
> The run is h. The rise is the change in position. [pause]
> So the average velocity is the slope of the line through the two points. That line is called a secant line.

## Shrinking h
**On screen:** The slider moves h to 2, then 1, then 0.5. The second dot slides toward the first; the secant pivots. A table fills in: h = 2, slope 1; h = 1, slope 4; h = 0.5, slope 6.25.
> To learn about the single moment t equals one, we shrink the interval. [pause]
> At h equals two, the average velocity is one mile per hour.
> At h equals one, it's four. [pause]
> At one half, about six and a quarter.
> Each time, the second moment is closer to the first, and the secant line tilts a little more.

## Settling on nine
**On screen:** More rows: h = 0.1, slope 8.41; h = 0.01, slope 8.9401. The digits after the 8 pulse. A target "9?" appears to the right.
> At one tenth of an hour, eight point four one. [pause]
> At one hundredth, eight point nine four. [pause]
> The slopes are settling down. They're closing in on nine. [long pause]

## Zooming in
**On screen:** The camera zooms toward the point (1, 16). The curve straightens out, and the secant line and the curve become hard to tell apart.
> Zoom in near the point, and the curve looks almost straight.
> Up close, a short secant line runs right along the curve. [pause]

## Why h can't be zero
**On screen:** The slider snaps to h = 0. The two dots merge. The fraction shows (16 − 16) / 0, becomes 0/0, and turns red.
> So why not set h equal to zero? [pause]
> Then the two moments are the same moment. The change in position is zero, the change in time is zero, [pause] and zero divided by zero has no value.

## The limit
**On screen:** Limit notation writes itself around the fraction: the limit as h approaches 0 of [s(1 + h) − s(1)] / h. Then "= s′(1) = 9."
> Instead, we ask what value the slopes approach as h gets closer and closer to zero, without ever reaching it.
> That's a limit, and we write it like this. [pause]
> This limit has a name: the derivative of s at one, written s prime of one. [pause]
> For Amara it's nine miles per hour. That's her velocity at the instant t equals one.

## The tangent line
**On screen:** Zoom back out. The secant lines from the table replay in sequence and settle onto one red line through (1, 16). Its slope is labeled 9.
> On the graph, the secant lines settle onto one line. It touches the curve at this point and runs in the same direction as the curve.
> That's the tangent line, and its slope is nine. [pause]

## Any function
**On screen:** The graph generalizes: s becomes f, 1 becomes a. The definition in a box: f′(a) = the limit as h approaches 0 of [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h.
> The same steps work for any function f at any input a.
> Take the average rate of change from a to a plus h. Then let h go to zero. [pause]

## Two meanings
**On screen:** The derivative row of the meaning panel: "Instantaneous rate: 9 mi/hr" fills the analytic cell; "tangent slope: 9" fills the graphical cell.
> So the derivative has two meanings.
> Analytically, it's the instantaneous rate of change. [pause]
> Graphically, it's the slope of the tangent line.
> They're the same number, seen two ways. [pause]

## The other form
**On screen:** Back to Amara's graph. The fixed point is relabeled (a, f(a)), and the second point's input is relabeled x instead of a + h. The bracket between them is labeled both "h" and "x − a." As the second point slides toward the first, a readout shows "h → 0" and "x → a" changing together. Then the second form of the definition: f′(a) = the limit as x approaches a of [f(x) − f(a)] / (x − a).
> There's a second way to write the same limit. Go back to the two points on the graph. [pause]
> Call the second input x instead of a plus h. Then the gap between the inputs, h, is x minus a. [pause]
> Shrinking h to zero slides the second point toward the first, which is exactly the same as bringing x toward a. [pause]
> So the derivative can also be written as the limit, as x approaches a, of f of x minus f of a, over x minus a. Same secant slopes, same limit, just different names. [long pause]

## Zeno's arrow, answered
**On screen:** The arrow returns with s(t) = 60t − 12t². "In Topic 1.1 the arrow's average velocities near t = 1 closed in on 36 m/s. Use the definition to find s′(1)." Each line keeps the limit, and the canceled terms are struck out as they cancel.
> Back to Zeno. The arrow's position is sixty t minus twelve t squared. Its average velocities near t equals one closed in on thirty-six. Use the definition to find s prime of one. [pause]
> s of one is sixty minus twelve, which is forty-eight. So the numerator is sixty times one plus h, minus twelve times one plus h squared, minus forty-eight.
> Expand: sixty plus sixty h, minus twelve, minus twenty-four h, minus twelve h squared, minus forty-eight. [pause]
> The constants cancel: sixty minus twelve minus forty-eight is zero. That leaves thirty-six h minus twelve h squared.
> Factor an h out of the top: h times thirty-six minus twelve h. Now the h on top cancels the h on the bottom. That's allowed because h is never zero inside the limit. [pause]
> What's left is the limit of thirty-six minus twelve h, which is thirty-six. [pause]
> Zeno said motion at an instant is impossible. The derivative gives us a way to talk about it anyway: the velocity at an instant is the limit of the average velocities around it. [pause]
> The arrow is moving at thirty-six meters per second at t equals one. [long pause]

## The h form
**On screen:** "Let f(x) = x². Use the definition to find f′(3)." Each line keeps the limit; the 9s cancel, then the factored h cancels.
> Let f of x be x squared. Use the definition to find f prime of three. [try it]
> f of three plus h is three plus h, squared, and f of three is nine.
> Expand: nine plus six h plus h squared, minus nine. The nines cancel. [pause]
> Factor an h out of what's left: h times six plus h. Cancel the h's.
> The limit of six plus h, as h approaches zero, is six. [long pause]

## The x to a form
**On screen:** "Let g(x) = 1/x. Use the x → a form of the definition to find g′(2)." Each line keeps the limit. The fractions combine, x − 2 is rewritten as −(2 − x), and the matching factors are struck out.
> Let g of x be one over x. Use the x approaches a form to find g prime of two. [pause]
> g of x minus g of two is one over x minus one half. Over a common denominator, that's two minus x, over two x.
> So the whole quotient is two minus x over two x, all divided by x minus two. [pause]
> Two minus x is the negative of x minus two. Write it that way, and the x minus two cancels. That's allowed because x isn't two inside the limit.
> What's left is negative one over two x. As x approaches two, that's negative one fourth. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An average rate of change
**On screen:** "Find the average rate of change of f(x) = √x on [1, 9]." Work appears line by line: f(9) = 3, f(1) = 1, (3 − 1)/(9 − 1) = 2/8 = 1/4. The graph of √x with the secant line through (1, 1) and (9, 3).
> Find the average rate of change of the square root of x on the interval from one to nine. [pause]
> The square root of nine is three, and the square root of one is one.
> Three minus one, over nine minus one. That's two over eight, which is one fourth. [pause]
> On the graph, one fourth is the slope of the secant line through those points.

## Example 2: A derivative from the definition
**On screen:** "Use the definition to find f′(2) for f(x) = x² + 3x." Each line keeps the limit. The constants 4, 6 and 10 are struck out as they cancel; then h is factored out of the top and the two h's are struck out.
> Use the definition to find f prime of two, for f of x equals x squared plus three x. [try it]
> f of two is four plus six, which is ten. So the numerator is two plus h, squared, plus three times two plus h, minus ten.
> Expand: four plus four h plus h squared, plus six plus three h, minus ten. [pause]
> The constants cancel: four plus six minus ten is zero. That leaves seven h plus h squared.
> Factor an h out of the top: h times seven plus h. Cancel it with the h on the bottom, since h isn't zero inside the limit. [pause]
> As h approaches zero, seven plus h approaches seven. So f prime of two is seven.

## Example 3: Reading a limit as a derivative
**On screen:** "The limit as h → 0 of [(3 + h)³ − 27] / h is f′(a) for some f and a. Find f, a, and the value." The pieces of the definition highlight in matching colors: f(a + h) matches (3 + h)³, f(a) matches 27.
> Soon we'll have rules that find derivatives in a line or two. Once we do, a limit like this on a test is really a derivative in disguise, and the fast way is to recognize it. This limit is the derivative of some function at some point. Which one, and what's its value? [try it]
> Match it to the definition. f of a plus h is three plus h, cubed. So f is x cubed, and a is three. And f of three is twenty-seven, which checks. [pause]
> For now, find the value with algebra. Three plus h, cubed, is twenty-seven plus twenty-seven h plus nine h squared plus h cubed. The twenty-sevens cancel.
> Factor an h out of the top and cancel it: twenty-seven plus nine h plus h squared. [pause]
> As h approaches zero, that's twenty-seven. [long pause]
