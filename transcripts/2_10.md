# 2.10 Finding the Derivatives of Tangent, Cotangent, Secant, and/or Cosecant Functions

## Four functions left
**On screen:** Six trig function names in a grid. Sine and cosine are already checked off with their derivatives from Topic 2.7. Tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant sit empty.
> We know how sine and cosine change. [pause]
> The other four trig functions are all built from those two. So we already have everything we need to find their derivatives. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.10 · Derivatives of tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant."

## A quick review
**On screen:** A unit circle with a point at angle θ: its x-coordinate is labeled cos θ and its y-coordinate sin θ. Then a list builds: tan θ = sin θ / cos θ, cot θ = cos θ / sin θ, sec θ = 1 / cos θ, csc θ = 1 / sin θ. Each reciprocal pair is joined by a bracket.
> First, a quick review. On the unit circle, the point at angle theta has x-coordinate cosine theta and y-coordinate sine theta. [pause]
> Tangent is sine over cosine, and cotangent is the flip: cosine over sine. [pause]
> Secant is one over cosine, and cosecant is one over sine. A handy check: each "co" function on the right goes with the one that has "co" in it, so secant goes with cosine, not cosecant. [pause]
> Every one of these is built from sine and cosine, and that's how we'll differentiate them. [long pause]

## Tangent by the quotient rule
**On screen:** tan x = sin x / cos x. The quotient rule fills in: low d-high, cos x times cos x; minus high d-low, sin x times negative sin x; over cos² x. The numerator becomes cos² x + sin² x, which turns into 1. The result 1/cos² x becomes sec² x.
> Tangent is sine over cosine, so use the quotient rule. [pause]
> Low d-high: cosine times cosine. Minus high d-low: sine times negative sine.
> That's cosine squared plus sine squared, all over cosine squared. [pause]
> And cosine squared plus sine squared is one. So the derivative of tangent is one over cosine squared, which is secant squared. [long pause]

## A picture of the nudge
**On screen:** A unit circle with a ray at angle θ. The ray hits the vertical line x = 1 at a point P, at height tan θ. The segment from the origin to P is labeled sec θ. The angle grows by a tiny dθ; the ray swings, and P slides up the line to a new point Q.
> A picture shows where the secant squared comes from. [pause]
> Draw the vertical line x equals one. The ray at angle theta hits it at height tangent theta. And the distance from the origin to that point is secant theta.
> Now nudge the angle by a tiny amount, d theta. The point slides up the line. [pause] How far?

## Two stretches
**On screen:** A zoomed view of P and Q. First, a small arc of radius sec θ swept by dθ, labeled sec θ · dθ, perpendicular to the ray. Then the vertical segment from P to Q, making angle θ with that arc. The tiny triangle shows the vertical segment is the arc divided by cos θ: sec θ · sec θ · dθ.
> Two things stretch the step. [pause]
> First, the point is secant theta away from the center. A point that far out sweeps secant theta times d theta when the angle turns.
> Second, that sweep is perpendicular to the ray, but the line it has to follow is vertical. They meet at angle theta, so the vertical step is longer by another factor of one over cosine theta, another secant. [pause]
> Altogether, the height changes by about secant squared times d theta.
> Divide by d theta and let the nudge shrink to zero, and the "about" becomes exact. The derivative of tangent is secant squared. [long pause]

## Secant
**On screen:** sec x = 1 / cos x. The quotient rule: (cos x · 0 − 1 · (−sin x)) / cos² x = sin x / cos² x, which splits into (1/cos x)(sin x/cos x) = sec x tan x.
> Secant is one over cosine. The quotient rule gives sine over cosine squared. [pause]
> Split that into one over cosine, times sine over cosine. That's secant times tangent. [pause]

## The co-functions
**On screen:** The six derivatives arranged in two rows: sin → cos, tan → sec², sec → sec tan on top; cos → −sin, cot → −csc², csc → −csc cot below. The "co" in each bottom-row name and each minus sign glow together.
> Cotangent and cosecant come out the same way. [pause]
> Notice the pattern. Each "co" function has a derivative that mirrors its partner, with a minus sign. [pause]
> Cosine gives negative sine. Cotangent gives negative cosecant squared. Cosecant gives negative cosecant cotangent. [long pause]

## A product with tangent
**On screen:** "Find d/dx [x² tan x]." x² is underlined and labeled u, tan x is labeled v. u′ = 2x, v′ = sec² x. u′v + uv′ = 2x tan x + x² sec² x.
> These combine with every rule we already have. Find the derivative of x squared times tangent x. [try it]
> u is x squared and v is tangent x. u prime is two x, and v prime is secant squared x. [pause]
> u prime v plus u v prime: two x tangent x, plus x squared secant squared x. [long pause]

## A tangent line
**On screen:** "Find the equation for the line tangent to y = tan x at x = π/4." The graph of tan x. Point (π/4, 1); slope sec²(π/4) = (√2)² = 2; y − 1 = 2(x − π/4).
> Find the equation for the line tangent to tangent x at pi over four. [try it]
> The point: tangent of pi over four is one. So pi over four, one.
> The slope: secant squared of pi over four. Secant of pi over four is root two, and squared, that's two. [pause]
> So the line is y minus one equals two times x minus pi over four. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The six-derivative table, with the note "rewrite with sine and cosine."
> Six trig derivatives. If you forget one, rewrite with sine and cosine and let the quotient rule rebuild it. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Sums and multiples
**On screen:** "Find dy/dx for y = 3 tan x − 2 sec x + x²." Term by term: 3 sec² x, −2 sec x tan x, 2x.
> Find d y d x for three tangent x, minus two secant x, plus x squared. [pause]
> Go term by term. Three tangent x gives three secant squared x.
> Negative two secant x gives negative two secant x tangent x. [pause]
> And x squared gives two x.
> Altogether: three secant squared x, minus two secant x tangent x, plus two x.

## Example 2: A product with a trig factor
**On screen:** "Find the derivative of x² cot x." Product rule: 2x cot x + x²(−csc² x) = 2x cot x − x² csc² x.
> Find the derivative of x squared times cotangent x. [pause]
> Product rule. u is x squared and v is cotangent x, so u prime is two x and v prime is negative cosecant squared x. u prime v is two x cotangent x.
> Plus u v prime: x squared times negative cosecant squared x. [pause]
> Two x cotangent x, minus x squared cosecant squared x.

## Example 3: Where is the slope a given value?
**On screen:** "For 0 ≤ x < π/2, where does y = 2x − tan x have a horizontal tangent?" y′ = 2 − sec² x = 0, so sec² x = 2, cos x = 1/√2, x = π/4. The graph shows its peak at x = π/4.
> Where does two x minus tangent x have a horizontal tangent line, between zero and pi over two? [pause]
> Rephrase the question first. A horizontal line has slope zero, so we're asking: when is this function's derivative zero? [pause]
> The derivative is two minus secant squared x. Set it to zero: secant squared x equals two.
> So cosine squared x is one half, and cosine x is one over root two. [pause]
> In this interval, that's x equals pi over four. On the graph, that's the top of the hump. [long pause]
