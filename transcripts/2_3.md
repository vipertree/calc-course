# 2.3 Estimating Derivatives of a Function at a Point

## When there's no formula
**On screen:** A weather station's temperature readings scroll by as a table: time in hours, temperature in degrees. No formula anywhere.
> Real data rarely comes with a formula. A weather station gives you temperatures every few hours, and that's all. [pause]
> You can still estimate how fast the temperature is changing at a particular moment. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.3 · Estimating derivatives of a function at a point."

## Using the closest data
**On screen:** A table: t equals 0, 2, 5, 7, 10; T equals 48, 55, 63, 66, 64. The target t equals 6 is marked between 5 and 7. The two nearest columns light up, and the average rate of change builds: (66 minus 63) over (7 minus 5) equals 1.5. A label: "estimate of T′(6)."
> To estimate the rate at t equals six, use the data closest to six on each side: t equals five and t equals seven. [pause]
> The temperature rose from 63 to 66 degrees over those two hours. That's 1.5 degrees per hour. [pause]
> That average rate of change is our estimate of the instantaneous rate. T prime of six is approximately 1.5 degrees per hour. [long pause]

## Why the closest points
**On screen:** The same table with a wider interval, 2 to 10, lighting up instead. Its slope, 9 over 8, is drawn as a secant on a sketch of the data, and it misses the local behavior near t equals 6.
> A wider interval averages over more of the day, including the late cooling. The closest data gives the best local picture. [pause]
> This is still an average rate. We're using a secant slope to estimate a tangent slope. [long pause]

## Pick nearby points
**On screen:** A curve with a point at x equals a and its tangent line. A secant through two far-away points (label "far away") misses the tangent badly. Then a secant through two close points on one side (label "close, one side"), and one through close points straddling a (label "close, both sides"). Both close secants nearly match the tangent.
> Which data points should you use? Pick the ones closest to the point. [pause]
> They can sit on one side, or straddle the point, one on each side. What matters is that they're close. Points far away give a secant that can miss the tangent badly. [long pause]

## Only one side
**On screen:** The temperature table. "Using the table, estimate T′(10)." The last two columns light up: (64 − 66)/(10 − 7) = −2/3 degrees per hour.
> Using the table, estimate T prime of ten. [try it]
> There's no data after ten, so use the closest data on the one side we have: seven and ten.
> Sixty-four minus sixty-six, over ten minus seven: negative two thirds of a degree per hour. [pause]
> The temperature is dropping at about two thirds of a degree per hour. [long pause]

## Reading a graph
**On screen:** A graph of a smooth curve with a tangent line drawn at x equals 3. Two grid points on the tangent line, (1, 0) and (5, 4), are marked, and a rise-over-run triangle shows a rise of 4 over a run of 4.
> From a graph, sketch the tangent line at the point, and find two points on that line where it's easy to read the grid. [pause]
> Rise over run on the tangent line estimates the derivative. Here it's four over four, so the slope is about one. [long pause]

## Calculators
**On screen:** Desmos shows the function x cubed and the derivative expression evaluated at 1.5, returning 6.75.
> A graphing calculator estimates derivatives numerically. In Desmos, you can type the derivative of the function and evaluate it at a point. [long pause]

## A calculator trap
**On screen:** The graph of the absolute value of x, with a sharp corner at the origin. An average rate of change using points on both sides of 0 gives 0, and the calculator reports 0. A warning label: "no tangent line here."
> But calculators can be fooled. For the absolute value of x at zero, an average rate of change using points on both sides gives zero, and some calculators report a derivative of zero. [pause]
> The graph has a sharp corner there, and there is no tangent line. The next lesson explains why. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The temperature table again, with the estimate 1.5 degrees per hour boxed and labeled with units.
> No formula needed. Choose the closest data around the point, compute the slope, and include units. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A rate from a table, with units
**On screen:** A table for a bacteria culture: t (hours) = 0, 3, 5, 8, 12; B(t) = 200, 260, 310, 400, 520. The question: "Estimate B′(4). Explain its meaning." The columns t = 3 and t = 5 light up.
> A culture of bacteria is counted every few hours. Estimate B prime of four, and say what it means. [pause]
> Four isn't in the table. The closest times on either side are three and five.
> Three hundred ten minus two hundred sixty is fifty. Divide by the two hours between them: twenty-five. [pause]
> So B prime of four is about twenty-five bacteria per hour.
> In words: four hours in, the population is growing at about twenty-five bacteria per hour.

## Example 2: A slope from a tangent line on a graph
**On screen:** A curve with a tangent line drawn at x = 1. The tangent passes through the grid points (−2, 5) and (4, 2). A rise-over-run triangle shows a rise of −3 over a run of 6.
> Here the tangent line at x equals one has been drawn for us. Estimate f prime of one. [pause]
> Find two grid points on the tangent line: negative two, five, and four, two.
> The rise is two minus five, negative three. The run is four minus negative two, six. [pause]
> Negative three over six is negative one half. The graph is falling at the rate of one half unit per unit.

## Example 3: Points on both sides
**On screen:** "Estimate f′(4) for f(x) = √x using x = 3.99 and x = 4.01." The quotient: (√4.01 − √3.99)/0.02 ≈ 0.25000. Beside it, the exact value from the definition: 1/4.
> Estimate the derivative of the square root of x at four, using inputs just below and just above four. [pause]
> The square root of four point zero one, minus the square root of three point nine nine, over the distance between them, point zero two.
> A calculator gives about zero point two five zero zero zero. [pause]
> From the definition, the exact value is one fourth. A secant through points this close is an excellent estimate. [long pause]
