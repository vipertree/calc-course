# 2.4 Connecting Differentiability and Continuity

## Guess the function
**On screen:** A framed graph with axes and evenly spaced tick marks, but no numbers on them. A curve that looks perfectly straight passes just above the origin, rising two ticks for every one across. A guess appears: "y = 2x + 1?"
> Here's a graph, zoomed in on one spot. The tick marks are evenly spaced, but there are no numbers yet. Can you guess the function? [try it]
> It looks like a straight line. It rises two ticks for every tick across, and it crosses the vertical axis one tick up. Maybe y equals two x plus one? [pause]

## Reveal the scale
**On screen:** Numbers appear on the ticks: 0.001, 0.002, 0.003. The guess changes to "y = 2x + 0.001?"
> Now the numbers. Each tick is one thousandth. [pause]
> So the line crosses at one thousandth, not one. Maybe it's y equals two x plus point zero zero one? [pause]

## Zoom out
**On screen:** The view zooms out slowly, with the tick labels updating: hundredths, tenths, ones. The "line" bends into a wave. The guess is replaced by y = sin(2x) + 0.001.
> Let's back up and see more. [pause] The straight line starts to bend, and it turns out to be a wave.
> The function is sine of two x, plus point zero zero one. [pause]
> Up close, near the origin, it looked exactly like a line with slope two. That line is its tangent line. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.4 · Connecting differentiability and continuity: determining when derivatives do and do not exist."

## Local linearity
**On screen:** The close-up view of the wave near the origin, with the phrase "locally linear at x = 0," then "differentiable at x = 0."
> A function that looks like a line when you zoom in far enough is called locally linear at that point. It has a tangent line, and the derivative exists there. [pause]
> We say the function is differentiable at that point. [long pause]

## A corner doesn't straighten
**On screen:** The graph of the absolute value of x, with numbered tick marks. The view zooms in on the origin, and the tick labels shrink from ones to tenths to hundredths to thousandths. The V shape never changes. Two slopes are labeled: negative 1 on the left and 1 on the right. Below: the limit as h → 0 of |h|/h, which is −1 from the left and 1 from the right.
> Now zoom in on the absolute value of x at zero. Watch the tick labels: ones, tenths, hundredths, thousandths. It's still a V. [pause]
> The left side has slope negative one, and the right side has slope one. No single line fits, so the derivative at zero does not exist. [pause]
> The picture is a way of looking at the limit definition. At zero, the definition's limit is the absolute value of h over h. From the left that's negative one, from the right it's one, so the limit doesn't exist. [long pause]

## Other ways to fail
**On screen:** Three more graphs, each zoomed at the trouble point: a cusp (the cube root of x squared), a vertical tangent (the cube root of x), and a jump.
> Corners aren't the only problem. A cusp is a corner so sharp that the slopes run off to infinity on both sides. [pause]
> A vertical tangent line has infinite slope, and infinity isn't a number, so there's no derivative. [pause]
> In each case, the real question is the same: does the limit in the definition of the derivative exist? At a cusp or a vertical tangent, the quotients blow up, so it doesn't.
> And at a discontinuity, like a jump, there's no tangent line at all. [long pause]

## The one-way street
**On screen:** Two boxes connected by an arrow: "differentiable at c" points to "continuous at c". A second arrow going back is crossed out, with the absolute value graph shown as the counterexample.
> Here's the key relationship. If a function is differentiable at a point, it must be continuous there. [pause]
> But continuity is not enough to guarantee a derivative. The absolute value of x is continuous at zero, and it still isn't differentiable there. [pause]
> So the arrow only goes one way. Differentiable means continuous. Continuous does not mean differentiable. [long pause]

## Check value and slope
**On screen:** "Is f differentiable at x = 1? f(x) = x² for x ≤ 1, and 2x − 1 for x > 1." The graph with both pieces. Values: 1² = 1 and the right piece approaches 1, a check. Slopes: 2x gives 2 at x = 1, and the line has slope 2, a check. The tangent line slides through the seam smoothly.
> Is this piecewise function differentiable at one? [try it]
> For a piecewise function, check two things at the seam. First, do the pieces meet? Both give one at x equals one, so the function is continuous there.
> Second, do the slopes match? The left piece has slope two x, which is two at one, and the right piece is a line with slope two. [pause]
> Both checks pass, so f is differentiable at one, and f prime of one is two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The close-up of the wave from the opening and the stubborn V side by side, both with numbered ticks.
> A derivative exists where the graph zooms in to a single, non-vertical line. Corners, cusps, vertical tangents and breaks all fail that test. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A smooth seam
**On screen:** "Is f differentiable at x = 2? f(x) = x² for x < 2, and 4x − 4 for x ≥ 2." Check 1, values: 4 and 4. Check 2, slopes: 2x gives 4, and the line has slope 4. Both checks turn green.
> Is this function differentiable at two? It's x squared on the left and four x minus four from two on. [pause]
> First, continuity. The left piece heads to four. The right piece gives eight minus four, also four. They meet.
> Second, slopes. The slope of x squared is two x, which is four at two. The line has slope four. [pause]
> They match, so f is differentiable at two, and f prime of two is four.

## Example 2: A corner at the seam
**On screen:** "Is g differentiable at x = 1? g(x) = x² + 1 for x ≤ 1, and 3x − 1 for x > 1." Values: 2 and 2, a green check. Slopes: 2 and 3, a red X. A zoom on the seam shows a slight corner.
> Now g: x squared plus one up to one, and three x minus one after. [pause]
> Values: one plus one is two, and three minus one is two. The pieces meet, so g is continuous at one.
> Slopes: two x gives two on the left. The line has slope three on the right. [pause]
> The slopes disagree, so there's a corner. g is continuous at one, but not differentiable there.

## Example 3: Making it smooth
**On screen:** "Find a and b so that h(x) = ax² for x ≤ 1, and 4x + b for x > 1, is differentiable at x = 1." Slopes: 2a = 4, so a = 2. Values: a = 4 + b, so b = −2.
> Find a and b so that h is differentiable at one. [pause]
> Differentiable needs two things, so we get two equations.
> Slopes: the slope of a x squared is two a x, which is two a at one. The line's slope is four. So two a equals four, and a is two. [pause]
> Values: the left piece gives a, which is two. The right piece gives four plus b. So b is negative two. [long pause]
