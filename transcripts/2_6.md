# 2.6 Derivative Rules: Constant, Sum, Difference, and Constant Multiple

## Lifting a graph
**On screen:** The graph of y equals x squared with a tangent line at x equals 1. The whole graph slides straight up by 3 units. The tangent line slides with it, keeping the same tilt.
> Here's x squared with its tangent line at x equals one. Now add three to the function. The whole graph lifts straight up. [pause]
> The tangent line lifts with it, but its tilt doesn't change. Adding a constant changes heights, not slopes. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.6 · Derivative rules: constant, sum, difference, and constant multiple."

## The constant rule
**On screen:** A horizontal line, y equals 5. Its tangent line is itself, with slope 0. The formula: the derivative of a constant is 0.
> A constant function is a horizontal line. It never changes, so its rate of change is zero, and its slope is zero. [pause]
> The derivative of any constant is zero. [long pause]

## Stretching a graph
**On screen:** The graph of x squared is stretched vertically by a factor of 3. At x equals 1, the rise-over-run triangle's rise triples while the run stays the same. Slope 2 becomes slope 6.
> Now multiply the function by three. Every height triples, so every rise triples, while the runs stay the same. [pause]
> So every rise over run triples, and so does its limit. [pause] The derivative of a constant times a function is that constant times the derivative. [long pause]

## Adding functions
**On screen:** Two graphs, f and g, near a point. Their sum is drawn by stacking heights. At the point, over a tiny nudge dx, the rise of the sum is the rise of f plus the rise of g: d(f + g) = df + dg. Dividing by dx gives d(f + g)/dx = df/dx + dg/dx.
> Add two functions, and their changes add. Nudge x by a tiny d x, and the sum rises by f's rise, d f, plus g's rise, d g.
> Divide by d x: the rate of the sum is f's rate plus g's. [pause] Let the nudge shrink to zero, and those rates become derivatives.
> So the derivative of the sum is the sum of the derivatives. The same goes for a difference. [long pause]

## A polynomial
**On screen:** "Find f′(x) for f(x) = 4x³ − 5x² + 7x − 2." The function is written with its name, f(x) = ..., and each term gets an arrow to its derivative: 12x², −10x, 7, 0. The answer is written with its name too: f′(x) = 12x² − 10x + 7.
> Together with the power rule, these rules let you differentiate any polynomial term by term. Find f prime of x for f of x equals four x cubed minus five x squared plus seven x minus two. [try it]
> Always write which function you're working with. f of x is the function; f prime of x is its derivative.
> Four x cubed: the four comes along, and the power rule gives three x squared. Twelve x squared. [pause]
> Negative five x squared gives negative ten x. Seven x gives seven, and the constant two disappears.
> So f prime of x equals twelve x squared minus ten x plus seven. [long pause]

## Rewrite, then differentiate
**On screen:** "Find dy/dx for y = (x³ − 2x + 5)/x." The fraction splits: x³/x − 2x/x + 5/x = x² − 2 + 5x⁻¹. Then dy/dx = 2x − 5x⁻². A warning card: "a/(b + c) ≠ a/b + a/c," with a red X, next to a correct split with a single-term denominator.
> Find d y d x for y equals x cubed minus two x plus five, all over x. [try it]
> The denominator is a single term, x. So we can split the fraction: x cubed over x, minus two x over x, plus five over x.
> That's x squared minus two plus five x to the negative one. Now the power rule term by term: d y d x is two x minus five x to the negative two. [pause]
> Be careful: you can split a fraction like this only when the denominator is a single term. Five over x plus two is not five over x plus five over two. [pause]
> When the denominator has several terms, you'll need the quotient rule, coming in two lessons. [long pause]

## Horizontal tangents
**On screen:** "Where does g(x) = x³ − 12x + 1 have horizontal tangent lines?" A question first: "What is the slope of a horizontal line?" Then "0," and the question rewritten: "When is g′(x) = 0?" Then g′(x) = 3x² − 12 = 0, x = ±2, with flat tangent segments drawn on the graph at x = −2 and x = 2.
> Where does g of x equals x cubed minus twelve x plus one have horizontal tangent lines? [pause]
> Before any algebra, think about the question. What's the slope of a horizontal line? [try it]
> Zero. So the question is really: when does the derivative equal zero? [pause]
> g prime of x is three x squared minus twelve. Set it equal to zero: x squared is four, so x is two or negative two.
> At those two inputs, the tangent lines are flat. [long pause]

## In context
**On screen:** "A company's cost to make n phone cases is C(n) = 500 + 20n − 0.01n² dollars. Find C′(100) and interpret it." C′(n) = 0 + 20 − 0.02n; C′(100) = 18. The 500 is dimmed: "fixed cost: no effect on the rate."
> A company's cost to make n phone cases is five hundred plus twenty n minus one hundredth n squared dollars. Find C prime of one hundred, and say what it means. [try it]
> The fixed five hundred is a constant, so its derivative is zero. C prime of n is twenty minus two hundredths n.
> At one hundred, that's twenty minus two: eighteen. [pause]
> When one hundred cases are being made, the cost is increasing at eighteen dollars per case. The fixed cost never affects the rate. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The four rules stacked on a single card.
> Constants vanish, multiples come along, and sums split apart. Those three facts, plus the power rule, handle every polynomial. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A polynomial, term by term
**On screen:** "Find f′(x) for f(x) = 6x⁵ − 4x³ + x − 9." f(x) is written out, each term gets its own arrow: 30x⁴, −12x², 1, 0. Result: f′(x) = 30x⁴ − 12x² + 1.
> Find f prime of x for six x to the fifth, minus four x cubed, plus x, minus nine. [pause]
> Go term by term. Six x to the fifth: the constant six comes along, and the power rule gives five x to the fourth. Thirty x to the fourth.
> Negative four x cubed gives negative twelve x squared. [pause]
> x gives one, and the constant nine disappears.
> f prime of x is thirty x to the fourth minus twelve x squared plus one.

## Example 2: Rewrite before differentiating
**On screen:** "Find dy/dx for y = (x² + 3)/√x." Split the fraction: x²/x^(1/2) + 3/x^(1/2) = x^(3/2) + 3x^(−1/2). Derivative: (3/2)x^(1/2) − (3/2)x^(−3/2).
> Find d y d x for x squared plus three, all over the square root of x. [pause]
> We don't have a rule for quotients yet, but we don't need one. The denominator is a single term, so we can split the fraction into two.
> x squared over x to the one half is x to the three halves. Three over x to the one half is three x to the negative one half. [pause]
> Now the power rule on each term: three halves x to the one half, minus three halves x to the negative three halves.

## Example 3: Horizontal tangents
**On screen:** "Where does y = x³ − 6x² + 9x + 1 have horizontal tangent lines?" y′ = 3x² − 12x + 9 = 3(x − 1)(x − 3) = 0, so x = 1 and x = 3. The graph shows flat tangent segments at (1, 5) and (3, 1).
> Where does x cubed minus six x squared plus nine x plus one have horizontal tangent lines? [pause]
> Rephrase the question first: a horizontal line has slope zero, so we're asking when the derivative equals zero.
> The derivative is three x squared minus twelve x plus nine. Factor out three: x squared minus four x plus three, which is x minus one times x minus three. [pause]
> The tangent is horizontal at x equals one and x equals three. On the graph, those are the top of the hump and the bottom of the dip. [long pause]
