# 2.7 Derivatives of cos x, sin x, e^x, and ln x

## Sliding along a sine wave
**On screen:** The graph of y equals sine x. A tangent line slides along the wave. Below, a second set of axes records its slope: 1 at x equals 0, 0 at the peak x equals pi over 2, negative 1 at pi. The recorded points trace out a cosine wave.
> Slide a tangent line along the sine wave and record its slope. [pause]
> At zero, the wave rises with slope one. At the peak, the slope is zero. At pi, the wave falls with slope negative one. [pause]
> The slopes trace out a familiar shape. It's the cosine wave. [long pause]

## A nudge around the circle
**On screen:** A unit circle with a point P at angle θ. Its height is sin θ and its horizontal position is cos θ. The angle grows by a tiny amount dθ, and P slides along the arc to a new point Q. The arc from P to Q is highlighted with length dθ. A small right triangle forms with the arc as its slanted side: its vertical leg is the change in sin θ, its horizontal leg the change in cos θ. The small triangle rotates into view next to the big triangle from the center to P; they're the same shape, turned a quarter turn.
> Here's a picture of why. Put a point on the unit circle at angle theta. Its height is sine theta. [pause]
> Nudge the angle by a tiny amount, d theta. On a unit circle, the point slides along an arc of length d theta.
> Zoom in on that little step. The arc is almost straight, and it makes a tiny right triangle. Its vertical leg is how much sine went up. [pause]
> The step runs along the circle, which means it's perpendicular to the radius. So the tiny triangle is the big triangle turned a quarter turn: same shape, much smaller.
> In the big triangle, the side next to the angle is cosine theta, and the long side is one. In the tiny triangle, the matching sides are the rise in sine, and the step, d theta. [pause]
> Same shape, same ratio: the rise in sine, over d theta, is about cosine theta over one. [long pause]

## From the picture to the limit
**On screen:** The ratio d(sin θ)/dθ ≈ cos θ, with the word "about" highlighted. As the nudge dθ shrinks, the arc and its straight chord become indistinguishable, and the ratio becomes exactly cos θ.
> Why only "about"? Because the arc isn't quite straight. [pause]
> But as the nudge d theta shrinks, the arc and its chord become indistinguishable. That's exactly the special limit from Topic one point eight: sine h over h goes to one.
> So in the limit, the ratio is exactly cosine theta. The derivative of sine is cosine. [pause]
> The same tiny triangle shows cosine too. Its horizontal leg points left, the direction cosine is shrinking, and it matches sine theta. So the derivative of cosine is negative sine. [long pause]

## Why sine's derivative is cosine
**On screen:** The difference quotient for sine: sine of x plus h, minus sine x, over h. The angle-addition formula expands sine of x plus h. The expression regroups into sine x times (cos h minus 1) over h, plus cosine x times sine h over h. The two special limits from Topic 1.8 slide in: (cos h minus 1) over h goes to 0, and sine h over h goes to 1.
> The picture gives the idea. The limit definition makes it airtight. [pause] Expand sine of x plus h with the angle-addition formula, and regroup.
> Two pieces appear that you've seen before, in Topic one point eight. Sine h over h goes to one. Cosine h minus one, over h, goes to zero. [pause]
> What's left is cosine x. The derivative of sine x is cosine x. [long pause]

## Cosine
**On screen:** The same sliding-tangent picture on the cosine wave. The slopes trace an upside-down sine wave, labeled negative sine x.
> Do the same with cosine, and the slopes trace out a sine wave flipped upside down. The derivative of cosine x is negative sine x. [pause]
> These rules use radians. The special limit sine h over h equals one is only true in radians. [long pause]

## Its own derivative
**On screen:** A question card: "Can a function be its own derivative?" Then "slope = height, everywhere," and a sketch of a rising curve with two tangent lines: one where the curve is low, labeled "small: nearly flat," one where it's high, labeled "big: steep."
> Here's a puzzle. Can you think of a function that is its own derivative? [try it]
> What would have to be true? Its slope would have to equal its height, everywhere. [pause]
> So as the function's value gets higher, its rate of change has to increase too. Where it's small, it's nearly flat. Where it's big, it's steep. [pause]
> That's the shape of exponential growth. Let's find the exact one. [long pause]

## A function that is its own slope
**On screen:** Three exponential curves, each labeled next to its curve: 2 to the x, 3 to the x, and e to the x. At x equals 0, tangent lines are drawn with slopes 0.693, 1.099, and exactly 1. The graph of e to the x has a tangent line at several points, each labeled "slope equals height."
> Remember the mysterious 0.693 from the first lesson of the course? It was the slope of two to the x at zero. Three to the x has slope 1.099 there. [pause]
> Somewhere between two and three is a base whose slope at zero is exactly one. That base is e, about 2.718. [pause]
> And e to the x has a remarkable property: at every point, its slope equals its height. It's its own derivative. [long pause]

## The natural log
**On screen:** The graph of natural log of x, the mirror image of e to the x across the line y equals x. Each curve is labeled beside it: "y = eˣ," "y = ln x," "y = x." At x equals 2 the tangent slope is one half; at x equals 4, one fourth. The slopes are labeled 1 over x.
> The natural log is the mirror image of e to the x across the line y equals x.
> Mirroring swaps every rise with its run. [pause] On e to the x, the tangent at height x has slope x, because slope equals height there. After the mirror, the rise and run trade places, so the matching tangent on the log graph has slope one over x.
> So the slope of the natural log at x is one over x. At two, the slope is one half. At four, one fourth. [pause] The graph keeps rising, but more and more slowly. [long pause]

## The four rules
**On screen:** A formula card: derivative of sine x is cosine x; of cosine x is negative sine x; of e to the x is e to the x; of natural log x is 1 over x.
> Four new rules for your toolkit. They combine with the rules from the last two lessons just like powers do. [long pause]

## In context
**On screen:** "A Ferris wheel seat is H(t) = 30 − 25cos t feet high after t minutes. Find H′(π/2) and interpret it." The height graph, with t-axis ticks labeled π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π and height ticks 20, 40, 60. H′(t) = 25 sin t; H′(π/2) = 25. The tangent at t = π/2 is the steepest part of the graph.
> A Ferris wheel seat is thirty minus twenty-five cosine t feet high after t minutes. Find H prime of pi over two, and say what it means. [try it]
> The thirty disappears, and the derivative of negative twenty-five cosine t is twenty-five sine t.
> At pi over two, sine is one, so H prime is twenty-five. [pause]
> At that moment the seat is rising at twenty-five feet per minute. It's halfway up, where the height graph is steepest. [long pause]

## Combine with earlier rules
**On screen:** "Find f′(x) for f(x) = 3sin x − 4eˣ + 2ln x − cos x." Each term is differentiated in turn, with the constant multiples carried along.
> Find f prime of x for three sine x, minus four e to the x, plus two natural log x, minus cosine x. [try it]
> Term by term: three cosine x, minus four e to the x, plus two times one over x, minus negative sine x. [pause]
> Clean up the signs: f prime of x is three cosine x minus four e to the x plus two over x plus sine x. [long pause]

## A tangent line
**On screen:** "Find the equation for the line tangent to y = eˣ at x = 0." Point (0, 1); slope e⁰ = 1; y − 1 = 1(x − 0), or y = x + 1.
> Find the equation for the line tangent to e to the x at x equals zero. [try it]
> The point: e to the zero is one, so zero, one.
> The slope: the derivative of e to the x is e to the x, and at zero that's one. [pause]
> So y minus one equals one times x minus zero, or y equals x plus one. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The sine wave and its slope graph, cosine, stacked, and e to the x with "slope equals height."
> Sine goes to cosine. Cosine goes to negative sine. e to the x is its own derivative. And the natural log of x goes to one over x. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: Mixing the new rules with the old
**On screen:** "Find f′(x) for f(x) = 4 sin x − 3eˣ + 5 ln x − x²." Term by term: 4 cos x, −3eˣ, 5/x, −2x.
> Find f prime of x for four sine x, minus three e to the x, plus five natural log x, minus x squared. [pause]
> The constant multiple and sum rules still work, so go term by term.
> Four sine x becomes four cosine x. Negative three e to the x stays negative three e to the x. [pause]
> Five natural log x becomes five over x. And x squared gives two x.
> Altogether: four cosine x, minus three e to the x, plus five over x, minus two x.

## Example 2: A tangent line to a sine curve
**On screen:** "Find the equation for the line tangent to y = 2 sin x at x = π/3." Point: 2 sin(π/3) = √3, so (π/3, √3). Slope: 2 cos(π/3) = 1. The line y − √3 = x − π/3 touches the wave.
> Find the equation for the line tangent to two sine x at x equals pi over three. [pause]
> The point: two sine pi over three is two times root three over two, which is root three.
> The slope: the derivative is two cosine x. Two cosine pi over three is two times one half, which is one. [pause]
> The tangent line is y minus root three equals x minus pi over three.

## Example 3: Where is the slope a given value?
**On screen:** "Where does y = eˣ − 2x have a horizontal tangent?" y′ = eˣ − 2 = 0, so eˣ = 2 and x = ln 2. The graph shows the minimum at x = ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
> Where does e to the x minus two x have a horizontal tangent line? [pause]
> The derivative is e to the x minus two. Set it equal to zero: e to the x equals two.
> Undo the exponential with the natural log: x equals the natural log of two. [pause]
> That's about zero point six nine three. That number again. [long pause]
