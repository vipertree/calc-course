# 2.8 The Product Rule

## A tempting mistake
**On screen:** A tempting shortcut is written: "derivative of the product equals the product of the derivatives." Then a test, one line at a time: x squared is x times x. The power rule gives 2x. Each factor, x, has derivative 1, so the shortcut gives 1 times 1, which is 1. A red "not equal" sign appears between 2x and 1.
> Here's a tempting shortcut. To differentiate a product, just multiply the derivatives. [pause]
> Let's check it with a simple function. Take x squared, which is really x times x. [pause]
> We already know its derivative from the power rule: two x. [pause]
> Now try the shortcut. Each factor is just x, and the derivative of x is one. So the shortcut says one times one, which is one. [pause]
> Two x is not one. So we can't just multiply the derivatives together. We need a real product rule. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.8 · The product rule."

## A rectangle that grows two ways
**On screen:** A rectangle with width w and length l, labeled "area A = w · l." Both sides depend on x. When x nudges forward by a tiny dx, the width grows by dw and the length grows by dl. The new area shows the original rectangle, a thin strip along one side of size dw by l, a thin strip along the other of size w by dl, and a tiny corner dw by dl. Each piece is shaded a different gray.
> Picture a product as the area of a rectangle, with width w and length l. Both of them depend on x. [pause]
> Nudge x by a tiny amount, d x. The width grows by a tiny d w, and the length grows by a tiny d l.
> The area gains two strips and a corner. [pause] One strip is d w wide and l long. The other is w wide and d l long. And in the corner, a tiny rectangle, d w by d l. [long pause]

## From the nudge to the limit
**On screen:** The change in area builds from the picture: dA = l·dw + w·dl + dw·dl. Divided by dx: dA/dx = l·(dw/dx) + w·(dl/dx) + dw·(dl/dx). A slider shrinks the nudge: the strips thin out, and the corner shrinks much faster. With the corner term's factor dw → 0 highlighted, what's left is dA/dx = (dw/dx)·l + w·(dl/dx).
> Add up the new area, d A: l d w, plus w d l, plus the corner, d w d l. [pause]
> Divide everything by d x. The strips give l times d w over d x, and w times d l over d x.
> The corner gives d w, times d l over d x. [pause]
> Now let the nudge shrink to zero. d w over d x and d l over d x are the rates the width and length change: they settle on real numbers.
> In the corner term, d l over d x heads to a number, but d w itself heads to zero. So the whole corner term vanishes. [pause]
> What's left: the derivative of w times l is d w d x times l, plus w times d l d x. [long pause]

## Reading off the rule
**On screen:** The letters swap: w becomes f(x) and l becomes g(x): (f · g)′ = f′ · g + f · g′. Then the letters swap again, to u and v, and the card the rest of the lesson uses appears: (uv)′ = u′v + uv′.
> Call the width f of x and the length g of x, and that's the product rule. The derivative of f times g is f prime times g, plus f times g prime. [pause]
> The way most people remember it uses u and v for the two factors. They aren't new kinds of things: u and v are just functions of x, the two factors being multiplied together. The derivative of u v is u prime v, plus u v prime. [long pause]

## In words
**On screen:** The rule in words: "derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second."
> In words: the derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.
> Each factor gets its turn to change while the other one holds still. [long pause]

## Two factors
**On screen:** "Find d/dx [x² sin x]." The factors are underlined and labeled: x² is u, sin x is v. Then u′ = 2x and v′ = cos x. Then u′v + uv′ = 2x sin x + x² cos x.
> Find the derivative of x squared times sine x. [try it]
> Underline the two factors and name them: u is x squared, and v is sine x.
> Their derivatives: u prime is two x, and v prime is cosine x. [pause]
> Put them into u prime v plus u v prime: two x sine x, plus x squared cosine x. [long pause]

## Exponential times a polynomial
**On screen:** "Find d/dx [(x³ − 1)eˣ]." u = x³ − 1 and v = eˣ are underlined and labeled; u′ = 3x², v′ = eˣ; u′v + uv′ = 3x²eˣ + (x³ − 1)eˣ = eˣ(x³ + 3x² − 1).
> Find the derivative of x cubed minus one, times e to the x. [try it]
> u is x cubed minus one, and v is e to the x.
> u prime is three x squared. v prime is e to the x, its own derivative. [pause]
> So the derivative is three x squared e to the x, plus x cubed minus one, times e to the x. Factoring out e to the x: e to the x times x cubed plus three x squared minus one. [long pause]

## At a point, no formula needed
**On screen:** "f(x) = (x² + 1)(x³ − 2x). Find f′(2)." u = x² + 1 and v = x³ − 2x are labeled. A small table fills in: u(2) = 5, v(2) = 4, u′(2) = 4, v′(2) = 10. Then f′(2) = u′(2)v(2) + u(2)v′(2) = (4)(4) + (5)(10) = 66.
> Let f of x be x squared plus one, times x cubed minus two x. Find f prime of two. [try it]
> You don't need the derivative as a formula at all. Label u and v, and find four numbers at x equals two. [pause]
> u of two is five, and v of two is eight minus four, which is four.
> u prime is two x, so u prime of two is four. v prime is three x squared minus two, so v prime of two is ten. [pause]
> Now put the four numbers into the rule: four times four, plus five times ten. Sixteen plus fifty is sixty-six. [long pause]

## In context
**On screen:** "A store sells q items per week at price p dollars, so revenue is R = pq. At one moment p = 40 and rising at $2 per week, while q = 300 and falling at 10 items per week. How fast is revenue changing?" u = p and v = q; R′ = u′v + uv′ = (2)(300) + (40)(−10) = 200 dollars per week.
> The product rule matters whenever two changing things multiply. Revenue is price times quantity. At one moment the price is forty dollars and rising two dollars a week, while sales are three hundred and falling ten a week. How fast is revenue changing? [try it]
> Let u be the price and v the quantity. Then u prime is two, and v prime is negative ten.
> Revenue changes at two times three hundred, plus forty times negative ten. [pause]
> That's two hundred dollars per week, still rising. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The product rule card, with "differentiate one factor at a time, then add" beneath it.
> So the key here is the product rule: the derivative of u times v is u prime v, plus u v prime. Differentiate one factor while the other stays the same, then switch, and add. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A product of two familiar functions
**On screen:** "Find dy/dx for y = x³eˣ." x³ is underlined and labeled u, eˣ is labeled v. u′ = 3x², v′ = eˣ. dy/dx = u′v + uv′ = 3x²eˣ + x³eˣ = x²eˣ(3 + x).
> Find d y d x for x cubed times e to the x. [try it]
> Label the factors: u is x cubed, and v is e to the x.
> u prime is three x squared, and v prime is e to the x, its own derivative. [pause]
> u prime v plus u v prime: three x squared e to the x, plus x cubed e to the x.
> You can factor it as x squared e to the x, times three plus x.

## Example 2: Values from a table
**On screen:** A table: at x = 2, f = 3, f′ = −1, g = 5, g′ = 4. "Let h(x) = f(x)g(x). Find h′(2)." The product rule fills in: (−1)(5) + (3)(4) = 7.
> Here's how the AP exam often asks it: no formulas, just values at a point. Find h prime of two, where h is f times g. [pause]
> h prime of two is f prime of two times g of two, plus f of two times g prime of two.
> That's negative one times five, plus three times four. [pause]
> Negative five plus twelve is seven.

## Example 3: Checking the rule on something we know
**On screen:** "Differentiate (2x + 1)(x² − 3) two ways." Product rule: 2(x² − 3) + (2x + 1)(2x) = 6x² + 2x − 6. Expanding first: 2x³ + x² − 6x − 3, derivative 6x² + 2x − 6. The two results sit side by side with a green equals sign.
> Differentiate two x plus one, times x squared minus three, two ways. [pause]
> With the product rule: two times x squared minus three, plus two x plus one times two x. That simplifies to six x squared plus two x minus six.
> Or expand first: two x cubed plus x squared minus six x minus three. The power rule gives six x squared plus two x minus six. [pause]
> Same answer. The rule agrees with what we already knew. [long pause]
