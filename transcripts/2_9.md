# 2.9 The Quotient Rule

## Dividing changing quantities
**On screen:** A car's fuel efficiency: miles driven divided by gallons used. Both numbers tick upward on two counters, and their ratio flickers.
> Many useful quantities are ratios: miles per gallon, cost per item, concentration per liter. When the top and bottom both change, how fast does the ratio change? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 2.9 · The quotient rule."

## The ratio as a rectangle
**On screen:** A rectangle with area f and width g. Its height is labeled Q, and a caption reads "Q = f / g, because height = area ÷ width." The area f is shaded.
> Here's a way to see a quotient. Take a rectangle whose area is f and whose width is g. [pause]
> Then its height has to be f divided by g. Call that height Q.
> So the quotient is the height of a rectangle. [long pause]

## Nudging the rectangle
**On screen:** x nudges by a tiny dx. The width grows by dg. The area grows by df. The height changes by dQ. The new area shows the familiar pieces: the original rectangle, a thin strip Q by dg, a thin strip g by dQ, and a tiny corner dg by dQ. The equation builds: df = Q·dg + g·dQ + dg·dQ.
> Now nudge x by a tiny d x. The area f changes by d f, and the width g changes by d g. The height has to adjust by some amount, d Q. [pause]
> Just like the product rule, the new area is the old rectangle plus two strips and a corner. [pause]
> So d f equals Q d g, plus g d Q, plus the tiny corner, d g d Q.

## Solving for the change in height
**On screen:** Divide by dx: df/dx = Q·(dg/dx) + g·(dQ/dx) + dg·(dQ/dx). A slider shrinks the nudge and the corner vanishes, leaving f′ = Q·g′ + g·Q′. Then the equation is solved for Q′ step by step, and Q is replaced by f/g: Q′ = (f′ − (f/g)·g′)/g = (f′g − f g′)/g².
> Divide by d x and let the nudge shrink to zero, exactly as before. The corner term vanishes, because d g goes to zero. [pause]
> What's left: f prime equals Q times g prime, plus g times Q prime.
> We want Q prime, the rate the height changes, so solve for it. Subtract Q g prime, and divide by g. [pause]
> Replace Q with f over g, and clear the fraction by multiplying top and bottom by g.
> The result: Q prime is f prime g, minus f g prime, all over g squared. [long pause]

## The rule and a memory aid
**On screen:** The quotient rule on a formula card in u and v: (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v². Beneath it, the f and g version, and the phrase "low d-high minus high d-low, over the square of what's below."
> Here's the quotient rule, written the way to remember it. With u on top and v on the bottom, the derivative of u over v is u prime v, minus u v prime, all over v squared. [pause]
> A classic way to say it: low d high, minus high d low, over the square of what's below. It's the same thing, just read from the other side. [pause]
> Order matters here. The minus sign means switching the terms changes the answer. [long pause]

## A rational function
**On screen:** "Find d/dx [(x² + 1)/(x − 3)]." The top is underlined and labeled u, the bottom v. u′ = 2x, v′ = 1. Then (u′v − uv′)/v² = [(2x)(x − 3) − (x² + 1)(1)]/(x − 3)² = (x² − 6x − 1)/(x − 3)².
> Find the derivative of x squared plus one, over x minus three. [try it]
> Label the top u and the bottom v. u prime is two x, and v prime is one.
> u prime v minus u v prime: two x times x minus three, minus x squared plus one times one. All over v squared, x minus three squared. [pause]
> The top simplifies to x squared minus six x minus one. [long pause]

## With exponentials
**On screen:** "Find d/dx [eˣ/x]." u = eˣ on top, v = x on the bottom, labeled. u′ = eˣ, v′ = 1. (u′v − uv′)/v² = (xeˣ − eˣ)/x² = eˣ(x − 1)/x².
> Find the derivative of e to the x, over x. [try it]
> u is e to the x and v is x. u prime is e to the x, and v prime is one.
> u prime v minus u v prime: x e to the x minus e to the x, over x squared. [pause]
> Factor out e to the x: e to the x times x minus one, over x squared. [long pause]

## At a point, no formula needed
**On screen:** "f(x) = (x² + 3)/(2x − 1). Find f′(2)." u = x² + 3, v = 2x − 1. A table fills in: u(2) = 7, v(2) = 3, u′(2) = 4, v′(2) = 2. f′(2) = [u′(2)v(2) − u(2)v′(2)]/v(2)² = (12 − 14)/9 = −2/9.
> Let f of x be x squared plus three, over two x minus one. Find f prime of two. [try it]
> No need for the whole derivative. Label u and v, and find four numbers at two. [pause]
> u of two is seven, and v of two is three. u prime is two x, so four at two. v prime is two.
> Into the rule: four times three, minus seven times two, all over three squared. [pause]
> Twelve minus fourteen is negative two, so f prime of two is negative two ninths. [long pause]

## Rewrite instead
**On screen:** "Find the derivatives of (a) (x³ + 4x)/x and (b) 5/x² without the quotient rule." (a) simplifies to x² + 4, derivative 2x. (b) rewrites as 5x⁻², derivative −10x⁻³.
> The quotient rule isn't always the easiest path. Find these two derivatives without it. [try it]
> First, x cubed plus four x, over x. The bottom is a single term, so divide: x squared plus four. Its derivative is two x. [pause]
> Second, five over x squared. Rewrite it as five x to the negative two, and the power rule gives negative ten x to the negative three. [long pause]

## In context
**On screen:** "Making n lamps costs C(n) = 2000 + 5n dollars, so the average cost per lamp is A(n) = (2000 + 5n)/n. Find A′(100) and interpret it." u = 2000 + 5n, v = n; A′(n) = [5n − (2000 + 5n)]/n² = −2000/n²; A′(100) = −0.2. The average-cost graph with its tangent at n = 100.
> Making n lamps costs two thousand plus five n dollars, so the average cost per lamp is that total divided by n. Find A prime of one hundred, and say what it means. [try it]
> u is two thousand plus five n, and v is n. u prime is five, and v prime is one.
> u prime v minus u v prime: five n minus two thousand minus five n. The five n's cancel, leaving negative two thousand over n squared. [pause]
> At one hundred, that's negative two thousand over ten thousand: negative point two.
> At one hundred lamps, the average cost is falling by about twenty cents per additional lamp. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The formula card with the memory phrase.
> Low d high minus high d low, over the square of what's below. And before you use it, check whether a simpler rewrite will do. [long pause]
# Worked examples

## Example 1: A rational function
**On screen:** "Find f′(x) for f(x) = (3x − 2)/(x + 4)." The top is labeled u, the bottom v. u′ = 3, v′ = 1. f′(x) = [(3)(x + 4) − (3x − 2)(1)]/(x + 4)². The 3x terms are struck out as they cancel; the top becomes 14.
> Find f prime of x for three x minus two, over x plus four. [try it]
> u is three x minus two, so u prime is three. v is x plus four, so v prime is one.
> u prime v minus u v prime: three times x plus four, minus three x minus two times one. [pause]
> The top is three x plus twelve, minus three x, plus two. The three x terms cancel, leaving fourteen.
> So f prime of x is fourteen over x plus four, squared.

## Example 2: A trig quotient
**On screen:** "Find dy/dx for y = sin x / (1 + cos x)." The pieces assemble: [(1 + cos x)(cos x) − (sin x)(−sin x)]/(1 + cos x)². The top simplifies with sin²x + cos²x = 1 to 1 + cos x, and the result is 1/(1 + cos x).
> Find d y d x for sine x, over one plus cosine x. [try it]
> u is sine x, with u prime cosine x. v is one plus cosine x, with v prime negative sine x. So u prime v is cosine x times one plus cosine x, and u v prime is sine x times negative sine x.
> Subtracting a negative, the top becomes cosine x, plus cosine squared x, plus sine squared x. [pause]
> Cosine squared plus sine squared is one. So the top is one plus cosine x, which cancels one factor from the bottom.
> The derivative is one over one plus cosine x.

## Example 3: Values at a point
**On screen:** A table: at x = 1, f = 6, f′ = 2, g = 3, g′ = −1. "Find the derivative of f/g at x = 1." [(3)(2) − (6)(−1)]/3² = 12/9 = 4/3.
> At x equals one: f is six, f prime is two, g is three, and g prime is negative one. Find the derivative of f over g there. [pause]
> Low d high: three times two, six. High d low: six times negative one, negative six.
> Six minus negative six is twelve. Divide by g squared, which is nine. [pause]
> Twelve ninths, or four thirds. [long pause]
