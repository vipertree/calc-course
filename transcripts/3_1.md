# 3.1 The Chain Rule

## Rates that feed each other
**On screen:** A bakery counter. A clock ticks, and cookies slide out of the oven at 24 per hour. Each cookie drops a 3-dollar coin into a jar. A readout: 24 cookies/hour, 3 dollars/cookie, income ? dollars/hour.
> A bakery sells twenty-four cookies an hour. Each cookie brings in three dollars. [pause]
> How fast is the money coming in? [pause]
> Twenty-four cookies an hour, times three dollars a cookie. Seventy-two dollars an hour. [long pause]

## Units cancel
**On screen:** The product written as fractions: 3 dollars/cookie × 24 cookies/hour. The word "cookie" in the numerator and denominator is struck out with one stroke. What's left: 72 dollars/hour.
> Look at the units. Dollars per cookie, times cookies per hour. [pause]
> The cookies cancel, and you're left with dollars per hour.
> One rate feeds into the next, and the rates multiply. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 3.1 · The chain rule." Unit 3 · Composite, implicit and inverse functions.

## In derivative notation
**On screen:** Three variables appear as labeled dials: t (hours), c (cookies), D (dollars). Arrows connect t → c → D. Under the first arrow, dc/dt = 24; under the second, dD/dc = 3. Below: dD/dt = dD/dc · dc/dt = 3 · 24 = 72.
> Write the same thing with derivatives. Time drives cookies, and cookies drive dollars. [pause]
> The cookies change with time at d c d t, twenty-four. The dollars change with cookies at d D d c, three.
> And the dollars change with time at d D d t, which is the product. [pause]
> In this notation, the cookie terms look like they cancel, just like the units did. [long pause]

## When the rates change
**On screen:** Three horizontal number lines stacked: x on top, u in the middle, y at the bottom. A short segment of length dx on the x-line maps to a longer segment on the u-line, labeled "stretched by du/dx," and that maps to a segment on the y-line, labeled "stretched by dy/du."
> In the bakery, the rates were constant. Usually they aren't. [pause]
> So think about a small nudge instead. Let y depend on u, and u depend on x.
> Nudge x by a tiny amount, d x. The change in u, d u, is about d u d x times that nudge. [pause]
> Then the change in u nudges y. The change in y is about d y d u, times the change in u.
> Each stage stretches the nudge by its own rate. [pause] So the total stretch is the product of the two. [long pause]

## From the nudge to the limit
**On screen:** With the nudges as fractions: dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx), with du ≠ 0 noted. The slider shrinks dx; both fractions settle on derivatives: dy/dx = dy/du · du/dx.
> With tiny nudges, d y over d x is d y over d u, times d u over d x. The d u's cancel, just like fractions. That's just algebra. [pause]
> Now let the nudge shrink to zero. Each fraction settles on a derivative.
> d y d x equals d y d u times d u d x. That's the chain rule. [long pause]

## Which u?
**On screen:** y = u³, u = x² + 1. Two formulas: dy/du = 3u², du/dx = 2x. The question: "dy/dx at x = 1?" The x-line shows 1, the u-line shows 2, with an arrow from x = 1 to u = 2.
> Here's a catch the formula hides. [pause]
> Let y equal u cubed, and u equal x squared plus one. Find d y d x at x equals one.
> d u d x is two x, so at x equals one it's two. And d y d u is three u squared. [pause]
> Three u squared, at which u?

## The trap
**On screen:** A wrong computation in red: 3(1)² · 2 = 6, crossed out. Then the right one in green: at x = 1, u = 2, so dy/du = 3(2)² = 12, and dy/dx = 12 · 2 = 24. A small graph check: the slope of (x² + 1)³ at x = 1 is 24.
> It's tempting to plug in one, because that's the x we care about. That gives three times two, six. [pause] Wrong.
> The rate d y d u has to be measured where u actually is. When x is one, u is one squared plus one, which is two. [pause]
> So d y d u is three times two squared, twelve. Times two, that's twenty-four. The graph agrees.
> The Leibniz formula is honest, but it's a little underspecified. It doesn't say where each derivative is measured. [long pause]

## Function notation says where
**On screen:** f(u) = u³ and g(x) = x² + 1 as two machines in a row. The chain rule in function notation: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x). The g(x) inside f′ glows. Then, boxed: with u = g(x), [f(u)]′ = f′(u) · u′.
> Function notation fixes that. [pause]
> Call the outside function f and the inside function g. The derivative of f of g of x is f prime of g of x, times g prime of x.
> The outside derivative is measured at the inside value, g of x. [pause] That's exactly the u we needed.
> Derivative of the outside, evaluated at the inside, times the derivative of the inside. [pause]
> The short way to write it, and the way to remember it, names the inside u. Then the derivative of f of u is f prime of u, times u prime. [long pause]

## Trig and exponential
**On screen:** Each part with its inside u and u′ named, then f′(u) · u′ written out.
> Find the derivatives of sine of three x, and of e to the x squared. [try it]
> For sine of three x, the inside is u equals three x, with u prime three. Cosine of the inside, times three: three cosine three x. [pause]
> For e to the x squared, the inside is u equals x squared, with u prime two x. e to the x squared, times two x. [long pause]

## A root
**On screen:** u and u′ named, the square-root rule with u inside, and the simplified result.
> Find the derivative of the square root of one plus cosine x. [try it]
> The inside is u equals one plus cosine x, and u prime is negative sine x.
> The outside is a square root, whose derivative is one over two root u. Times u prime. [pause]
> So the derivative is negative sine x, over two times the square root of one plus cosine x. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The bakery returns: the dollars dial, the cookies dial and the clock dial, with the two rates multiplying. Below, the f′(g(x)) · g′(x) formula.
> Rates along a chain multiply. And each rate is measured where its own input actually is. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A fixed-rate chain
**On screen:** "A car uses 1/30 gallon of gas per mile and drives 60 miles per hour. How fast is it using gas?" The units line up: (1/30 gal/mile)(60 miles/hr) = 2 gal/hr.
> A car uses one thirtieth of a gallon of gas per mile, driving at sixty miles per hour. How fast is it burning gas? [pause]
> Gallons per mile, times miles per hour. The miles cancel.
> One thirtieth times sixty is two. Two gallons per hour. [pause]

## Example 2: Outside and inside
**On screen:** "Find the derivative of (2x² + 3)⁴." The outside u⁴ is boxed in one color and the inside 2x² + 3 in another. Result: 4(2x² + 3)³ · 4x = 16x(2x² + 3)³.
> Find the derivative of two x squared plus three, all to the fourth. [pause]
> The outside is a fourth power. Its derivative is four times the inside cubed. Keep the inside exactly as it is.
> Then multiply by the derivative of the inside: four x. [pause]
> Four times four x is sixteen x. The answer is sixteen x, times two x squared plus three, cubed.

## Example 3: Trig and exponential layers
**On screen:** Left: d/dx sin(x³) = cos(x³) · 3x². Right: d/dx e^(5x) = e^(5x) · 5. The inside of each is highlighted.
> Two more. [pause]
> Part a: the derivative of sine of x cubed. [try it]
> The outside is sine, so its derivative is cosine, evaluated at the inside: cosine of x cubed. Times the inside's derivative, three x squared.
> Part b: the derivative of e to the five x. [try it]
> The outside is e to the u, its own derivative, so e to the five x, times five. [pause]

## Example 4: From a table
**On screen:** A table: x = 1: f = 3, f′ = −2, g = 4, g′ = 5; x = 4: f = 0, f′ = 7, g = 1, g′ = −1. "h(x) = f(g(x)). Find h′(1)." The chain rule fills in: f′(g(1)) · g′(1) = f′(4) · 5 = 7 · 5 = 35. The f′(1) cell is crossed out.
> Here's the version the AP exam loves. h of x is f of g of x. Find h prime of one from the table. [pause]
> h prime of one is f prime of g of one, times g prime of one.
> First, g of one. The table says four. So we need f prime of four, which is seven. [pause]
> And g prime of one is five. Seven times five is thirty-five.
> Notice what we didn't use: f prime of one. The outer derivative lives at the inside value. [long pause]
