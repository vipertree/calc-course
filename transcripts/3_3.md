# 3.3 Differentiating Inverse Functions

## Undoing a function
**On screen:** A machine f takes 1 and outputs 2. A note: "algebraic reverse: undo each step, in the opposite order." It gives way to a boxed definition: "g is the inverse of f: f(a) = b means g(b) = a." Under it, "inputs become outputs, outputs become inputs." A second machine, g = f⁻¹, takes 2 and outputs 1. The pair is shown as the points (1, 2) and (2, 1).
> An inverse function undoes a function. [pause]
> We often think of an inverse as an algebraic reverse: undo each step, in the opposite order. That's useful, but the key definition is simpler. [pause]
> If g is the inverse of f, then f of a equals b means g of b equals a. [long pause]
> The inputs become outputs, and the outputs become inputs. [pause]
> So if f turns one into two, then g turns two back into one. [pause]
> On a graph, the point one, two on f becomes the point two, one on the inverse. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 3.3 · Differentiating inverse functions."

## Three ways to say inverse
**On screen:** A card titled "How a problem tells you g is the inverse of f." Three cues appear one at a time: (a) "g is the inverse of f" (or g = f⁻¹); (b) "for every a and b, f(a) = b means g(b) = a"; (c) "f(g(x)) = x for every x."
> A problem can tell you two functions are inverses in a few different ways. Watch for all three. [pause]
> One: it says it outright. g is the inverse of f, or g equals f inverse.
> Two: it gives you the definition. For every a and b, f of a equals b means g of b equals a. [pause]
> Three: it says f of g of x equals x, for every x. Feed an output of g into f, and you get back where you started. So f undoes g, and g is the inverse of f. [long pause]

## The mirror
**On screen:** A curve f and the dashed line y = x. The curve reflects across the line to become f⁻¹. A point (a, b) on f travels across to (b, a) on the inverse.
> Swapping the coordinates of every point is the same as reflecting the whole graph across the line y equals x. [pause]
> So the graph of an inverse is a mirror image.

## Rise and run trade places
**On screen:** A tangent line at (a, b) on f with a rise-over-run triangle: run 1, rise 3. The triangle reflects to the tangent line at (b, a) on f⁻¹, where it becomes run 3, rise 1.
> Watch what the mirror does to a tangent line. [pause]
> Here the tangent to f rises three for every one across. Its slope is three.
> In the mirror, across and up trade places. Now it rises one for every three across. [pause]
> The slope is one third. The reciprocal. [long pause]

## The formula
**On screen:** If f(a) = b, then (f⁻¹)′(b) = 1 / f′(a). The a and b glow in matching colors on the graph.
> So here's the rule. If f of a is b, then the derivative of the inverse at b is one over f prime of a. [pause]
> The slopes are reciprocals, measured at matching points. [long pause]

## An example without the inverse
**On screen:** f(x) = x³ + x. The question: (f⁻¹)′(2)? First: solve a³ + a = 2, giving a = 1. Then f′(1) = 4, and the answer 1/4.
> Take f of x equals x cubed plus x. Find the derivative of its inverse at two. [pause]
> We don't need a formula for the inverse. We need the matching point: where does f equal two?
> One cubed plus one is two, so a is one. [pause]
> f prime of x is three x squared plus one, so f prime of one is four. The inverse's slope at two is one fourth. [long pause]

## The trap
**On screen:** A red computation: 1/f′(2) = 1/13, crossed out. A green arrow points from 2 on the inverse's axis to 1 on f's axis.
> It's tempting to plug two into f prime. [pause] That gives one thirteenth, and it's wrong.
> Two is an input to the inverse. On f's graph, it's an output. The matching input is one. [long pause]

## From a table
**On screen:** The table x = 1, 4; g = 4, 6; g′ = 3, 1/2, with the inverse formula in the corner. "Cue: the problem names g⁻¹ outright." Then "(g⁻¹)′(4) needs g⁻¹(4): what x gives g(x) = 4?" The 4 in the g(x) column lights up, then the x = 1 beside it, with an arrow from the 4 to the 1. Then g(1) = 4, so g⁻¹(4) = 1, and (g⁻¹)′(4) = 1/g′(1) = 1/3.
> g is invertible. Use the table to find the derivative of g inverse at four. [pause]
> The question names g inverse outright, so we know we're working with the inverse of g. [pause]
> The formula needs the matching point, g inverse of four. So what is g inverse of four asking? [pause] It's asking: what x value gives a g value of four? [long pause]
> Look in the g of x column for a four. There it is. Now look across to its x: one. So g of one is four, and g inverse of four is one. [pause]
> The inverse's slope is one over g prime of one: one third. [pause]
> The trap would be g prime of four. The four is an output of g, not an input. [long pause]

## Implicit differentiation agrees
**On screen:** y = f⁻¹(x) rewritten as f(y) = x. Differentiate: f′(y) dy/dx = 1, so dy/dx = 1/f′(y).
> There's another way to see it. If y is f inverse of x, then f of y equals x. [pause]
> Differentiate both sides implicitly: f prime of y, times d y d x, equals one.
> So d y d x is one over f prime of y. [pause] Same rule, and y is exactly the matching point. [long pause]

## The natural log, again
**On screen:** y = ln x rewritten as e^y = x, differentiated implicitly, and solved: dy/dx = 1/e^y = 1/x.
> Use the natural log as the inverse of e to the x to find its derivative. [try it]
> If y is the natural log of x, then e to the y equals x.
> Differentiate implicitly: e to the y, times d y d x, equals one. [pause]
> So d y d x is one over e to the y. And e to the y is x, so the derivative is one over x, just as we found before. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The mirror picture with the two reciprocal slope triangles.
> Reflect the graph, swap rise and run, and the slope flips over. Just measure it at the matching point. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: From given values
**On screen:** "f(g(x)) = x for every x. f(2) = 5 and f′(2) = −4. Find g′(5)." The inverse formula in the corner. Work: "Cue: f(g(x)) = x, so g is the inverse of f." f(2) = 5, so g(5) = 2. g′(5) = 1/f′(2) = −1/4.
> f of g of x equals x for every x. f of two is five, and f prime of two is negative four. Find g prime of five. [pause]
> Here we're told f of g of x equals x, so g is the inverse of f.
> g of five asks: what x gives an f value of five? f of two is five, so g of five is two. [pause]
> So the answer is one over f prime of two: negative one fourth.

## Example 2: A table with a distractor
**On screen:** "For every a and b, g(a) = b means h(b) = a. Find h′(3)." A table: g(1) = 3, g′(1) = 4, g(3) = 7, g′(3) = 2. The inverse formula in the corner. Work: "Cue: g(a) = b means h(b) = a, so h is the inverse of g." "h′(3) needs h(3): what x gives g(x) = 3?" The 3 in the g(x) column lights up, then the x = 1 beside it. g(1) = 3, so h(3) = 1. h′(3) = 1/g′(1) = 1/4. The g′(3) cell flashes: "g′(3) is a distractor."
> For every a and b, g of a equals b means h of b equals a. Use the table to find h prime of three. [pause]
> First, notice how the problem tells us about an inverse. g of a equals b means h of b equals a. That's the definition, so h is the inverse of g.
> The formula needs the matching point, h of three. And h of three asks: what x value gives a g value of three? [pause]
> Look down the g of x column for a three. It's in the first row, where x is one. So g of one is three, and h of three is one. [pause]
> The answer is one over g prime of one: one fourth.
> Three also shows up as an x in the table, next to g prime of three. That's a distractor. The three we care about is an output of g. [long pause]

## Example 3: A tangent line to an inverse
**On screen:** "g is the inverse of f(x) = x³ + x + 1. Find the equation for the line tangent to g at x = 3." The inverse formula in the corner. Work: "Cue: it says g is the inverse of f outright." f(1) = 3, so g(3) = 1; f′(1) = 4, so g′(3) = 1/4. Line: y − 1 = (1/4)(x − 3).
> g is the inverse of x cubed plus x plus one. Find the equation for the line tangent to g at x equals three. [pause]
> This one says it outright: g is the inverse of f.
> Point: g of three asks what x gives an f value of three. f of one is three, so g of three is one. The point on g is three, one.
> Slope: f prime of x is three x squared plus one, so f prime of one is four. The slope of g there is one fourth. [pause]
> The line is y minus one equals one fourth times x minus three. [long pause]
