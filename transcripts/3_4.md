# 3.4 Differentiating Inverse Trigonometric Functions

## Angles from ratios
**On screen:** A ramp with a height-to-length ratio of 1/2. The question "what angle?" appears, and arcsin(1/2) = π/6 answers it. The graphs of sin x on [−π/2, π/2] and arcsin x appear as mirror images across y = x.
> Sine takes an angle and gives a ratio. Arcsine goes backward: give it a ratio, and it gives the angle. [pause]
> Arcsine of one half is pi over six.
> So arcsine is an inverse function, and last lesson told us how to differentiate those. First, a good look at what the inverse trig functions are. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 3.4 · Derivatives of inverse trig functions."

## Undoing sine
**On screen:** A unit circle. The point at angle π/6 appears with its height, 1/2, drawn as a vertical segment. Then the point at 5π/6 appears at the same height, joined by a dashed line, with "sin(π/6) = sin(5π/6) = 1/2" and "Sine is not one-to-one." at the right. The right half of the circle, from −π/2 to π/2, lights up and the 5π/6 point fades. A card: arcsin x takes inputs −1 ≤ x ≤ 1 and gives outputs from −π/2 to π/2.
> On the unit circle, the sine of an angle is the height of its point. [pause]
> Pi over six has height one half. But so does five pi over six. Sine is not one-to-one, so as it stands it can't be undone.
> The fix is to keep one piece of the circle that hits every height exactly once: the right half, from negative pi over two up to pi over two. [pause]
> So arcsine takes a number from negative one to one, and gives back an angle from negative pi over two to pi over two. [long pause]

## Undoing cosine
**On screen:** The unit circle again. The points at π/3 and −π/3 appear with their horizontal distances, both 1/2, joined by a dashed vertical line, with "cos(π/3) = cos(−π/3) = 1/2" at the right. The top half of the circle, from 0 to π, lights up. A card: arccos x takes inputs −1 ≤ x ≤ 1 and gives outputs from 0 to π.
> Cosine is the other coordinate: how far left or right the point is. [pause]
> Pi over three and negative pi over three both have cosine one half, so again we keep just one piece.
> This time it's the top half, from zero to pi. Every left-right position from negative one to one shows up there once. [pause]
> So arccosine takes a number from negative one to one, and gives an angle from zero to pi. [long pause]

## Undoing tangent
**On screen:** The unit circle with a line through the center at angle π/4, labeled "slope 1". It passes through the points at π/4 and 5π/4, with "tan(π/4) = tan(5π/4) = 1" at the right. The right half lights up with open dots at the top and bottom. A card: arctan x takes every real number and gives outputs strictly between −π/2 and π/2.
> Tangent is the slope of the line from the center to the point. [pause]
> Pi over four and five pi over four sit on the same line, so they have the same tangent, one.
> Keep the right half again, but leave off the very top and bottom. A vertical line has no slope, so tangent isn't defined there. [pause]
> Every slope shows up exactly once on that half. So arctangent takes any number at all, and gives an angle strictly between negative pi over two and pi over two. [long pause]

## Arcsecant, briefly
**On screen:** "sec x = 1/cos x, so arcsec x = arccos(1/x)." The top half of the unit circle lights up, with an open dot at the top, π/2. A card: arcsec x takes inputs x ≤ −1 or x ≥ 1 and gives outputs from 0 to π, except π/2.
> Secant is one over cosine, so arcsecant of x is arccosine of one over x. [pause]
> It uses the same top half as arccosine, minus the very top, where cosine is zero.
> Its inputs are numbers at least one, or at most negative one. Arccosecant and arccotangent are built the same way, and you'll rarely need them. [long pause]

## Domains and ranges
**On screen:** A table with a row each for arcsin, arccos, arctan and arcsec: inputs (domain), outputs (range), and the part of the circle the outputs come from. The outputs column lights up.
> Here they are together. The inputs are the numbers each function accepts. [pause]
> The outputs column is the one to watch. An inverse trig function always answers with an angle from its own piece of the circle. [long pause]

## Try a few
**On screen:** A unit circle on the left. Four questions appear one at a time at the right: arcsin(1/2), arccos(−√2/2), arctan(−1) and arcsin(−1). For each, the allowed piece of the circle lights up, the matching point appears, and the answer is filled in: π/6, 3π/4, −π/4, −π/2. For arctan(−1), the point at 3π/4 shows briefly in gray and is crossed out.
> Try a few. For each one, find the point on the allowed piece of the circle.
> Arcsine of one half. [pause]
> On the right half, the point at height one half is at pi over six.
> Arccosine of negative root two over two. [pause]
> On the top half, that's left of center, at three pi over four.
> Arctangent of negative one. [pause]
> On the right half, slope negative one points down and to the right, at negative pi over four. Three pi over four has that slope too, but it's not in the range.
> Arcsine of negative one. [pause]
> Height negative one is the very bottom of the circle: negative pi over two. [long pause]

## Differentiate implicitly
**On screen:** y = arcsin x, so sin y = x. Differentiating: cos y · dy/dx = 1, so dy/dx = 1/cos y.
> Now the derivatives. Let y be arcsine of x. Then sine of y equals x. [pause]
> Differentiate both sides with respect to x. Cosine y times d y d x, equals one.
> So d y d x is one over cosine y. [pause]
> That's correct, but it's in terms of y. We want it in terms of x. [long pause]

## The triangle
**On screen:** A right triangle with angle y. The opposite side is labeled x and the hypotenuse 1, since sin y = x/1. The adjacent side fills in as √(1 − x²) by the Pythagorean theorem. Then cos y = √(1 − x²) and the final formula 1/√(1 − x²).
> Draw a right triangle to translate. [pause]
> Sine y is x, which is x over one. So the opposite side is x and the hypotenuse is one.
> By the Pythagorean theorem, the adjacent side is the square root of one minus x squared. [pause]
> Now read off cosine y: adjacent over hypotenuse, the square root of one minus x squared.
> So the derivative of arcsine x is one over the square root of one minus x squared. [long pause]

## Arctangent
**On screen:** y = arctan x, so tan y = x. Differentiating: sec² y · dy/dx = 1. The identity sec² y = 1 + tan² y = 1 + x² replaces it. Result: 1/(1 + x²).
> Arctangent works the same way. If y is arctangent x, then tangent y equals x. [pause]
> Differentiate: secant squared y, times d y d x, equals one.
> This time an identity does the translating: secant squared is one plus tangent squared, which is one plus x squared. [pause]
> So the derivative of arctangent x is one over one plus x squared. [long pause]

## All six derivatives
**On screen:** A chart of all six derivatives. The left column fills in first: arcsin, arcsec, arctan. Then the right column: arccos, arccsc, arccot, each beside its partner and equal to its negative. The heading "Memorize these." appears above the chart.
> Here are all six together. Arcsine, arcsecant and arctangent come first. [pause]
> Arcsecant comes from the same steps, with secant y equals x. The notes show where its absolute value comes from. [pause]
> Then the co-functions: arccosine, arccosecant and arccotangent. Each one is the negative of the one beside it. [pause]
> Don't worry too much about the proofs. You do need to memorize these six. [long pause]

## Remembering them
**On screen:** The chart stays up. The minus signs in the right column glow. Then the S in arcsin and arcsec glows, along with the subtractions under their square roots, 1 − x² and x² − 1, and the label "S for subtraction" appears. The plus in arctan's 1 + x² glows in another color. Last, the chain-rule form d/dx arctan(u(x)) = u′(x)/(1 + u(x)²) appears below.
> Two tips. First, notice which ones have the minus sign out front: the co-functions, every time. [pause]
> Second, S for subtraction. Sine and secant both start with S, and their derivatives have a subtraction under the square root. [pause]
> Arctangent has an addition instead, one plus x squared, and no square root. [pause]
> With the chain rule, the inside goes in for x, and its derivative goes on top. [long pause]

## Arcsine with the chain rule
**On screen:** Each inverse-trig rule written with u, then u and u′ substituted.
> Find the derivatives of arctangent of three x and arcsine of x squared. [try it]
> For arctangent of three x, u is three x. One over one plus u squared, times u prime: three over one plus nine x squared. [pause]
> For arcsine of x squared, u is x squared. One over the square root of one minus u squared, times two x: two x over the square root of one minus x to the fourth. [long pause]

## Arcsecant of x squared
**On screen:** The arcsecant rule with u stays in the corner. Substituting u = x², u′ = 2x gives 2x/(|x²|√(x⁴ − 1)). The sentence "x² is never negative, so |x²| = x²." Then the x cancels: 2/(x√(x⁴ − 1)).
> Find the derivative of arcsecant of x squared.
> Use the rule with u equal to x squared and u prime equal to two x. That yields two x, over the absolute value of x squared times the square root of x to the fourth minus one.
> Now look at the absolute value. X squared is never negative, so the absolute value of x squared is just x squared. [pause]
> Cancel an x. The derivative is two over x times the square root of x to the fourth minus one, and the absolute value is gone. [long pause]

## Arcsecant of x cubed
**On screen:** The arcsecant rule with u stays in the corner. Substituting u = x³, u′ = 3x² gives 3x²/(|x³|√(x⁶ − 1)). The sentence "x³ has the same sign as x, so |x³| = x²|x|." Then the x² cancels: 3/(|x|√(x⁶ − 1)). Last, "Even powers: |x²| = x². Odd powers keep the sign of x, so the absolute value stays."
> Now find the derivative of arcsecant of x cubed. [try it]
> This time u is x cubed and u prime is three x squared. That yields three x squared, over the absolute value of x cubed times the square root of x to the sixth minus one.
> But x cubed can be negative: it has the same sign as x. So the absolute value of x cubed is x squared times the absolute value of x. [pause]
> Cancel the x squared. The derivative is three, over the absolute value of x times the square root of x to the sixth minus one. This time the absolute value stays. [pause]
> So watch the power. An even power is never negative, so its absolute value is itself. An odd power keeps the sign of x, so the absolute value stays on the x. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The triangle with sides x, 1 and √(1 − x²), and the two main formulas.
> Rewrite as a trig equation, differentiate implicitly, and let a triangle or an identity put the answer back in terms of x. Then memorize the chart. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A slope
**On screen:** "Find the slope of y = arcsin x at x = 1/2." Work: 1/√(1 − 1/4) = 1/(√3/2) = 2/√3.
> Find the slope of arcsine x at x equals one half. [pause]
> One minus one fourth is three fourths. Its square root is root three over two.
> One over that is two over root three. [pause]

## Example 2: The chain rule
**On screen:** "Find d/dx arctan(3x)." The outside 1/(1 + u²) with u = 3x, times the inside's derivative 3: 3/(1 + 9x²).
> Find the derivative of arctangent of three x. [pause]
> The outside gives one over one plus the inside squared: one over one plus nine x squared.
> Times the derivative of the inside, three. [pause]
> Three over one plus nine x squared.

## Example 3: A tangent line
**On screen:** "Find the equation for the line tangent to y = arctan x at x = 1." arctan 1 = π/4, so the point is (1, π/4). Slope 1/(1 + 1) = 1/2. Line: y − π/4 = (1/2)(x − 1), drawn on the curve.
> Find the equation for the line tangent to arctangent x at x equals one. [pause]
> Arctangent of one is pi over four, so the point is one, pi over four.
> The slope is one over one plus one, one half. [pause]
> The line is y minus pi over four equals one half times x minus one. [long pause]
