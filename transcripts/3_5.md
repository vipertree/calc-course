# 3.5 Selecting Procedures for Calculating Derivatives

## A toolbox, not a recipe
**On screen:** A toolbox opens. Labeled tools rise out of it one at a time: power rule, sum and constant rules, product rule, quotient rule, chain rule, implicit differentiation, the special derivatives (sin, cos, eˣ, ln x, tan, arcsin, arctan).
> You now have a full toolbox of derivative rules. [pause]
> The hard part isn't using any one of them. It's knowing which one to pick up first. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 3.5 · Choosing the right rule."

## The last operation
**On screen:** The function x² sin(3x). A number, 2, is plugged in, and the evaluation steps animate in order: square 2, multiply 3 by 2, take the sine, and finally multiply the two results. The final step, "multiply," glows.
> Here's the question to ask. If you plugged in a number, what would be the last thing you'd do? [pause]
> For x squared times sine of three x, you'd square, you'd find the sine, and then, last, you'd multiply.
> So the function is, at its outermost level, a product. [pause] Start with the product rule. [long pause]

## Then repeat inside
**On screen:** The product rule splits the function into two factors. The first, x², is a simple power. The second, sin(3x), is highlighted, and its last operation, "sine of something," glows. The chain rule handles it: cos(3x) · 3. The pieces assemble: 2x sin(3x) + 3x² cos(3x).
> The product rule hands you two smaller jobs: the derivative of each factor. [pause]
> x squared is a power. Easy.
> Sine of three x: its last operation is taking the sine of three x, an input built from x. That calls for the chain rule. [pause]
> Peel from the outside in, one layer at a time. [long pause]

## Layers
**On screen:** The last operation identified as a product, u and v labeled, v′ found with the chain rule, and u′v + uv′ assembled.
> Find the derivative of x squared times sine of three x. [try it]
> The last operation is a multiplication, so start with the product rule: u is x squared, v is sine three x.
> u prime is two x. v prime needs the chain rule: cosine three x, times three. [pause]
> Put it together: two x sine three x, plus three x squared cosine three x. [long pause]

## Rewrite before you start
**On screen:** (x³ − 2√x)/x. A big quotient-rule template starts to fill in, then fades. Instead, each term divides by x: x² − 2x^(−1/2). The power rule finishes it in one line.
> Sometimes the best first move isn't a rule at all. [pause]
> This is a quotient, but the bottom is just x. Divide it into each term first.
> Now it's two powers, and the power rule finishes in one line. [pause]
> Look for rewrites: dividing through, negative exponents, and log rules that turn products into sums. [long pause]

## Rewrite first
**On screen:** The fraction split over its single-term denominator, then the power rule on each term.
> Find the derivative of x cubed minus two root x, all over x. [try it]
> The bottom is a single term, so divide first: x squared minus two x to the negative one half.
> Now the power rule on each term: two x, plus x to the negative three halves. [pause]
> That's much shorter than the quotient rule. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The toolbox, with the question "What's the last operation?" written on its lid.
> Find the last operation, choose its rule, and repeat on each piece. Rewrite whenever it makes the work shorter. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Chain on the outside
**On screen:** "Find d/dx √(x eˣ)." The last operation, a square root, glows. Chain rule: 1/(2√(x eˣ)) times the derivative of the inside. The inside is a product: eˣ + x eˣ.
> Find the derivative of the square root of x e to the x. [pause]
> Last operation: the square root. So the chain rule goes first: one over two times the square root of the inside.
> Now the inside, x times e to the x, is a product: e to the x plus x e to the x. [pause]
> Multiply the two pieces together.

## Example 2: Logs first
**On screen:** "Find d/dx ln(x²/(x + 1))." Log rules rewrite it: 2 ln x − ln(x + 1). Derivative: 2/x − 1/(x + 1).
> Find the derivative of the natural log of x squared over x plus one. [pause]
> You could use the chain rule and then the quotient rule. Or use log rules first.
> The log of a quotient is a difference of logs, and the power comes out front: two ln x minus ln of x plus one. [pause]
> Now it's two over x, minus one over x plus one.

## Example 3: A composite from given values
**On screen:** "f(3) = 1, f′(3) = 4, g(1) = 5, g′(1) = −2, g′(3) = 7. h(x) = x · g(f(x)). Find h′(3)." Product rule first, then chain: g(f(3)) + 3 · g′(f(3)) · f′(3) = 5 + 3(−2)(4) = −19. The g′(3) value is crossed out.
> h of x is x times g of f of x. Find h prime of three. [pause]
> The last operation is a product, so start with the product rule. Derivative of x is one, times g of f of three.
> Plus x, which is three, times the derivative of g of f of x. That's the chain rule: g prime of f of three, times f prime of three. [pause]
> f of three is one. So we need g of one, which is five, and g prime of one, which is negative two.
> Five plus three times negative two times four: negative nineteen. And g prime of three was never needed. [long pause]
