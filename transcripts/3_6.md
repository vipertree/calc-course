# 3.6 Calculating Higher-Order Derivatives

## A derivative is a function
**On screen:** The graph of f(x) = x³ − 3x. Below it, the graph of f′(x) = 3x² − 3 appears. Then, below that, a third set of axes waits with a question mark.
> The derivative of a function is another function. [pause]
> So it has a derivative of its own. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 3.6 · Higher-order derivatives."

## The second derivative
**On screen:** The third axes fill with f″(x) = 6x. The notations appear in a row: f″(x), y″, d²y/dx², d²/dx² [f(x)]. Under d²y/dx², a small note: "d/dx of dy/dx."
> Differentiate f prime, and you get the second derivative, f double prime. [pause]
> For x cubed minus three x, the first derivative is three x squared minus three, and the second is six x.
> In Leibniz notation, it's written d two y d x squared. It means d d x of d y d x: the rate of change of the rate of change. [long pause]

## Keep going
**On screen:** A chain of derivatives: x³ − 3x → 3x² − 3 → 6x → 6 → 0 → 0. Then four terms placed around a loop with arrows between them: sin x → cos x → −sin x → −cos x → back to sin x.
> You can keep going. For x cubed minus three x, the derivatives are three x squared minus three, then six x, then six, then zero. [pause]
> Every polynomial ends up at zero eventually, because each derivative lowers the degree by one. [pause]
> Sine does something different. Its derivatives cycle: sine, cosine, negative sine, negative cosine, and then back to sine. Every four derivatives, you're right back where you started. [pause]
> These are just interesting for now, but both facts come back later in the course. [long pause]

## Position, velocity, acceleration
**On screen:** Three stacked graphs over the same time axis: position s(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t, velocity v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9, acceleration a(t) = 6t − 12. A vertical cursor slides across all three together, and a small car on a track at the top moves to match.
> For motion, this has a familiar name. [pause]
> Position's rate of change is velocity. Velocity's rate of change is acceleration.
> So acceleration is the second derivative of position. [pause]
> Watch the cursor. When acceleration is positive, velocity climbs. When it's negative, velocity falls. [long pause]

## Reading t = 3
**On screen:** The cursor stops at t = 3. Readouts: v(3) = 0, a(3) = 6. The car sits still for an instant, then moves off to the right.
> At three seconds, the velocity is zero. The particle is stopped. [pause]
> But the acceleration is six, so the velocity is increasing. A moment later, the particle is moving to the right. [long pause]

## Implicit second derivatives
**On screen:** The circle x² + y² = 25. First, y′ = −x/y. Then the quotient rule on −x/y, with the y′ inside replaced by −x/y as it appears. The expression simplifies to −(x² + y²)/y³, and the circle's equation turns it into −25/y³.
> Curves defined implicitly have second derivatives too. [pause]
> For the circle, the first derivative is negative x over y.
> Differentiate that with the quotient rule. A y prime shows up in the result, so replace it with negative x over y. [pause]
> After simplifying, the top is x squared plus y squared. And the circle's own equation says that's twenty-five. [pause]
> So the second derivative is negative twenty-five over y cubed. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The chain of stacked graphs, f → f′ → f″, with an arrow continuing to f‴.
> Keep differentiating as long as you need. For motion, the second derivative is the acceleration. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A second derivative
**On screen:** "Find f″(x) for f(x) = x⁴ − 3x² + e^(2x)." Work: f′ = 4x³ − 6x + 2e^(2x); f″ = 12x² − 6 + 4e^(2x).
> Find the second derivative of x to the fourth minus three x squared plus e to the two x. [pause]
> First derivative: four x cubed minus six x plus two e to the two x.
> Differentiate again: twelve x squared minus six plus four e to the two x. [pause]

## Example 2: A particle
**On screen:** "s(t) = 2t³ − 9t² + 12t. Find the acceleration at t = 2, and when it is zero." Work: v = 6t² − 18t + 12; a = 12t − 18; a(2) = 6; a = 0 at t = 1.5.
> A particle's position is two t cubed minus nine t squared plus twelve t. [pause]
> Velocity: six t squared minus eighteen t plus twelve. Acceleration: twelve t minus eighteen.
> At t equals two, the acceleration is six. [pause]
> It's zero when twelve t equals eighteen: t equals one and a half seconds.

## Example 3: Implicit, with a product
**On screen:** "For xy = 4, find y″." Work: y′ = −y/x. Quotient rule: y″ = −(x y′ − y)/x² = −(−y − y)/x² = 2y/x².
> For x y equals four, find the second derivative. [pause]
> First derivative: y plus x y prime equals zero, so y prime is negative y over x.
> Quotient rule on negative y over x: negative, x y prime minus y, over x squared. [pause]
> Replace y prime with negative y over x. The top becomes negative y minus y, negative two y. With the outer minus sign, the result is two y over x squared. [long pause]
