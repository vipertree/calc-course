# 4.2 Straight-Line Motion: Connecting Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

## Back and forth on a line
**On screen:** A car on a straight road marked as a number line. It drives right, slows, turns around to face left at t = 1, drives back, turns around again at t = 3, and drives right. Below, the graph of its position x(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t draws itself, rising, falling, then rising.
> Here's a particle moving along a straight line. Picture it as a car on a straight road. It goes right, turns around, comes back, and turns around again. [pause]
> Its position is x of t equals t cubed minus six t squared plus nine t. The graph tracks the position over time. [pause]
> Careful: the graph is not the path. The car only ever moves left and right on its road. The graph's height is its position. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.2 · Straight-line motion: connecting position, velocity, and acceleration." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## Three functions
**On screen:** Three lines, one at a time: x(t), position, in meters, with a note "(you may also see s(t) or y(t))"; v(t) = x′(t), velocity, in meters per second; a(t) = v′(t), acceleration, in meters per second squared, with a note "(also x″(t))". Then, for the running example: v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9 and a(t) = 6t − 12.
> Start with position. x of t tells you where the particle is at time t. You might also see it called s of t, or y of t. [pause]
> Now think about this. We start at one spot and move to another, and we want to know how fast that happens. What do we call that? [pause]
> That's velocity: the rate of change of position. So v of t is x prime of t. [pause]
> And when our velocity is changing, what do we call that in everyday language? [pause]
> Acceleration: the rate of change of velocity. a of t is v prime of t. That makes it the second derivative of position, x double prime of t. [pause]
> For our particle, the velocity is three t squared minus twelve t plus nine, and the acceleration is six t minus twelve. [long pause]

## Direction
**On screen:** "v > 0: moving right; v < 0: moving left." Then v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t² − 4t + 3) = 3(t − 1)(t − 3), one line at a time. Beside it: t − 1 = 0 or t − 3 = 0, so t = 1 or t = 3. A box: v is a polynomial, so it is continuous; it can change sign only at its zeros, so one test value per section is enough.
> The sign of the velocity tells the direction. Positive means moving right, and negative means moving left. So where is v positive, and where is it negative? [pause]
> Start with the velocity we found: three t squared minus twelve t plus nine. Every term has a factor of three, so pull it out: three times t squared minus four t plus three. [pause]
> Now factor the quadratic. Two numbers that multiply to three and add to negative four: negative one and negative three. So v of t is three times t minus one, times t minus three. [pause]
> Where is the velocity zero? A product is zero only when one of its factors is zero. So t minus one is zero, or t minus three is zero: t equals one, or t equals three. [pause]
> Those two numbers are all we need, because v is a polynomial. That makes it continuous, and a continuous function can only change sign by passing through zero. So on each section between the zeros, the sign stays the same, and one test value tells us the sign for the whole section. A stranger function, with a break in it, might not let us do that. [long pause]

## Testing each section
**On screen:** A number line for t with big marks at t = 1 and t = 3, cutting it into three sections. In each section a question mark, then a test value: v(0) = 9 > 0, v(2) = 12 − 24 + 9 = −3 < 0, v(4) = 48 − 48 + 9 = 9 > 0, and the sign +, −, + centered in its section. Below, a sign table for the factors: 3 is + + +; t − 1 is − + +; t − 3 is − − +; their product v is + − +. Above the line: right, left, right, and "rest" at 1 and 3.
> Mark the zeros on a number line for t: one and three. They cut it into three sections. [pause]
> Before one: is v positive or negative there? [pause] Try t equals zero. v of zero is nine. Positive. [pause]
> Between one and three: try t equals two. v of two is twelve minus twenty-four plus nine: negative three. Negative. [pause]
> After three: try t equals four. v of four is forty-eight minus forty-eight plus nine: nine. Positive. [pause]
> There's a second way to see it: check the sign of each factor. The three is always positive. Before one, t minus one and t minus three are both negative, and plus times minus times minus is plus. Between one and three, t minus one turns positive while t minus three is still negative: plus times plus times minus is minus. After three, all three factors are positive. [pause]
> Both ways give the same chart: plus, minus, plus. So the particle moves right, then left, then right. At one and three it's momentarily at rest: that's where it turns around. [long pause]

## Speed
**On screen:** Velocity −5 and velocity 5 appear side by side, each with an arrow. Both have the same speed label: 5. The formula speed = |v(t)|.
> Speed is how fast, with no direction. It's the absolute value of the velocity. [pause]
> A velocity of negative five and a velocity of five have the same speed: five. [long pause]

## Speeding up and slowing down
**On screen:** "Speeding up when a > 0?" Then four roads, one per case, each with its signs and arrows for v and a, and a car that drives across, facing the way it moves, leaving dots at equal time steps (spreading out when it speeds up, bunching up when it slows down): v > 0, a > 0, speeding up; v < 0, a < 0, speeding up; v > 0, a < 0, slowing down; v < 0, a > 0, slowing down. Then a box: check the signs of v and a. Same signs: speeding up. Different signs: slowing down.
> When is the particle speeding up? It's tempting to say "when the acceleration is positive." But that's not the whole story. [pause]
> Case one: velocity positive, acceleration positive. The car moves right, and the acceleration pushes it right too. It's speeding up. [pause]
> Case two: velocity negative, acceleration negative. The car moves left, and the acceleration also points left. So it moves left faster and faster. That's also speeding up. [pause]
> Case three: velocity positive, acceleration negative. The car moves right, but it's being pulled left. It's slowing down. [pause]
> Case four: velocity negative, acceleration positive. The car moves left, but it's being pushed right, like a driver braking while backing up. It's slowing down. [pause]
> So you don't need the picture. Just check the signs of velocity and acceleration. Same signs: speeding up. Different signs: slowing down. [long pause]

## Distance versus displacement
**On screen:** "What is the total distance traveled from t = 0 to t = 4?" The number line with the trip: from 0 out to 4, back to 0, out to 4 again. Two labels: displacement = x(4) − x(0) = 4 − 0 = 4; total distance = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.
> What is the total distance traveled from t equals zero to t equals four? [pause]
> First, displacement: final position minus initial position. x of four minus x of zero is four minus zero: four. [pause]
> But displacement isn't what the question asks for. The particle went out to four, back to zero, then out to four again. Total distance adds up every stretch traveled, whichever direction: four plus four plus four is twelve. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A box: v(t) = x′(t), a(t) = v′(t) = x″(t), with three short reminders: sign of v = direction; |v| = speed; v and a with the same sign = speeding up.
> Velocity tells direction. Speed is its size. And speeding up means velocity and acceleration have the same sign. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: When is it moving left?
**On screen:** "x(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t for t ≥ 0. When is the particle at rest? When is it moving left?" Work: at rest means v(t) = 0, moving left means v(t) < 0; v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3); at rest at t = 1 and t = 3; v < 0 for 1 < t < 3.
> For x of t equals t cubed minus six t squared plus nine t, when is the particle at rest, and when is it moving left? [pause]
> First, say each question in calculus. At rest means the velocity is zero. Moving left means the velocity is negative.
> Now find the velocity: three t squared minus twelve t plus nine. Factor out three: three times t minus one times t minus three.
> The velocity is zero at t equals one and t equals three. Those are the times it's at rest. [pause]
> Between them, the velocity is negative, so the particle moves left for t between one and three.

## Example 2: Speeding up or slowing down?
**On screen:** "Same particle. Is it speeding up or slowing down at t = 1.5? At t = 2.5?" Work: speeding up means v and a have the same sign, slowing down means opposite signs; v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9, a(t) = 6t − 12; v(1.5) = −2.25, a(1.5) = −3: same sign, speeding up. v(2.5) = −2.25, a(2.5) = 3: opposite signs, slowing down.
> Same particle. Is it speeding up or slowing down at one and a half seconds, and at two and a half? [pause]
> In calculus terms: speeding up means velocity and acceleration have the same sign. Slowing down means opposite signs.
> So we need both. The velocity is three t squared minus twelve t plus nine, and the acceleration is six t minus twelve.
> At one and a half, the velocity is negative two point two five, and the acceleration is six times one and a half minus twelve: negative three. Same sign. Speeding up.
> At two and a half, the velocity is again negative two point two five, but the acceleration is positive three. Opposite signs. Slowing down. Same velocity both times, but different stories. That's why you need the acceleration too.

## Example 3: Total distance
**On screen:** "Find the total distance traveled from t = 0 to t = 4." Work: total distance adds every stretch traveled, so split the trip where v changes sign; turning points t = 1, 3; x(0) = 0, x(1) = 4, x(3) = 0, x(4) = 4; distance = |4 − 0| + |0 − 4| + |4 − 0| = 12. Displacement x(4) − x(0) = 4.
> Find the total distance the particle travels from t equals zero to t equals four. [pause]
> Total distance adds up every stretch, whichever direction. When you know the position function, here's the quick way: split the trip wherever the velocity changes sign. In Unit 8 you'll do this with integrals, and that's what AP free-response questions expect.
> It turns around at one and three, so split the trip there. The positions are zero, four, zero, and four.
> The legs have lengths four, four, and four. The total distance is twelve. [pause]
> The displacement is only four, because the trip doubled back. [long pause]
