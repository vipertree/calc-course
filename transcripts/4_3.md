# 4.3 Rates of Change in Applied Contexts Other Than Motion

## Rates everywhere
**On screen:** Three small panels, each with an illustration: a petri dish of bacteria colonies labeled "bacteria per hour," a brass cash register labeled "dollars per item," and a glass thermometer labeled "degrees per minute."
> Velocity is one rate of change. But rates are everywhere. [pause]
> Bacteria per hour. Dollars per item. Degrees per minute. Each one is a derivative, and you already know how to compute it. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.3 · Rates of change in applied contexts other than motion." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## The rate of the rate
**On screen:** A rising curve that bends downward: a population over time. Tangent lines at three points get less steep. Two labels: P′ > 0, "growing"; P″ < 0, "growing more slowly."
> Here's a population that's growing. Its derivative is positive. [pause]
> But look at the tangent lines. They get flatter. The growth rate itself is shrinking.
> That's the second derivative. P double prime is negative: the population is still growing, just more slowly. [long pause]

## Rate in, rate out
**On screen:** A glass tank with a gauge. Water pours in from a faucet at the top, labeled R(t), and drips out of a drain pipe at the bottom, labeled D(t). The equation A′(t) = R(t) − D(t) appears. With a strong stream in and a slow drip out, the level rises; then the stream thins, the drain runs fast, and the level falls.
> Many problems have something flowing in and something flowing out. [pause]
> If water flows in at rate R and drains at rate D, the amount in the tank changes at R minus D.
> When more comes in than goes out, the amount is increasing. When more goes out, it's decreasing. [long pause]

## Marginal cost
**On screen:** A cost curve C(x) with a tangent line at x = 100. A small step from 100 to 101 shows the actual extra cost next to the tangent line's estimate C′(100).
> In economics, the derivative has its own name: marginal. [pause]
> The marginal cost, C prime of x, is the rate at which cost rises per extra item. [pause]
> So C prime of a hundred estimates the cost of making the hundred and first item. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The three panels from the opening, each now with a derivative and its units beneath it.
> Whatever the context, a derivative is a rate. Find it, give its units, and say what it means. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A fish population
**On screen:** "A lake has P(t) = 1200 + 80t − 2t² fish after t months. Find P′(5) and P″(5), and interpret each." Work: P′(t) = 80 − 4t, P′(5) = 60; P″(t) = −4.
> A lake has twelve hundred plus eighty t minus two t squared fish after t months. Find P prime of five and P double prime of five, and interpret each. [pause]
> P prime of t is eighty minus four t. At five months, that's sixty.
> At five months, the fish population is increasing at sixty fish per month. [pause]
> P double prime is negative four. The growth rate is decreasing by four fish per month, every month. The population grows, but more slowly.

## Example 2: In and out of a tank
**On screen:** "Water flows into a tank at R(t) = 20 + 4t gallons per minute and drains at D(t) = 3t² gallons per minute. Is the amount of water increasing or decreasing at t = 3? At t = 4?" Work: R(3) − D(3) = 32 − 27 = 5; R(4) − D(4) = 36 − 48 = −12.
> Water flows in at twenty plus four t gallons per minute and drains at three t squared. Is the amount increasing or decreasing at three minutes? At four? [pause]
> At three, it flows in at thirty-two and drains at twenty-seven. The net rate is five. Increasing, at five gallons per minute.
> At four, it flows in at thirty-six and drains at forty-eight. The net rate is negative twelve. [pause]
> Decreasing, at twelve gallons per minute.

## Example 3: Marginal profit
**On screen:** "Revenue R(x) = 40x − 0.02x² and cost C(x) = 12x + 3000 dollars for x items. Find P′(500) and the x where P′(x) = 0." Work: P(x) = 28x − 0.02x² − 3000; P′(x) = 28 − 0.04x; P′(500) = 8; P′(x) = 0 at x = 700.
> Revenue is forty x minus point oh two x squared, and cost is twelve x plus three thousand. Find the marginal profit at five hundred items, and where the marginal profit is zero. [pause]
> Profit is revenue minus cost: twenty-eight x minus point oh two x squared minus three thousand. Its derivative is twenty-eight minus point oh four x.
> At five hundred items, that's eight dollars per item: the five hundred and first item adds about eight dollars of profit. [pause]
> The marginal profit is zero at seven hundred items. Past that, each extra item lowers the profit. [long pause]
