# 4.4 Introduction to Related Rates

## A ripple
**On screen:** A stone drops into a pond. Ripples spread out from where it landed, the leading ring growing steadily with two fainter rings behind it. Two readouts tick upward: r, and the area A inside the ripple.
> A stone drops into a pond, and a ripple spreads out. Its radius grows at a steady pace. [pause]
> The area inside the ripple grows too, but not steadily. The bigger the circle, the faster its area grows. [pause]
> The radius and the area are linked, so their rates are linked. That's what this topic is about. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.4 · Introduction to related rates." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## Everything depends on time
**On screen:** The equation A = πr², with small labels "A(t)" and "r(t)" under each variable, and d/dt in front.
> In this problem, both the radius and the area change as time passes. Each one is a function of t. [pause]
> The equation A equals pi r squared holds at every moment. So we can differentiate both sides with respect to time. [long pause]

## The chain rule on every variable
**On screen:** d/dt [A] = d/dt [πr²] becomes dA/dt = 2πr · dr/dt, with the factor dr/dt highlighted.
> On the left, the derivative of A with respect to t is d A d t. [pause]
> On the right, r squared is a function of t, so the chain rule applies: two r, times d r d t. [pause]
> That factor, d r d t, is the whole point. Every variable gets one. [long pause]

## Formulas you need
**On screen:** "No formula sheet on the AP exam." Then, to know by heart: circle A = πr² and C = 2πr; triangle A = ½bh; rectangle A = lw; a² + b² = c²; similar triangles. A box: for a cone, cylinder or sphere, the volume formula is given in the problem when you need it.
> The equations come from geometry, so you need the formulas. [pause]
> There's no formula sheet on the AP exam. Know the flat ones by heart: the area and circumference of a circle, the area of a triangle and a rectangle, the Pythagorean theorem, and similar triangles. [pause]
> For solids, such as a cone, a cylinder or a sphere, the problem gives you the volume formula when you need it. [long pause]

## Where the equation comes from
**On screen:** Four small cards: a right triangle with x² + y² = z²; two similar triangles; a glass cone with V = (1/3)πr²h, marked "given"; a right triangle with tan θ = y/x.
> The equation almost always comes from a picture. [pause]
> Right triangles give the Pythagorean theorem. Shadows and cones give similar triangles. [pause]
> Tanks and balloons give volume formulas, and angles give right-triangle trig. [long pause]

## Four steps to the equation
**On screen:** A list builds up: 1. Draw and label a picture. 2. Name what changes: each changing quantity is a variable. 3. Relate: write an equation that holds at every moment. 4. Differentiate both sides with respect to t.
> Every one of these goes the same way, in four steps. [pause]
> One: draw a picture and label it. [pause]
> Two: name what changes. Each changing quantity gets a letter, and it's a function of t. Anything that never changes, such as the length of a ladder, stays a number. [pause]
> Three: write an equation that relates the quantities, one that holds at every moment. [pause]
> Four: differentiate both sides with respect to t, with the chain rule on every variable. [long pause]

## The ripple
**On screen:** "A stone drops into a pond, and a circular ripple spreads out. Write an equation that relates the area inside the ripple and its radius, then differentiate with respect to time." The pond with rings and the radius r. Work: changing: the radius r and the area A; A = πr²; d/dt[A] = d/dt[πr²]; dA/dt = 2πr dr/dt.
> A stone drops into a pond, and a circular ripple spreads out. Write an equation that relates the area inside the ripple and its radius, then differentiate with respect to time. [pause]
> The picture is labeled. Two things change: the radius r and the area A.
> The equation is the area of a circle: A equals pi r squared.
> Now differentiate both sides with respect to t.
> d A d t equals two pi r, d r d t. That's the equation for the rates. The next topic puts numbers into it. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The four steps again, with "Differentiate both sides with respect to t" highlighted.
> Draw and label, name what changes, write an equation, then differentiate with respect to t. [pause]
> An equation that relates quantities also relates their rates. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: The ladder
**On screen:** "A 13-ft ladder leans against a wall, and its bottom slides away from the wall. Write an equation that relates the bottom's distance from the wall and the height of the top, then differentiate with respect to time." A brick wall and a wooden ladder, labeled x along the ground, y up the wall, and 13 along the ladder, with dx/dt at the foot. Work: changing: x and y; the ladder stays 13 ft; x² + y² = 13²; d/dt[x² + y²] = d/dt[13²]; 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt = 0.
> A thirteen-foot ladder leans against a wall, and its bottom slides away from the wall. Write an equation that relates the bottom's distance from the wall and the height of the top, then differentiate with respect to time. [pause]
> What changes: x, the distance from the wall to the foot, and y, the height of the top.
> The ladder itself never changes. It's always thirteen feet long, so it stays a number.
> The ladder is the hypotenuse, so x squared plus y squared equals thirteen squared.
> Differentiate both sides with respect to t.
> Two x d x d t, plus two y d y d t, equals zero. Thirteen squared is a constant, so its derivative is zero. [long pause]

## Example 2: Two cars
**On screen:** "Priya drives west toward an intersection, and Kai drives north away from it. Write an equation that relates the distance between the two cars to each car's distance from the intersection, then differentiate with respect to time." Two roads at right angles: Priya's car on the east road facing the intersection, x; Kai's car on the north road heading away, y; the distance z between them; rate arrows dx/dt and dy/dt. Work: changing: x, y and z; z² = x² + y²; d/dt[z²] = d/dt[x² + y²]; 2z dz/dt = 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt.
> Priya drives west toward an intersection, and Kai drives north away from it. Write an equation that relates the distance between the two cars to each car's distance from the intersection, then differentiate with respect to time.
> What changes: x, Priya's distance from the intersection, y, Kai's, and z, the distance between them. Kai has already driven through, and they keep heading north.
> The roads meet at a right angle, so z is the hypotenuse: z squared equals x squared plus y squared.
> Which way each car goes doesn't change the equation. Differentiate both sides with respect to t.
> Two z d z d t equals two x d x d t plus two y d y d t. [long pause]

## Example 3: A shadow
**On screen:** "Ana, who is 5 ft tall, walks away from a 15-ft lamppost. Write an equation that relates her distance from the post and the length of her shadow, then differentiate with respect to time." A street lamp, Ana walking, the light ray over her head and her shadow, with x and s marked along the ground. Work: changing: x and s; the heights stay 15 and 5; 15/(x + s) = 5/s; 15s = 5x + 5s; 10s = 5x, so s = x/2; ds/dt = ½ dx/dt.
> Ana is five feet tall, and she walks away from a fifteen-foot lamppost. Write an equation that relates her distance from the post and the length of her shadow, then differentiate with respect to time. [pause]
> What changes: x, her distance from the post, and s, the length of her shadow.
> The two heights never change: fifteen feet and five feet.
> The light ray makes two right triangles, a big one under the lamp and a small one under Ana, and they're similar. So fifteen over x plus s equals five over s.
> Cross-multiply: fifteen s equals five x plus five s.
> Subtract five s from both sides: ten s equals five x, so s equals x over two.
> Differentiate: d s d t is one half of d x d t. [long pause]

## Example 4: Filling a cone
**On screen:** "Water pours into a cone, point down, that is 9 ft tall with radius 3 ft at the top. Write an equation that relates the volume of the water and its depth, then differentiate with respect to time. (The volume of a cone is V = ⅓πr²h.)" A glass cone with water, labeled h, r, 9 and 3. Work: changing: V, h and r; the cone stays 9 by 3; r/h = 3/9, so r = h/3; V = ⅓π(h/3)²h; V = (π/27)h³; dV/dt = (π/9)h² dh/dt.
> Water pours into a cone, point down, that is nine feet tall with a radius of three feet at the top. Write an equation that relates the volume of the water and its depth, then differentiate with respect to time. The problem gives the cone's volume formula.
> What changes: the volume V, the depth h, and the radius r of the water's surface.
> The cone itself never changes: nine feet tall, with a radius of three at the top.
> We want volume and depth only, so r has to go. The water is always the same shape as the cone, so by similar triangles, r over h equals three over nine. So r equals h over three.
> Put that into the volume formula: one third pi, times h over three squared, times h.
> That's pi over twenty-seven, h cubed.
> Differentiate: d V d t equals pi over nine, h squared, d h d t. [long pause]
