# 4.4 Introduction to Related Rates

## A ripple
**On screen:** A stone drops into a pond. Ripples spread out from where it landed, the leading ring growing steadily with two fainter rings behind it. Two readouts tick upward: r, and the area A inside the ripple.
> A stone drops into a pond, and a ripple spreads out. Its radius grows at a steady two centimeters per second. [pause]
> The area inside the ripple grows too, but not steadily. The bigger the circle, the faster its area grows. [pause]
> The radius and the area are linked, so their rates are linked. That's what this topic is about. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.4 · Introduction to related rates." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## Everything depends on time
**On screen:** The equation A = πr², with small labels "A(t)" and "r(t)" under each variable, and d/dt in front.
> In this problem, both the radius and the area change as time passes. Each one is a function of t. [pause]
> The equation A equals pi r squared holds at every moment. So we can differentiate both sides with respect to time. [long pause]

## The chain rule on every variable
**On screen:** d/dt [A] = d/dt [πr²] becomes dA/dt = 2πr · dr/dt, with the factor dr/dt highlighted.
> On the left, the derivative of A with respect to t is d A d t. [pause]
> On the right, r squared is a function of t, so the chain rule applies: two r, times d r d t. [pause]
> That factor, d r d t, is the whole point. Every variable gets one. [long pause]

## Differentiate first, then substitute
**On screen:** Two paths from A = πr². Wrong: substitute r = 10 to get A = 100π, then differentiate to get 0, crossed out. Right: differentiate to get dA/dt = 2πr dr/dt; then r = 10 and dr/dt = 2 go in. A note: substituting and solving is Topic 4.5.
> Here's the trap. [pause] If you plug in r equals ten first, the area becomes the constant one hundred pi, and its derivative is zero. That's wrong: the area is growing.
> The radius is only ten for an instant. Differentiate first, while it's still a variable. [pause]
> Only then do the values for that instant go in. Substituting and solving is the next topic. In this one, the goal is the differentiated equation. [long pause]

## Formulas you need
**On screen:** "No formula sheet on the AP exam." Then, to know by heart: circle A = πr² and C = 2πr; triangle A = ½bh; rectangle A = lw; a² + b² = c²; similar triangles. A box: for a cone, cylinder or sphere, the volume formula is given in the problem when you need it.
> The equations come from geometry, so you need the formulas. [pause]
> There's no formula sheet on the AP exam. Know the flat ones by heart: the area and circumference of a circle, the area of a triangle and a rectangle, the Pythagorean theorem, and similar triangles. [pause]
> For solids, like a cone, a cylinder or a sphere, the problem gives you the volume formula when you need it. [long pause]

## Five steps to the equation
**On screen:** A list builds up: 1. Know: values at the instant, and the given rates. 2. Want: the rate you're after. 3. Fixed: constants that never change. 4. Equation: relate the quantities. 5. Differentiate with respect to t.
> Every setup goes the same way, in five steps. [pause]
> One: know. Write down the values at the instant, and the rates you're given, with units. [pause]
> Two: want. Name the rate you're looking for. [pause]
> Three: fixed. Some things never change, like the length of a ladder. Those are just numbers, and they can go in from the start. [pause]
> Four: write an equation that relates the quantities. [pause]
> Five: differentiate both sides with respect to t. [long pause]

## A growing square
**On screen:** A square with sides s and area A. Work: know ds/dt = 3 cm/s and s = 4 cm; want dA/dt, nothing fixed; A = s²; dA/dt = 2s ds/dt.
> The side of a square grows at three centimeters per second. How fast is the area growing when the side is four? Let's set it up. [pause]
> Know: d s d t is three centimeters per second, and at the instant, s is four.
> Want: d A d t. Nothing here is fixed. [pause]
> The equation: A equals s squared.
> Differentiate with respect to t: d A d t equals two s d s d t. That's the setup. In the next topic, we'll put in the numbers. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The five steps again, with "Differentiate with respect to t" highlighted.
> Know, want, fixed, an equation, then differentiate. [pause]
> An equation that relates quantities also relates their rates. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: The ripple's area
**On screen:** "A ripple's radius grows at 2 cm/s. How fast is the area growing when r = 10 cm? Set it up: stop at the differentiated equation." A pond with rings and the radius r. Work: know dr/dt = 2, r = 10, want dA/dt; A = πr²; dA/dt = 2πr dr/dt.
> A ripple's radius grows at two centimeters per second. How fast is the area growing when the radius is ten? Set it up. [pause]
> Know: d r d t is two, and r is ten. Want: d A d t.
> The equation: A equals pi r squared.
> Differentiate: d A d t equals two pi r d r d t. [long pause]

## Example 2: The ladder
**On screen:** "A 13-ft ladder leans against a wall. The bottom slides away at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the bottom is 5 ft from the wall? Set it up." A brick wall and a wooden ladder, labeled x along the ground, y up the wall, and 13 along the ladder, with dx/dt at the foot. Work: know dx/dt = 2 ft/s, x = 5 ft; want dy/dt; fixed: the ladder, 13 ft; x² + y² = 13²; 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt = 0.
> A thirteen-foot ladder leans against a wall. The bottom slides away at two feet per second. How fast is the top sliding down when the bottom is five feet from the wall? Let's set it up. [pause]
> Know: x is the distance from the wall to the foot. d x d t is two feet per second, and at the instant, x is five.
> Want: y is the height of the top, so we want d y d t.
> Fixed: the ladder itself. It's always thirteen feet long.
> The equation: the ladder is the hypotenuse, so x squared plus y squared equals thirteen squared.
> Differentiate: two x d x d t, plus two y d y d t, equals zero. Thirteen squared is a constant, so its derivative is zero. [long pause]

## Example 3: Filling a cone
**On screen:** "Water pours into a cone, point down, 9 ft tall with radius 3 ft, at 2 ft³/min. How fast is the water rising when it is 6 ft deep? (The volume of a cone is V = ⅓πr²h.) Set it up." A glass cone with water, labeled h, r, 9 and 3. Work: know dV/dt = 2, h = 6, want dh/dt; fixed: r/h = 3/9, so r = h/3; V = (π/27)h³; dV/dt = (π/9)h² dh/dt.
> Water pours into a cone, point down, nine feet tall with a radius of three feet, at two cubic feet per minute. How fast is the water rising when it's six feet deep? The problem gives the cone's volume formula. Let's set it up. [pause]
> Know: d V d t is two cubic feet per minute, and h is six. Want: d h d t.
> Fixed: the cone's shape. The water is always the same shape as the cone, so by similar triangles, r over h equals three over nine. So r equals h over three.
> Put that into the volume formula: one third pi times h over three squared, times h. That's pi over twenty-seven, h cubed.
> Differentiate: d V d t equals pi over nine, h squared, d h d t. [long pause]

## Example 4: Two cars
**On screen:** "Two cars leave an intersection at the same time, one north at 30 mph and one east at 40 mph. How fast is the distance between them growing after 2 hours? Set it up." Two roads at right angles with a car on each, x east, y north, and the distance z between them. Work: know dx/dt = 40, dy/dt = 30, t = 2; want dz/dt; z² = x² + y²; 2z dz/dt = 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt.
> Two cars leave an intersection at the same time, one heading north at thirty miles per hour and one heading east at forty. How fast is the distance between them growing after two hours? Set it up. [pause]
> Know: d x d t is forty, east, and d y d t is thirty, north. The instant is two hours in.
> Want: d z d t, where z is the distance between the cars.
> The equation: z is the hypotenuse, so z squared equals x squared plus y squared.
> Differentiate: two z d z d t equals two x d x d t plus two y d y d t. [long pause]

## Example 5: A shadow
**On screen:** "Ana, who is 5 ft tall, walks away from a 15-ft lamppost at 4 ft/s. How fast is her shadow lengthening? Set it up." A street lamp, Ana walking, the light ray over her head and her shadow, with x and s marked along the ground. Work: know dx/dt = 4, want ds/dt, fixed heights 15 and 5; 15/(x + s) = 5/s; s = x/2; ds/dt = ½ dx/dt.
> Ana is five feet tall, and she walks away from a fifteen-foot lamppost at four feet per second. How fast is her shadow lengthening? Set it up. [pause]
> Know: x is her distance from the post, and d x d t is four. Want: d s d t, where s is her shadow's length. Fixed: the two heights, fifteen and five.
> The equation: the big triangle and the small one are similar, so fifteen over x plus s equals five over s.
> Cross-multiply and simplify: fifteen s equals five x plus five s, so s equals x over two.
> Differentiate: d s d t is one half of d x d t. [long pause]
