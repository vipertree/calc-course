# 4.6 Approximating Values of a Function Using Local Linearity and Linearization

## Zoom in
**On screen:** The graph of y = √x with a point marked at x = 4 and its tangent line. The camera zooms in on the point, again and again, until the curve and the tangent line can't be told apart.
> Here's the graph of y equals the square root of x, with its tangent line at x equals four. [pause]
> Let's zoom in on that point. And again. And again. [pause]
> Up close, the curve and its tangent line are almost the same line. A differentiable curve is locally linear. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.6 · Approximating values using local linearity and linearization." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## The tangent line as an estimate
**On screen:** The point (a, f(a)) and the tangent line. The camera zooms in close around the point, where the curve and the tangent line nearly overlap. A dot starts at (a, f(a)) and slides a short way along the tangent line; the small run x − a and the rise f′(a)(x − a) appear under it, with the curve's true value just beside the dot. The camera zooms back out, and the formula L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) is written.
> The tangent line at x equals a starts at the height f of a. Let's zoom in close to that point. [pause]
> Start at the value we know, and move just a little: a run of x minus a. The line rises by the slope times the run: f prime of a, times x minus a. Up close, the curve is right there beside the line. [pause]
> So L of x equals f of a, plus f prime of a times x minus a. We call it the linearization of f at a. [pause]
> For x near a, f of x is approximately L of x. [pause]
> Don't think of this as a new formula to memorize. It's just the equation of the tangent line, which you already know how to write. Start at a point, move along the slope a little, and evaluate. [long pause]

## From point-slope form
**On screen:** A board, one move per line, with a small gray note beside some lines: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) ("point-slope form"); y = y₁ + m(x − x₁) ("add y₁ to both sides"); point: (x₁, y₁) = (a, f(a)); slope: m = f′(a); y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) ("substitute"); L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a), boxed, labeled "the linearization".
> That formula comes straight from point-slope form, from Algebra 1: y minus y one equals m times x minus x one. [pause]
> Add y one to both sides: y equals y one plus m times x minus x one. [pause]
> For the tangent line at a, the point is a, f of a. So x one is a, and y one is f of a. [pause]
> The slope is the derivative at a: m equals f prime of a. [pause]
> Substitute both: y equals f of a, plus f prime of a times x minus a. [pause]
> Call that y L of x, and you have the linearization. So you can work either way: write point-slope form and solve for y, or go straight to L of x. [long pause]

## Using given values
**On screen:** "f(4) = 10 and f′(4) = 2.5. Estimate f(4.2)." Set aside and labeled: "point (4, 10), slope m = 2.5". Then L(x) = 10 + 2.5(x − 4), evaluated at 4.2.
> f of four is ten, and f prime of four is two and a half. Estimate f of four point two. [pause]
> Those are the point and the slope. The point is four, ten, and the slope is m equals two point five. [pause]
> The linearization at four is ten plus two point five times x minus four. [pause]
> At four point two: ten plus two point five times point two. Ten point five. [long pause]

## Choosing a
**On screen:** "Estimate √26." The values 25 and 26 on a number line, with 25 circled: √25 = 5 is exact.
> To estimate the square root of twenty-six, choose a nearby a where the function is easy to evaluate exactly. [pause]
> Twenty-five is close, and its square root is exactly five. [long pause]

## Too big or too small
**On screen:** Two panels. Left: a curve bending down, below its tangent line, with the line's estimate marked above the true value: "overestimate." Right: a curve bending up, above its tangent line: "underestimate."
> Is the estimate too big or too small? Look at which side of the line the curve is on. [pause]
> If the curve bends down, below its tangent line, the line's values are too big: an overestimate. [pause]
> If the curve bends up, above its tangent line, the line's values are too small: an underestimate. [pause]
> In Unit 5 these shapes get names: concave down and concave up. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The formula L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) in a box, with the curve and its tangent line behind it.
> So the key here: a linearization is just the tangent line. Write its equation, evaluate it near the point, and use the curve's bend to tell you whether the estimate is too big or too small. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: The square root of 26
**On screen:** "Use a linearization to estimate √26. Is the estimate too big or too small?" Work: f(x) = √x, a = 25; point: f(25) = 5, so (25, 5); f′(x) = 1/(2√x); slope: m = f′(25) = 1/10; set aside and labeled, "point (25, 5), slope m = 1/10"; worked in point-slope form: y − 5 = (1/10)(x − 25), then y = 5 + (1/10)(x − 25); at x = 26, 5 + (1/10)(1) = 5.1; the graph bends down, so 5.1 is an overestimate.
> Use a linearization to estimate the square root of twenty-six. Is the estimate too big or too small? [pause]
> Let f of x be the square root of x, and use a equals twenty-five. f of twenty-five is five, so the point is twenty-five, five: x one is twenty-five and y one is five.
> f prime of x is one over two root x, so the slope m is f prime of twenty-five, one tenth. [pause]
> Set them aside: the point is twenty-five, five, and the slope is m equals one tenth. [pause]
> This time, work straight from point-slope form: y minus five equals one tenth times x minus twenty-five. [pause]
> Solve for y: y equals five plus one tenth times x minus twenty-five. That's the linearization. [pause]
> At twenty-six: five plus one tenth times one. The square root of twenty-six is about five point one. [pause]
> The square root graph bends down, below its tangent lines. So five point one is a little too big: an overestimate.

## Example 2: From given values
**On screen:** "f(2) = 7 and f′(2) = −3. Estimate f(2.1)." Work: set aside and labeled, "point (2, 7), slope m = −3"; L(x) = 7 − 3(x − 2); f(2.1) ≈ 7 − 3(0.1) = 6.7.
> f of two is seven, and f prime of two is negative three. Estimate f of two point one. [pause]
> We don't have a formula for f, and we don't need one. The point is two, seven, and the slope is m equals negative three. [pause]
> L of x equals seven minus three times x minus two.
> At two point one: seven minus three times one tenth. About six point seven.

## Example 3: A cube root
**On screen:** "Use a linearization to estimate ∛8.12." Work: a = 8; point: f(8) = 2, so (8, 2); f′(x) = 1/(3x^(2/3)); slope: m = f′(8) = 1/12; set aside and labeled, "point (8, 2), slope m = 1/12"; y − 2 = (1/12)(x − 8); y = 2 + (1/12)(x − 8); ∛8.12 ≈ 2 + (1/12)(0.12) = 2.01.
> Use a linearization to estimate the cube root of eight point one two. [pause]
> Use a equals eight, since the cube root of eight is exactly two. So the point is eight, two: x one is eight, and y one is two.
> The derivative of x to the one third is one third x to the negative two thirds. At eight, that's one third times one fourth: the slope m is one twelfth. [pause]
> Set them aside: the point is eight, two, and the slope is m equals one twelfth. [pause]
> Point-slope form: y minus two equals one twelfth times x minus eight. [pause]
> Solve for y: y equals two plus one twelfth times x minus eight. [pause]
> At eight point one two: two plus one twelfth times point one two. Two point zero one. [long pause]
