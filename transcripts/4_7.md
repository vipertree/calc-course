# 4.7 Using L'Hospital's Rule for Determining Limits of Indeterminate Forms

## A race to zero
**On screen:** Two graphs on the same axes, f(x) = sin(3x) and g(x) = x, both passing through the origin. As x slides toward 0, readouts show f(x), g(x) and their ratio. Both heights shrink toward 0, but the ratio settles near 3.
> Here are two functions, sine of three x and x. Both pass through zero at x equals zero. [pause]
> As x closes in on zero, both heights shrink to nothing. So what happens to their ratio? [pause]
> Zero over zero doesn't tell us. But the readout settles near three. Where does three come from? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 4.7 · Using L'Hospital's Rule for determining limits of indeterminate forms." Unit 4 · Contextual Applications of Differentiation.

## Zoom in on zero over zero
**On screen:** Zoom in at the origin. Each curve straightens into its tangent line: f looks like y = 3x and g looks like y = x. The slopes 3 and 1 are labeled.
> Zoom in at the point where both graphs cross zero. [pause]
> Up close, each curve looks like its tangent line. The top one looks like three x, and the bottom one looks like one x. [pause]
> So near zero, the ratio is close to three x over x. The x's cancel. Three over one: the ratio of the slopes. [long pause]

## The rule
**On screen:** L'Hospital's Rule in a box: if lim f(x) = 0 and lim g(x) = 0, or both limits are infinite, then lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f′(x)/g′(x). Beneath it: "not the quotient rule."
> That's L'Hospital's Rule. If the top and the bottom both go to zero, or both go to infinity, the limit of the ratio equals the limit of the ratio of their derivatives. [pause]
> Differentiate the top and the bottom separately. This is not the quotient rule. [long pause]

## Check the form first
**On screen:** The limit as x approaches 1 of (x² + 1)/(x + 1). Plugging in gives 2/2 = 1. A crossed-out wrong path uses the rule anyway and gets 2.
> The rule only works for zero over zero or infinity over infinity. [pause]
> This limit is two over two. That's just one. Use the rule here and you get two, which is wrong. [pause]
> On the AP exam, you must show the form before you use the rule. It's worth a point. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A checklist: 1. Check the form: 0/0 or ∞/∞? 2. Differentiate top and bottom separately. 3. Take the new limit; repeat if it's still indeterminate.
> Check the form. Differentiate the top and the bottom. Take the new limit, and if it's still indeterminate, do it again. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A sine over x
**On screen:** "Find lim as x → 0 of sin(3x)/x." Work: lim sin(3x) = 0 and lim x = 0: 0/0; lim 3cos(3x)/1 = 3.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of sine of three x, over x. [pause]
> Check the form: the top goes to zero, and the bottom goes to zero. Zero over zero.
> Differentiate the top and the bottom: three cosine three x, over one. [pause]
> At zero, that's three.

## Example 2: A log against a line
**On screen:** "Find lim as x → ∞ of (ln x)/x." Work: ∞/∞; lim (1/x)/1 = 0.
> Find the limit as x approaches infinity of the natural log of x, over x. [pause]
> Both the top and the bottom grow without bound: infinity over infinity.
> Differentiate: one over x, over one. [pause]
> As x goes to infinity, one over x goes to zero. The line wins.

## Example 3: Twice
**On screen:** "Find lim as x → 0 of (eˣ − 1 − x)/x²." Work: 0/0; lim (eˣ − 1)/(2x): still 0/0; lim eˣ/2 = 1/2.
> Find the limit as x approaches zero of e to the x, minus one, minus x, all over x squared. [pause]
> At zero, the top is one minus one minus zero, which is zero. The bottom is zero. Zero over zero.
> Differentiate: e to the x minus one, over two x. [pause]
> Check again: that's still zero over zero. So use the rule a second time.
> e to the x over two. At zero, that's one half. [long pause]
