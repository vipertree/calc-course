# 5.1 Using the Mean Value Theorem

## A speeding ticket
**On screen:** A straight toll road with two toll booths 120 miles apart. A car enters at 1:00 and leaves at 3:00. A ticket appears: "average speed 60 mph."
> A car enters a toll road at one o'clock and leaves a hundred and twenty miles later, at three. [pause]
> Its average speed was sixty miles per hour. [pause]
> Here's the claim: at some instant, the speedometer read exactly sixty. Not about sixty. Exactly sixty. Why must that be true? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.1 · Using the Mean Value Theorem." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## Slide the secant
**On screen:** A smooth curve with points at x = a and x = b joined by a secant line. A copy of the secant slides sideways, staying parallel, until it touches the curve at a point c, where it becomes a tangent line. Then the copy slides back across the secant to the other side and touches the curve again at a second point, c₂. A note: "only one c is guaranteed."
> Here's a smooth curve. Connect the points at a and b with a secant line. Its slope is the average rate of change. [pause]
> Now slide a copy of that line sideways, keeping it parallel. [pause]
> At some point it just touches the curve. There, the tangent line has the same slope as the secant line. [pause]
> So at that point c, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate. [pause]
> Slide the line the other way, and it touches the curve a second time. [pause]
> This curve happens to have two such points. The theorem only guarantees one. [long pause]

## The theorem
**On screen:** The Mean Value Theorem in a box: if f is continuous on [a, b] and differentiable on (a, b), then f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a) for some c in (a, b). Below the box, in words: "At some c between a and b, the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change over [a, b]."
> That's the Mean Value Theorem. If f is continuous on the closed interval, and differentiable on the open interval, [pause]
> then somewhere between a and b, f prime of c equals f of b minus f of a, over b minus a. [pause]
> In words: at some point c between a and b, the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change over the interval. [long pause]

## Closed and open
**On screen:** The two conditions on the right: "continuous on [a, b]" with "endpoints included" beneath it, and "differentiable on (a, b)" with "endpoints don't need a derivative" beneath it. Then, on the left, the graph of y = x³ − x running well past both ends of a shaded interval [a, b], labeled "polynomial: continuous and differentiable everywhere." Last: "Justify with both: continuous and differentiable."
> Let's slow down on those two conditions. [pause]
> Continuous on the closed interval means no jumps or holes anywhere from a to b, including the endpoints. [pause]
> Differentiable on the open interval means no corners or cusps strictly between a and b. The endpoints themselves don't need a derivative. [pause]
> On most problems, that difference between closed and open never comes up. You'll usually have something like a polynomial, which is continuous and differentiable well past both ends of the interval. [pause]
> So the endpoints rarely matter. But when you justify the Mean Value Theorem, still state both conditions: continuous and differentiable. [long pause]

## When it fails
**On screen:** The graph of y = |x| from −1 to 2 with its secant line, slope 1/3. The tangent slopes are only −1 and 1. The corner at 0 is circled.
> Both conditions matter. Here's the absolute value of x, from negative one to two. [pause]
> The secant line has slope one third. But every tangent line has slope negative one or one. None is parallel. [pause]
> The problem is the corner at zero: f isn't differentiable there, so the theorem makes no promise. [long pause]

## From given values
**On screen:** The two conditions checked, the average rate 2, and the MVT conclusion.
> f is differentiable, f of one is four, and f of five is twelve. Must f prime of c equal two somewhere between one and five? [try it]
> Check the conditions. Differentiable means continuous too, so both hold on the interval.
> The average rate of change: twelve minus four, over four. Two. [pause]
> So yes: by the Mean Value Theorem, f prime of c is two for some c between one and five. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The boxed theorem, with "continuous on [a, b]" and "differentiable on (a, b)" highlighted. Beneath: "On the AP exam: name the theorem and check both conditions."
> Somewhere, the instantaneous rate equals the average rate, as long as both conditions hold. On the AP exam, name the theorem and check its conditions. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Finding c
**On screen:** "Find every c guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem for f(x) = x³ − x on [0, 2]." Work, one line at a time: f is a polynomial, so it is continuous and differentiable; average rate (6 − 0)/2 = 3; f′(c) = 3c² − 1 = 3; 3c² = 4; c² = 4/3; c = ±√(4/3) = ±2/√3; 2/√3 ≈ 1.15 is in (0, 2), −2/√3 is not; c = 2/√3.
> Find every c guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem for f of x equals x cubed minus x, on zero to two. [pause]
> f is a polynomial, so it's continuous and differentiable everywhere. The theorem applies.
> The average rate of change: f of two is six, f of zero is zero, so six over two is three. [pause]
> Set the derivative equal to that: three c squared minus one equals three. [pause]
> Add one to both sides: three c squared equals four.
> Divide both sides by three: c squared equals four thirds. [pause]
> Take the square root of both sides, and keep the plus or minus: c equals plus or minus the square root of four thirds, which is plus or minus two over root three. [pause]
> Now check which ones are in the interval. Two over root three is about one point one five, between zero and two, so it counts. Negative two over root three is below zero, so it doesn't. [pause]
> So c equals two over root three. [long pause]

## Example 2: Does it apply?
**On screen:** "Does the Mean Value Theorem apply to f(x) = 1/(x − 3) on [1, 5]?" Work: f is not continuous at x = 3, which is in [1, 5]; the theorem does not apply.
> Does the Mean Value Theorem apply to one over x minus three, on one to five? [pause]
> Check the conditions. At x equals three, f is undefined, so it isn't continuous there.
> Three is inside the interval. So the theorem doesn't apply.

## Example 3: The speedometer
**On screen:** "A car's position s(t) is differentiable. It travels 120 miles from t = 1 to t = 3 hours. Show that its velocity was exactly 60 mph at some time." Work: s is differentiable, so it is continuous on [1, 3] and differentiable on (1, 3); by the MVT, s′(c) = (s(3) − s(1))/(3 − 1) = 120/2 = 60 for some c in (1, 3).
> A car's position s of t is differentiable. It travels a hundred and twenty miles from t equals one to t equals three hours. Show that its velocity was exactly sixty at some time. [pause]
> s is differentiable, so it's also continuous, on the interval from one to three.
> By the Mean Value Theorem, some c between one and three has s prime of c equal to a hundred and twenty over two. [pause]
> Sixty miles per hour. [long pause]

## Example 4: From a table
**On screen:** "g is differentiable. Must there be a value c, with 0 < c < 10, for which g′(c) = −3?" Table: t = 0, 2, 5, 9, 10; g(t) = 4, 10, 1, 13, 7. Work: need a pair with average rate −3; whole table (7 − 4)/10 = 0.3, no help; (g(5) − g(2))/(5 − 2) = (1 − 10)/3 = −3, with the t = 2 and t = 5 columns lit up; g is differentiable, so continuous on [2, 5] and differentiable on (2, 5); MVT: g′(c) = −3 for some c in (2, 5), which is inside (0, 10); "Tables: try pairs until one gives the rate you need."
> g is differentiable, with the values in this table. Must there be a value c between zero and ten where g prime of c equals negative three? [try it]
> The Mean Value Theorem makes g prime equal to an average rate of change. So look for two values in the table whose average rate of change is negative three. [pause]
> The whole table, from zero to ten, gives seven minus four, over ten. Three tenths. That doesn't help. [pause]
> Try other pairs. From two to five: one minus ten, over three. Negative three. That's the pair. [pause]
> g is differentiable, so it's continuous on the interval from two to five, and differentiable between them.
> By the Mean Value Theorem, g prime of c equals negative three for some c between two and five. And that c is between zero and ten, so the answer is yes. [pause]
> On table problems, choosing the right pair of values is most of the work. Try pairs until one gives the rate you need. [long pause]
