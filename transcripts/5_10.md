# 5.10 Introduction to Optimization Problems

## A fixed amount of fence
**On screen:** A river along the top of the screen (blue water, pale ripples, a sandy bank) above a meadow. A rectangular pen of grass hangs off the river, fenced on three sides with a wooden fence and posts, with a sheep inside. As the sides perpendicular to the river grow and shrink, the side along the river changes to use exactly 200 feet of fence. An area readout changes, and a graph of area against x is traced to the right.
> Here's a pen along a river. We have two hundred feet of fence, and the river side needs none. [pause]
> Make the pen deep and narrow, and the area is small. Make it shallow and wide, and the area is small again. [pause]
> Somewhere in between is the biggest pen. The graph of area against x shows exactly where. [long pause]

## What optimization means
**On screen:** "optimize: make it the best". Beneath, "in math, best means a maximum or a minimum", with three examples: "most area", "least cost", "shortest time". Then: "max or min? the derivative". Last: "situation → function: the hard part", with "differentiable function → we can optimize it" boxed.
> Today we're looking at optimization problems. [pause]
> To optimize something means to make it the best it can be. In everyday life, best could mean cheapest, or fastest, or strongest. [pause]
> In math, best always means a maximum or a minimum: the most area, the least cost, the shortest time. [long pause]
> So how do we find a maximum or a minimum? We already know how: with the derivative. Critical points, sign charts, the candidates test. [pause]
> The hard part is usually getting a function in the first place: turning the situation into a formula. [pause]
> That's the tool calculus gives us. Whenever we can represent a situation as a differentiable function, we can optimize it. [long pause]

## One quantity, one variable
**On screen:** The pen with its sides labeled x, x and y. "Area: A = xy." "Constraint: 2x + y = 200." Then y is replaced: "A(x) = x(200 − 2x)." Then "domain: 0 < x < 100."
> Here's how to build that function for the pen. First, name the quantity you want to make biggest or smallest. Here, the area: x times y. [pause]
> That has two variables. The constraint, two hundred feet of fence, connects them: two x plus y is two hundred. [pause]
> Solve for y: y is two hundred minus two x. Substitute, and the area is a function of one variable: x times two hundred minus two x. [pause]
> Last, the domain. Picture the pen. If x were zero, there'd be no pen at all. If x were one hundred, the two sides would use all two hundred feet, and the river side would have length zero. Anything bigger, and the fence runs out. So x is between zero and one hundred. [long pause]

## Setting up
**On screen:** The problem on top. On the right, a rectangle with its width labeled w along the bottom and its length labeled ℓ up the side. Board: 2w + 2ℓ = 40; 2ℓ = 40 − 2w, ℓ = 20 − w; A(w) = w(20 − w); w = 25: ℓ = 20 − 25 = −5 (no rectangle); "As w → 20 or w → 0, the rectangle flattens to a line."; domain: 0 < w < 20. The rectangle on the right changes shape as w changes: wide and thin as w nears 20, tall and thin as w nears 0. At "w = 25", a red note beside it: "ℓ = −5? No such rectangle."
> A rectangle has perimeter forty. Write its area as a function of its width w, and give the domain. [pause]
> Draw it. Call the width w and the length l. Going around, the perimeter is w plus l plus w plus l: two w plus two l equals forty. [pause]
> Solve for l: two l is forty minus two w, so l is twenty minus w.
> The area is width times length: w times twenty minus w. [pause]
> Now the domain. Think about the actual rectangle. What if w were twenty-five? Then l would be twenty minus twenty-five: negative five. A side can't be negative five feet long. That rectangle can't exist. [pause]
> Make w close to twenty, and the rectangle gets thinner and thinner. At twenty, the length is zero and the rectangle collapses into a line. Make w close to zero, and the same thing happens the other way. [pause]
> So the rectangles that actually exist have w between zero and twenty. [long pause]

## Then it's a Unit 5 problem
**On screen:** "A′(x) = 200 − 4x"; "200 − 4x = 0, 4x = 200, x = 50"; "A″(x) = −4 < 0: maximum"; "y = 200 − 2(50) = 100"; "A(50) = 50 · 100 = 5000 ft²."
> Now it's the same problem as the last few topics: find the absolute maximum of a function on an interval. [pause]
> A prime of x is two hundred minus four x. [pause]
> Set it equal to zero: four x equals two hundred, so x equals fifty. That's inside the domain.
> A double prime is negative four. Negative, so that's a maximum, and since it's the only critical point, it's the absolute maximum. [pause]
> Then y is two hundred minus two times fifty, one hundred. The biggest pen is fifty by one hundred: five thousand square feet. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** Four steps: "1. Name the quantity. 2. Write it with the variables. 3. Use the constraint to get one variable. 4. Find the domain." Then: "Then find the absolute max or min."
> Name the quantity, use the constraint to get one variable, find the domain, and then you're back on familiar ground. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Largest product
**On screen:** "Two numbers add to 20. What is the largest possible product?" Board: P = xy, x + y = 20; y = 20 − x; P(x) = x(20 − x) = 20x − x²; P′(x) = 20 − 2x; 20 − 2x = 0, x = 10; P″ = −2 < 0: maximum; y = 20 − 10 = 10; P = 10 · 10 = 100.
> Two numbers add to twenty. What is the largest possible product? [pause]
> The product is x times y, and the constraint is x plus y equals twenty. Solve for y: y is twenty minus x.
> So the product is x times twenty minus x, which is twenty x minus x squared. [pause]
> Its derivative is twenty minus two x. Set it equal to zero: two x equals twenty, so x equals ten.
> The second derivative is negative two, so that's a maximum. [pause]
> Then y is twenty minus ten, also ten. The largest product is ten times ten, one hundred. [long pause]

## Example 2: The riverside pen
**On screen:** "A rectangular pen along a river uses 200 ft of fence on three sides. What dimensions give the largest area?" Board: A(x) = x(200 − 2x), 0 < x < 100; = 200x − 2x²; A′(x) = 200 − 4x; 200 − 4x = 0, x = 50; A″ = −4 < 0, the only critical point: absolute max; y = 200 − 100 = 100; 50 ft by 100 ft, A = 5000 ft².
> A rectangular pen along a river uses two hundred feet of fence on three sides. What dimensions give the largest area? [pause]
> With x as each side perpendicular to the river, the area is x times two hundred minus two x, for x between zero and one hundred. Multiply out: two hundred x minus two x squared.
> A prime is two hundred minus four x. Set it equal to zero: four x is two hundred, so x is fifty. [pause]
> A double prime is negative four, and it's the only critical point, so it's the absolute maximum.
> The river side is two hundred minus two times fifty, one hundred. The pen is fifty feet by one hundred feet, with an area of five thousand square feet. [long pause]

## Example 3: A number and its reciprocal
**On screen:** "Find the positive number x for which x + 9/x is as small as possible." Board: f′(x) = 1 − 9/x²; 1 − 9/x² = 0, 9/x² = 1, x² = 9, x = ±3; only x = 3 is positive; f″(x) = 18/x³, f″(3) = 18/27 > 0; only critical point on (0, ∞): absolute min; f(3) = 3 + 3 = 6.
> Find the positive number x for which x plus nine over x is as small as possible. [pause]
> The derivative is one minus nine over x squared. [pause]
> Set it equal to zero: nine over x squared is one, so x squared is nine, and x is plus or minus three. Only three is positive.
> The second derivative is eighteen over x cubed. At three, that's eighteen over twenty-seven: positive. [pause]
> It's the only critical point for positive x, so it's the absolute minimum. At three, the sum is three plus three, six. [long pause]
