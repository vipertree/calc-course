# 5.11 Solving Optimization Problems

## Folding a box
**On screen:** On the left, a square cardboard sheet, 12 inches on a side, seen from above; small squares of side x are marked at the corners and cut away. On the right, the same sheet seen at an angle: the four flaps fold up along dashed lines into an open-top box. The inside walls and floor are a darker shade than the outside, and the top rim is outlined, so the box is clearly open. Then, as x changes, both pictures change, and a volume readout rises and falls.
> Start with a square sheet of cardboard, twelve inches on a side. Cut a square from each corner and fold up the sides, and you get an open box. [pause]
> Cut tiny squares, and the box is wide but almost flat. Cut big squares, and it's tall but narrow. [pause]
> Which cut holds the most? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.11 · Solving optimization problems." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## The whole procedure
**On screen:** Five steps: 1. Draw, label, name the quantity. 2. One variable and a domain. 3. Critical points. 4. Justify: absolute max or min. 5. Answer the question, with units.
> Here's the full procedure. Draw a picture and name the quantity to optimize. [pause]
> Write it as a function of one variable, using the constraint, and find the domain. [pause]
> Find the critical points. Then justify that you have the absolute maximum or minimum. [pause]
> And answer the question that was asked, with units. [long pause]

## Justifying the answer
**On screen:** Two cards. "Closed interval: compare the candidates (critical points and endpoints)." "Open interval: one critical point that's a relative extremum, so it's absolute."
> The justification depends on the domain. On a closed interval, compare the values at the critical points and the endpoints. [pause]
> On an open interval, show there's only one critical point and that it's a relative extremum. Then it's the absolute one. [long pause]

## Closest point
**On screen:** The problem on top. On the right, the line y = x + 1, the point (3, 0), and a point (x, x + 1) on the line joined to (3, 0) by a dashed segment labeled D; the point slides along the line and settles at (1, 2). Board: D² = (x − 3)² + (x + 1)²; = x² − 6x + 9 + x² + 2x + 1 = 2x² − 4x + 10; (D²)′ = 4x − 4; 4x − 4 = 0, x = 1; second derivative 4 > 0: minimum; y = 1 + 1 = 2: the point (1, 2); D = √((1 − 3)² + 2²) = √8 = 2√2.
> Find the point on the line y equals x plus one closest to three, zero. [try it]
> Draw it: the line, the point three, zero, and a point x, x plus one on the line. The distance between them is D. [pause]
> Minimizing D is the same as minimizing D squared, which avoids the square root: x minus three squared, plus x plus one squared.
> Expand: x squared minus six x plus nine, plus x squared plus two x plus one. That's two x squared minus four x plus ten. [pause]
> Its derivative is four x minus four. Set it equal to zero: x equals one.
> The second derivative is four, positive, and that's the only critical point, so it's the minimum. [pause]
> Then y is one plus one, two. The closest point is one, two, at a distance of root eight, two root two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The folded box at its best shape, with "x = 2 in, V = 128 in³" beneath it.
> One variable, a domain, critical points, a justification, and units. That's every optimization problem. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: The open box
**On screen:** "An open box is made from a 12 × 12 inch sheet by cutting squares of side x from the corners. What x gives the largest volume, and what is that volume? (Volume = length × width × height.)" On the right, a sketch of the open box with its height labeled x and its base edges labeled 12 − 2x. Board: V(x) = x(12 − 2x)²; domain 0 ≤ x ≤ 6; V′(x) = (12 − 2x)² + x · 2(12 − 2x)(−2); = (12 − 2x)[(12 − 2x) − 4x] = (12 − 2x)(12 − 6x); 12 − 2x = 0 or 12 − 6x = 0, so x = 6 or x = 2; V(0) = 0, V(2) = 2 · 8² = 128, V(6) = 0; x = 2 in, V = 128 in³.
> An open box is made from a twelve by twelve inch sheet by cutting squares of side x from the corners. What x gives the largest volume, and what is that volume? [pause]
> Sketch the box. Its height is x, and each side of the base is twelve minus two x, because a square comes off both ends. So the volume is x times twelve minus two x, squared. [pause]
> For the domain, think about the sheet. At x equals zero there are no flaps: the box is flat. At six, the cuts meet in the middle and there's no base left. So x runs from zero to six.
> Take the derivative with the product rule: twelve minus two x squared, plus x times two times twelve minus two x, times negative two. [pause]
> Factor out twelve minus two x. What's left is twelve minus two x minus four x, which is twelve minus six x.
> Set each factor equal to zero: twelve minus two x is zero at six, and twelve minus six x is zero at two. [pause]
> Compare the candidates: V of zero is zero. V of two is two times eight squared, one hundred twenty-eight. V of six is zero.
> Cut two-inch squares. The box holds one hundred twenty-eight cubic inches. [long pause]

## Example 2: The closest point
**On screen:** "Find the point on y = √x closest to (3, 0)." On the right, the graph of y = √x, the point (3, 0), and a dashed segment D from (3, 0) to the closest point. Board: D² = (x − 3)² + (√x)²; = x² − 6x + 9 + x = x² − 5x + 9; (D²)′ = 2x − 5; 2x − 5 = 0, x = 5/2; second derivative 2 > 0, only critical point: minimum; the point (5/2, √(5/2)); D² = 25/4 − 25/2 + 9 = 11/4, D = √11/2.
> Find the point on y equals root x closest to the point three, zero. [pause]
> A point on the curve is x, root x. Minimize the square of its distance to three, zero: x minus three squared, plus root x squared. [pause]
> Expand: x squared minus six x plus nine, plus x. That's x squared minus five x plus nine.
> Its derivative is two x minus five. Set it equal to zero: x equals five halves. [pause]
> The second derivative is two, positive, and it's the only critical point, so it's the minimum.
> The closest point is five halves, root five halves. D squared there is eleven fourths, so the distance is root eleven over two. [long pause]

## Example 3: The can
**On screen:** "A closed cylindrical can holds 16π in³. What radius and height use the least material? (For a cylinder, V = πr²h and S = 2πr² + 2πrh.)" On the right, a sketch of a cylinder: an ellipse on top with a radius r drawn in, the height h along the side, and the back half of the bottom ellipse dashed. The top and bottom light up for "2πr²", then the side for "2πrh". Board: πr²h = 16π; h = 16/r²; S = 2πr² + 2πrh = 2πr² + 2πr · 16/r² = 2πr² + 32π/r; S′ = 4πr − 32π/r²; 4πr = 32π/r², 4πr³ = 32π, r³ = 8, r = 2; S″ = 4π + 64π/r³ > 0, only critical point: minimum; h = 16/2² = 4; r = 2 in, h = 4 in.
> A closed cylindrical can holds sixteen pi cubic inches. What radius and height use the least material? [pause]
> Sketch the can: radius r, height h. The volume is pi r squared h, and it has to be sixteen pi. [pause]
> Divide both sides by pi r squared: h equals sixteen over r squared.
> The material is the surface area: the top and bottom circles, two pi r squared, plus the side, two pi r h. Substitute for h: two pi r squared plus thirty-two pi over r. [pause]
> S prime is four pi r minus thirty-two pi over r squared. Set it equal to zero and multiply both sides by r squared: four pi r cubed equals thirty-two pi, so r cubed is eight, and r is two.
> S double prime is four pi plus sixty-four pi over r cubed, positive for every positive r. It's the only critical point, so it's the minimum. [pause]
> Then h is sixteen over four, four. The can has radius two inches and height four inches. [long pause]
