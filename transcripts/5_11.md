# 5.11 Solving Optimization Problems

## Folding a box
**On screen:** A square sheet, 12 inches on a side. Small squares of side x are cut from the corners and the flaps fold up into an open box. As x changes, the box changes shape, and a volume readout rises and falls.
> Start with a square sheet, twelve inches on a side. Cut a square from each corner and fold up the sides, and you get an open box. [pause]
> Cut tiny squares, and the box is wide but almost flat. Cut big squares, and it's tall but narrow. [pause]
> Which cut holds the most? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.11 · Solving optimization problems." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## The whole procedure
**On screen:** Five steps: 1. Draw, label, name the quantity. 2. One variable and a domain. 3. Critical points. 4. Justify: absolute max or min. 5. Answer the question, with units.
> Here's the full procedure. Draw a picture and name the quantity to optimize. [pause]
> Write it as a function of one variable, using the constraint, and find the domain. [pause]
> Find the critical points. Then justify that you have the absolute maximum or minimum. [pause]
> And answer the question that was asked, with units. [long pause]

## Justifying the answer
**On screen:** Two cards. "Closed interval: compare the candidates (critical points and endpoints)." "Open interval: one critical point that's a relative extremum, so it's absolute."
> The justification depends on the domain. On a closed interval, compare the values at the critical points and the endpoints. [pause]
> On an open interval, show there's only one critical point and that it's a relative extremum. Then it's the absolute one. [long pause]

## Closest point
**On screen:** D² written in one variable, its derivative set to 0, and the closest point.
> Find the point on the line y equals x plus one closest to three, zero. [try it]
> Minimize the square of the distance: x minus three squared, plus x plus one squared.
> Its derivative is two times x minus three plus two times x plus one: four x minus four. That's zero at one. [pause]
> The point is one, two, at a distance of two root two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The folded box at its best shape, with "x = 2 in, V = 128 in³" beneath it.
> One variable, a domain, critical points, a justification, and units. That's every optimization problem. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: The open box
**On screen:** "An open box is made from a 12 × 12 inch sheet by cutting squares of side x from the corners. What x gives the largest volume, and what is that volume?" Work: V(x) = x(12 − 2x)², 0 ≤ x ≤ 6; V′(x) = (12 − 2x)(12 − 6x) = 0 at x = 2 (x = 6 is an endpoint); V(0) = 0, V(2) = 128, V(6) = 0; x = 2 in, V = 128 in³.
> An open box is made from a twelve by twelve inch sheet by cutting squares of side x from the corners. What x gives the largest volume, and what is that volume? [pause]
> The base is twelve minus two x on each side, and the height is x. So the volume is x times twelve minus two x, squared. x runs from zero to six.
> The derivative factors as twelve minus two x, times twelve minus six x. That's zero at two and at six. [pause]
> Compare the candidates: zero at x equals zero, one hundred twenty-eight at two, and zero at six.
> Cut two-inch squares. The box holds one hundred twenty-eight cubic inches.

## Example 2: The closest point
**On screen:** "Find the point on y = √x closest to (3, 0)." Work: D² = (x − 3)² + (√x)² = x² − 5x + 9; d/dx D² = 2x − 5 = 0 at x = 5/2; second derivative 2 > 0, only critical point: minimum; the point is (5/2, √(5/2)), distance √11/2.
> Find the point on y equals root x closest to the point three, zero. [pause]
> Minimize the square of the distance: x minus three squared, plus root x squared. That simplifies to x squared minus five x plus nine.
> Its derivative is two x minus five, zero at five halves. [pause]
> The second derivative is two, positive, and it's the only critical point, so it's the minimum.
> The closest point is five halves, root five halves. Its distance is root eleven over two.

## Example 3: The can
**On screen:** "A closed cylindrical can holds 16π in³. What radius and height use the least material?" Work: πr²h = 16π, so h = 16/r²; S = 2πr² + 2πrh = 2πr² + 32π/r; S′ = 4πr − 32π/r² = 0 at r³ = 8, r = 2; S″ > 0, only critical point: minimum; r = 2 in, h = 4 in.
> A closed cylindrical can holds sixteen pi cubic inches. What radius and height use the least material? [pause]
> The volume constraint gives h equals sixteen over r squared.
> The surface area is two pi r squared plus two pi r h, which becomes two pi r squared plus thirty-two pi over r. [pause]
> S prime is four pi r minus thirty-two pi over r squared, zero when r cubed is eight: r equals two.
> S double prime is positive and that's the only critical point, so it's the minimum. The can has radius two inches and height four inches. [long pause]
