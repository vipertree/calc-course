## Absolute and relative
**On screen:** The graph of a function on a closed interval. The highest point is labeled "absolute max," the lowest "absolute min." A lower peak is labeled "relative max" and a valley "relative min."
> The absolute maximum is the highest value on the whole interval. The absolute minimum is the lowest. [pause]
> A relative maximum is higher than everything near it, like the top of one hill. A relative minimum is lower than everything near it. [pause]
> An endpoint can be an absolute extremum, as this one is. [long pause]

## A little Latin
**On screen:** A two-column table, "one" and "two or more": maximum / maxima, minimum / minima, extremum / extrema, each row appearing as it is said. "extremum = a max or a min" beside the last row. Beneath: "In English, also: maximums, minimums, extreme values."
> A quick word about the words. They come from Latin. [pause]
> One maximum, two maxima. One minimum, two minima. [pause]
> An extremum is either one: a maximum or a minimum. The plural is extrema, so the extrema of a function are all its maxes and mins together. [pause]
> In everyday English you'll also hear maximums, minimums, or extreme values. Those mean the same thing. Textbooks and the AP exam often use the Latin forms, so it's good to recognize them. [long pause]

## The Extreme Value Theorem
**On screen:** The Extreme Value Theorem in a box: if f is continuous on [a, b], then f has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on [a, b]. Then two failures side by side: y = x on the open interval (0, 1), climbing toward an open circle; y = 1/x on [−1, 1], shooting off to infinity.
> If f is continuous on a closed interval, it has an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. That's the Extreme Value Theorem. [pause]
> Both conditions matter. On an open interval, y equals x creeps toward one but never gets there: no maximum. [pause]
> And one over x on negative one to one isn't continuous. It shoots off to infinity: no maximum. [long pause]

## Critical points
**On screen:** Three peaks side by side: a smooth hilltop with a horizontal tangent (f′ = 0), a sharp corner (f′ undefined), and the graph of x³ at 0 with a horizontal tangent but no peak.
> Where can a relative extremum be? At a smooth peak, the tangent line is horizontal: f prime is zero. [pause]
> At a sharp peak, there's no tangent line at all: f prime is undefined. [pause]
> Points where f prime is zero or undefined are called critical points. [pause]
> But be careful: x cubed has a horizontal tangent at zero and keeps on rising. A critical point is only a candidate. [long pause]

## Find the critical points
**On screen:** f′(x) = 4x³ − 16x; = 4x(x² − 4); = 4x(x − 2)(x + 2); 4x = 0 or x − 2 = 0 or x + 2 = 0; x = 0, 2, −2; f′ is never undefined.
> Find the critical points of x to the fourth minus eight x squared. [try it]
> The derivative is four x cubed minus sixteen x. [pause]
> Factor out four x: four x, times x squared minus four.
> And x squared minus four is a difference of squares: x minus two, times x plus two. [pause]
> Set each factor equal to zero. Four x is zero at x equals zero. x minus two is zero at two. x plus two is zero at negative two. [pause]
> And a polynomial's derivative is never undefined, so those three are all the critical points. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Critical point: f′(c) = 0 or f′(c) undefined, with c in the domain of f." Beneath: "Every relative extremum is at a critical point. Not every critical point is an extremum."
> Every relative extremum happens at a critical point. But not every critical point is an extremum. The next topics show how to tell. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A cubic
**On screen:** "Find the critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x²." Work: f′(x) = 3x² − 6x; = 3x(x − 2); 3x = 0 or x − 2 = 0; x = 0 or x = 2; f′ is never undefined, so the critical points are x = 0 and x = 2.
> Find the critical points of f of x equals x cubed minus three x squared. [pause]
> f prime of x is three x squared minus six x. [pause]
> Factor out three x: three x, times x minus two.
> Set each factor equal to zero. Three x equals zero gives x equals zero. x minus two equals zero gives x equals two. [pause]
> f prime is a polynomial, so it's never undefined. The critical points are zero and two.

## Example 2: A cusp
**On screen:** "Find the critical points of f(x) = x^(2/3)(x − 5), given f′(x) = 5(x − 2)/(3x^(1/3))." Work: f′(x) = 0 at x = 2; f′ is undefined at x = 0, and f(0) = 0 is defined; critical points x = 0 and x = 2.
> Find the critical points of x to the two thirds times x minus five. Its derivative is five times x minus two, over three times the cube root of x. [pause]
> The derivative is zero when the top is zero: x equals two.
> It's undefined when the bottom is zero: x equals zero. And f of zero is defined, so zero counts. [pause]
> Two critical points: zero and two.

## Example 3: Is a maximum guaranteed?
**On screen:** "Does the Extreme Value Theorem guarantee an absolute maximum for f(x) = 1/x² on [−1, 1]? On [1, 3]?" Work: on [−1, 1], f is not continuous at 0: no guarantee (in fact no maximum); on [1, 3], f is continuous on a closed interval: yes.
> Does the Extreme Value Theorem guarantee an absolute maximum for one over x squared, on negative one to one? On one to three? [pause]
> On negative one to one, f isn't continuous at zero. No guarantee, and in fact the graph shoots up forever.
> On one to three, f is continuous on a closed interval. Yes: the maximum is guaranteed. [long pause]
