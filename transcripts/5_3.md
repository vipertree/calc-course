# 5.3 Determining Intervals on Which a Function Is Increasing or Decreasing

## Uphill and downhill
**On screen:** The graph of a wavy function. A small tangent line rides along the curve, colored green where it slopes up and red where it slopes down. Under the graph, the same stretches are marked "increasing" and "decreasing."
> Ride a tangent line along this curve. [pause]
> Where the graph goes uphill, the tangent slopes up: f prime is positive. Where it goes downhill, f prime is negative. [pause]
> So the sign of the derivative tells you the direction of the function. [long pause]

## Title

## f and f prime together
**On screen:** Two stacked graphs on the same x-axis: the wavy curve y = f(x) on top, y = f′(x) underneath. A tangent line rides along f; below it, a dot moves along the graph of f′ at the height of the tangent's slope. Dashed vertical lines connect each peak and valley of f to a zero of f′. Stretches where f′ > 0 are green on both graphs, stretches where f′ < 0 are red.
> Here's a curve on top, and its derivative underneath, on the same x-axis. [pause]
> As the tangent line rides along f, the dot below tracks its slope. The height of the dot is f prime. [pause]
> Where f climbs, the dot is above the axis. Where f falls, the dot is below. [pause]
> At each peak and valley of f, the tangent is flat, so f prime crosses zero. [long pause]

## Height is not slope
**On screen:** The stacked graphs stay. At x = 2, a dashed line through both graphs: on top "f > 0" (above the axis), below "f′ < 0". At x = 4.2, another: on top "f < 0", below "f′ > 0". Then: "sign of f: where the graph is. sign of f′: which way it's going."
> Don't mix up the height of f with its slope. [pause]
> Here, f is above the axis, so f is positive. But it's going downhill, so f prime is negative. [pause]
> And here, f is below the axis, negative. But it's climbing, so f prime is positive. [pause]
> The sign of f tells you where the graph is. The sign of f prime tells you which way it's going. [long pause]

## The rule
**On screen:** Two lines: "f′(x) > 0 on an interval ⇒ f is increasing there." "f′(x) < 0 on an interval ⇒ f is decreasing there."
> If f prime is positive on an interval, f is increasing there. [pause]
> If f prime is negative on an interval, f is decreasing there. It's the sign that matters, not the size. [long pause]

## A sign chart
**On screen:** f′(x) = 3x² − 12 = 3(x − 2)(x + 2). A number line with −2 and 2 marked. Test values −3, 0 and 3 are plugged in, and the signs +, −, + appear above the pieces, with "inc", "dec", "inc" beneath.
> Here's how to find the intervals without a graph. Take f prime of x equals three x squared minus twelve. [pause]
> It's zero at negative two and two. Those critical points cut the number line into three pieces. [pause]
> In between, f prime can't change sign, so one test value per piece is enough. Try negative three: positive. Zero: negative. Three: positive. [pause]
> So f is increasing, then decreasing, then increasing again. [long pause]

## Increasing from a sign chart
**On screen:** The problem on top. Board: "Increasing means f′(x) > 0. When is f′(x) > 0?"; f′(x) = 3x² − 6x; = 3x(x − 2); 3x = 0 or x − 2 = 0, so x = 0 or x = 2; test values −1, 1, 3; f′(−1) = 3 + 6 = 9 > 0; f′(1) = 3 − 6 = −3 < 0; f′(3) = 27 − 18 = 9 > 0; increasing on (−∞, 0) and (2, ∞). On the right, a number line with 0 and 2 marked; each sign appears over its piece as it is tested, then "inc", "dec", "inc" beneath.
> Where is x cubed minus three x squared plus four increasing? [try it]
> Increasing means f prime is positive. So the real question is: when is f prime of x greater than zero? [pause]
> Take the derivative: three x squared minus six x. [pause]
> Factor out three x: three x, times x minus two.
> f prime is zero when three x is zero or x minus two is zero: at x equals zero and x equals two. Those are the critical points. [pause]
> Put them on a number line. They cut it into three pieces: left of zero, between zero and two, and right of two. [pause]
> f prime can only change sign at a critical point, so one test value tells us the sign on a whole piece. Pick easy numbers: negative one, one, and three. [pause]
> f prime of negative one: three plus six, nine. Positive.
> f prime of one: three minus six, negative three. Negative.
> f prime of three: twenty-seven minus eighteen, nine. Positive. [pause]
> f prime is positive left of zero and right of two. So f is increasing on negative infinity to zero, and on two to infinity. [long pause]

## From the graph of f prime
**On screen:** "The graph of f′ on [−4, 6] is shown: line segments and a semicircle. Where is f increasing? Decreasing? Where does f change direction?" The figure: a segment from (−4, 2) down to (−2, 0), a semicircle below the axis from (−2, 0) to (2, 0) with its bottom at (0, −2), a segment up to (4, 2), then flat to (6, 2). Each piece turns green (f′ > 0) or red (f′ < 0) as it is read. Board: read the sign of f′; (−4, −2): f′ > 0, so f increasing; (−2, 2): f′ < 0, so f decreasing; (2, 6): f′ > 0, so f increasing; relative max at x = −2, relative min at x = 2; "f′ decreasing on (−4, −2) but still positive: f still increasing."
> The graph of f prime is shown. It's made of line segments and a semicircle. Where is f increasing? Where is it decreasing? Where does f change direction? [pause]
> This is the graph of f prime, not f. So read its sign: above the axis or below. [pause]
> From negative four to negative two, f prime is above the axis. Positive, so f is increasing. [pause]
> From negative two to two, the semicircle dips below the axis. f prime is negative, so f is decreasing. [pause]
> From two to six, f prime is above the axis again, so f is increasing. [pause]
> At negative two, f switches from increasing to decreasing: a relative maximum. At two, it switches from decreasing to increasing: a relative minimum. [pause]
> One trap. From negative four to negative two, the graph of f prime is heading down. But it's still above the axis, so f is still increasing. What matters is the sign of f prime, not which way f prime is moving. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A cubic
**On screen:** "On what intervals is f(x) = x³ − 12x + 1 increasing? Decreasing?" Board: f′(x) = 3x² − 12; = 3(x² − 4) = 3(x − 2)(x + 2); x = 2 or x = −2; f′(−3) = 27 − 12 = 15 > 0; f′(0) = −12 < 0; f′(3) = 15 > 0; increasing on (−∞, −2) and (2, ∞), decreasing on (−2, 2). On the right, a number line with −2 and 2; signs +, −, + appear as tested, then inc, dec, inc.
> On what intervals is f of x equals x cubed minus twelve x plus one increasing? Decreasing? [pause]
> f prime is three x squared minus twelve. [pause]
> Factor out the three: three times x squared minus four. That's a difference of squares: three times x minus two times x plus two.
> So f prime is zero at x equals two and x equals negative two. Mark them on the number line. [pause]
> Test negative three: twenty-seven minus twelve, fifteen. Positive.
> Test zero: negative twelve. Negative.
> Test three: fifteen again. Positive. [pause]
> So f is increasing on negative infinity to negative two and on two to infinity, and decreasing on negative two to two. [long pause]

## Example 2: An exponential
**On screen:** "On what intervals is f(x) = xe^(−x) increasing? Decreasing?" Board: f′(x) = e^(−x) − xe^(−x); = (1 − x)e^(−x); e^(−x) > 0 always, so the sign of f′ is the sign of 1 − x; 1 − x = 0 at x = 1; f′(0) = 1 > 0; f′(2) = −e^(−2) < 0; increasing on (−∞, 1), decreasing on (1, ∞). On the right, a number line with 1 marked; signs +, − appear as tested, then inc, dec.
> On what intervals is x times e to the negative x increasing? Decreasing? [pause]
> Use the product rule: e to the negative x, minus x e to the negative x. [pause]
> Both terms share e to the negative x. Factor it out: one minus x, times e to the negative x.
> e to the negative x is always positive, so the sign of f prime is the sign of one minus x. That's zero at x equals one. [pause]
> Test zero: f prime is one. Positive.
> Test two: one minus two is negative one, times a positive number. Negative. [pause]
> f is increasing for x less than one and decreasing for x greater than one. [long pause]

## Example 3: A repeated factor
**On screen:** "f′(x) = (x + 1)(x − 3)². On what intervals is f increasing?" Board: x + 1 = 0 or x − 3 = 0, so x = −1 or x = 3; f′(−2) = (−1)(25) = −25 < 0; f′(0) = (1)(9) = 9 > 0; f′(4) = (5)(1) = 5 > 0; no sign change at 3: (x − 3)² is never negative; increasing on (−1, 3) and (3, ∞). On the right, a number line with −1 and 3; signs −, +, + appear as tested, then dec, inc, inc.
> f prime of x is x plus one, times x minus three squared. On what intervals is f increasing? [pause]
> f prime is zero when x plus one is zero or x minus three is zero: x equals negative one or three. [pause]
> Test negative two: negative one times twenty-five. Negative.
> Test zero: one times nine. Positive.
> Test four: five times one. Positive. [pause]
> The sign doesn't change at three, because x minus three squared is never negative.
> So f is increasing on negative one to three, and on three to infinity. [long pause]

## Example 4: From the graph of f
**On screen:** "The graph of f on [0, 4] is shown. On what intervals is f′ negative? Is f′(2) positive or negative?" The figure: f(x) = (x³ − 6x² + 9x)/2 + 1, above the axis everywhere, with a peak at (1, 3) and a valley at (3, 1). The falling stretch from 1 to 3 turns red. Board: f is decreasing on (1, 3), so f′ < 0 on (1, 3); f(2) = 2 > 0, but f is falling at x = 2, so f′(2) < 0.
> The graph of f is shown. On what intervals is f prime negative? And is f prime of two positive or negative? [pause]
> f prime is negative where f is decreasing. The graph falls from its peak at one to its valley at three. So f prime is negative on one to three. [pause]
> At x equals two, the graph is above the axis: f of two is two, positive. [pause]
> But the graph is going downhill there, so f prime of two is negative. The height of f doesn't decide the sign of f prime. Its direction does. [long pause]
