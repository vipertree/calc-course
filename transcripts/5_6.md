# 5.6 Determining Concavity of Functions over Their Domains

## Bowls and hills
**On screen:** Two arcs side by side: one bending up like a bowl, one bending down like a hill. On each, a tangent line slides from left to right while a readout shows its slope: rising on the bowl, falling on the hill.
> Two curves, both smooth. One bends upward like a bowl. The other bends downward like a hill. [pause]
> Slide a tangent line across the bowl, and watch its slope: it keeps increasing. [pause]
> Across the hill, the slope keeps decreasing. [pause]
> So the derivative tells us which way the graph bends. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.6 · Determining concavity of functions over their domains." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## Why up and down
**On screen:** One hill-shaped arc, drawn thin. An eye below it, with the label "from below: caved in, concave". An eye above it, with "from above: bulging out, convex". Then both eye labels fade, an arrow points at the open side underneath, and the label "opens downward: concave down" appears. Beside it, a bowl with an arrow at its open side on top: "opens upward: concave up".
> A word about the words. In everyday English, concave means caved in, like the inside of a bowl. Convex means bulging out, like the outside of one. [pause]
> But a graph is just a thin line. Look at this hill from underneath, and it's caved in: concave. Look at it from above, and it bulges toward you: convex. Same curve. [pause]
> So calculus never just says concave. It says which way the curve opens. This hill opens downward, so it's concave down. [pause]
> A bowl opens upward: concave up. Up or down tells you exactly which side the hollow is on. [long pause]

## Concavity and the second derivative
**On screen:** "Concave up: f′ increasing, f″ > 0. The graph lies above its tangent lines." "Concave down: f′ decreasing, f″ < 0. The graph lies below its tangent lines."
> When the slopes increase, the graph is concave up. Slopes increasing means f prime is increasing, so f double prime is positive. [pause]
> When the slopes decrease, the graph is concave down, and f double prime is negative. [pause]
> Remember the tangent lines from linearization: a concave up graph sits above its tangent lines, and a concave down graph sits below them. [long pause]

## Points of inflection
**On screen:** The graph of y = x³ − 3x² + 1 with its hill part and bowl part colored differently, meeting at the point (1, −1), which is marked "point of inflection." Below, the sign chart of f″(x) = 6x − 6: −, then +.
> Where a graph switches from one kind of bend to the other, it has a point of inflection. [pause]
> Here, f double prime is six x minus six. It's negative before one and positive after. So the concavity changes at x equals one. [pause]
> The test is a sign change of f double prime, not just f double prime equal to zero. x to the fourth has f double prime zero at the origin, but it's a bowl on both sides. [long pause]

## Concavity of a cubic
**On screen:** The problem on top. Board: f′(x) = 3x² + 6x; f″(x) = 6x + 6; 6x + 6 = 0, 6x = −6, x = −1; f″(−2) = −12 + 6 = −6 < 0; f″(0) = 6 > 0; concave down on (−∞, −1), concave up on (−1, ∞); f(−1) = −1 + 3 = 2: inflection point (−1, 2). On the right, a number line for f″ with −1 marked; signs −, + appear as tested, "down, up" beneath, then "inflection" over −1.
> Where is x cubed plus three x squared concave up? Find any points of inflection. [try it]
> f prime is three x squared plus six x. [pause]
> Differentiate again: f double prime is six x plus six.
> Set f double prime equal to zero: six x plus six equals zero, so six x equals negative six, and x equals negative one. [pause]
> Mark negative one on a number line for f double prime, and test each side. f double prime of negative two: negative twelve plus six, negative six. Negative.
> f double prime of zero: six. Positive. [pause]
> So the graph is concave down for x less than negative one, and concave up for x greater than negative one.
> f double prime changes sign at negative one, so there's a point of inflection there. f of negative one is negative one plus three, two. The point is negative one, two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A bowl labeled "f″ > 0," a hill labeled "f″ < 0," and between them a point labeled "f″ changes sign: inflection."
> The sign of f double prime tells which way the graph bends. Where it changes sign, there's a point of inflection. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A cubic
**On screen:** "Where is f(x) = x³ − 6x² + 5 concave up? Concave down? Find any points of inflection." Board: f′(x) = 3x² − 12x; f″(x) = 6x − 12; 6x − 12 = 0, so x = 2; f″(0) = −12 < 0; f″(3) = 18 − 12 = 6 > 0; concave down on (−∞, 2); concave up on (2, ∞); f(2) = 8 − 24 + 5 = −11: inflection point (2, −11). On the right, a number line for f″ with 2 marked; signs −, + appear as tested, "down, up" beneath, then "inflection" over 2.
> Where is x cubed minus six x squared plus five concave up? Concave down? Find any points of inflection. [pause]
> f prime is three x squared minus twelve x. [pause]
> f double prime is six x minus twelve.
> Set it equal to zero: six x equals twelve, so x equals two. [pause]
> Test zero: f double prime is negative twelve. Negative.
> Test three: eighteen minus twelve, six. Positive. [pause]
> So the graph is concave down on negative infinity to two, and concave up on two to infinity.
> The concavity changes at x equals two. f of two is eight minus twenty-four plus five, negative eleven. The point of inflection is two, negative eleven. [long pause]

## Example 2: Two candidates
**On screen:** "Find the points of inflection of f(x) = x⁴ − 4x³." Board: f′(x) = 4x³ − 12x²; f″(x) = 12x² − 24x; = 12x(x − 2); x = 0 or x = 2; f″(−1) = 12 + 24 = 36 > 0; f″(1) = 12 − 24 = −12 < 0; f″(3) = 108 − 72 = 36 > 0; f(0) = 0, f(2) = 16 − 32 = −16; inflection points (0, 0) and (2, −16). On the right, a number line for f″ with 0 and 2; signs +, −, + appear as tested, "up, down, up" beneath, then "inflection" over both points.
> Find the points of inflection of x to the fourth minus four x cubed. [pause]
> f prime is four x cubed minus twelve x squared. [pause]
> f double prime is twelve x squared minus twenty-four x.
> Factor out twelve x: twelve x, times x minus two. That's zero at x equals zero and x equals two. [pause]
> Test negative one: twelve plus twenty-four, thirty-six. Positive.
> Test one: twelve minus twenty-four, negative twelve. Negative.
> Test three: one hundred eight minus seventy-two, thirty-six. Positive. [pause]
> f double prime changes sign at both zero and two. f of zero is zero, and f of two is sixteen minus thirty-two, negative sixteen. The points of inflection are zero, zero and two, negative sixteen. [long pause]

## Example 3: An exponential
**On screen:** "Where is f(x) = xe^x concave down?" Board: f′(x) = e^x + xe^x = (x + 1)e^x; f″(x) = e^x + (x + 1)e^x = (x + 2)e^x; e^x > 0, so f″ has the sign of x + 2; x + 2 = 0 at x = −2; f″(−3) = (−1)e^(−3) < 0; f″(0) = 2 > 0; concave down on (−∞, −2). On the right, a number line for f″ with −2 marked; signs −, + appear as tested, "down, up" beneath.
> Where is x times e to the x concave down? [pause]
> The product rule: f prime is e to the x plus x e to the x. Factor: x plus one, times e to the x. [pause]
> The product rule again: f double prime is e to the x plus x plus one times e to the x. That's x plus two, times e to the x.
> e to the x is always positive, so f double prime has the sign of x plus two. That's zero at negative two. [pause]
> Test negative three: negative one times a positive number. Negative.
> Test zero: two. Positive. [pause]
> The graph is concave down on negative infinity to negative two. [long pause]
