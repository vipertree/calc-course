# 5.8 Sketching Graphs of Functions and Their Derivatives

## Two graphs, one story
**On screen:** Two stacked graphs with the same x-axis: f on top, a wavy curve with a peak and a valley; f′ underneath, starting empty. A tangent line rides along f, and as it moves, a pencil draws f′ below at the height of the tangent's slope.
> On top is a function f. Below it, we'll draw its derivative, one slope at a time. [pause]
> Where f climbs, the slopes are positive, so the lower graph sits above the axis. Where f falls, it sits below. [pause]
> At the peak and the valley of f, the tangent is flat, so f prime crosses zero. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.8 · Sketching graphs of functions and their derivatives." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## Reading the graph of f prime
**On screen:** The two stacked graphs again. Dashed vertical lines connect matching features: f′ crossing zero ↔ f's peak and valley; f′'s own valley ↔ f's inflection point. Labels appear: "f′ > 0: f rises," "f′ crosses zero: f turns," "f′ rising: f concave up," "valley of f′: inflection of f."
> Every feature of f shows up in f prime, one level down. [pause]
> f prime above the axis: f rises. Below: f falls. Crossing the axis: f turns around. [pause]
> Where f prime is rising, f is concave up. Where it's falling, f is concave down. [pause]
> And where f prime has its own peak or valley, f changes concavity: a point of inflection. [long pause]

## Crossing, not touching
**On screen:** Stacked graphs: f(x) = x³/3 on top, f′(x) = x² below. At x = 0, the graph of f′ touches the axis and bounces back up; a dashed line links it to the flat spot on f. Labels: "f′ = 0, but no sign change" below, "flat for an instant, keeps rising" above. Then: "max or min: f′ must cross (+ to − or − to +)". "inflection: f′ must turn around (f″ changes sign)".
> One caution. f prime equal to zero gives a horizontal tangent, but not always a turn. [pause]
> Here the graph of f prime touches the axis at zero and bounces back up. The sign stays positive on both sides, so f flattens for an instant and keeps rising. [pause]
> A maximum or minimum needs a crossing: f prime going from positive to negative, or from negative to positive. [pause]
> The same goes one level down. An inflection point needs f prime to actually turn around, so f double prime changes sign. Topic 5.9 puts all three levels together. [long pause]

## Reading this graph
**On screen:** The graph of f′(x) = ½(x + 2)(x − 1)(x − 3), its three zeros marked. Board: f′ crosses at −2, 1 and 3; x = −2: − to +, relative min; x = 1: + to −, relative max; x = 3: − to +, relative min. On the graph, each crossing gets its label as it is read.
> Here's the graph of f prime, not f. Where does f have a relative maximum? A relative minimum? [pause]
> Look where f prime crosses the axis: at negative two, one, and three. Check each crossing's direction. [pause]
> At negative two, f prime goes from below the axis to above: negative to positive. A relative minimum of f.
> At one, it goes from above to below: positive to negative. A relative maximum. [pause]
> At three, from below to above again: another relative minimum. [long pause]

## The trap
**On screen:** A single graph labeled "y = f′(x)" with its peak circled and the wrong label "maximum of f?" crossed out, replaced by "inflection point of f."
> The most common mistake is reading a graph of f prime as if it were f. [pause]
> A peak on the graph of f prime is not a maximum of f. It's where f stops getting steeper: an inflection point. Always read the label on the axis. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A two-column table: "graph of f′" and "f": above axis / rises; crosses zero / turns; rising / concave up; peak or valley / inflection.
> The graph of f prime is a graph of slopes. Read it one level down. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Sketching the derivative
**On screen:** "Sketch the graph of f′ for f(x) = x³ − 3x." A graph of f is shown. Work: f′(x) = 3x² − 3; f′ = 0 at x = ±1 (the peak and valley of f); f′ > 0 for |x| > 1 and f′ < 0 for |x| < 1; f′ is a parabola opening up with vertex (0, −3).
> Sketch the graph of f prime for x cubed minus three x. [pause]
> f prime is three x squared minus three.
> Set it equal to zero: three x squared equals three, so x squared equals one, and x is plus or minus one. Those are exactly where f has its peak and its valley. [pause]
> f rises outside those points and falls between them, so f prime is positive outside and negative between.
> The sketch is a parabola opening up, with its vertex at zero, negative three: where f is falling most steeply.

## Example 2: From f prime to f
**On screen:** "The graph of f′(x) = 4 − x² is shown. Describe f: where it increases, its extrema, its concavity." Work: f′ > 0 on (−2, 2): f increasing; f′ < 0 for |x| > 2: decreasing; relative min at x = −2, relative max at x = 2; f′ rising for x < 0: concave up; falling for x > 0: concave down; inflection at x = 0.
> The graph of f prime of x equals four minus x squared is shown. Describe f: where it increases, its extrema, and its concavity. [pause]
> f prime is above the axis between negative two and two, so f is increasing there, and decreasing outside.
> f prime goes from negative to positive at negative two: a relative minimum. From positive to negative at two: a relative maximum. [pause]
> f prime rises until zero and then falls. So f is concave up for x less than zero and concave down after.
> f prime peaks at zero, so f has a point of inflection there.

## Example 3: From conditions to a sketch
**On screen:** "Sketch a continuous f with f(0) = 1, f′(x) < 0 for x < 0, f′(x) > 0 for x > 0, and f″(x) > 0 for all x." Work: f decreases then increases: relative (and absolute) min at (0, 1); concave up everywhere: a bowl shape with its lowest point at (0, 1).
> Sketch a continuous function with f of zero equal to one, f prime negative for x less than zero, positive for x greater than zero, and f double prime positive everywhere. [pause]
> f falls, then rises, with the turn at zero. So the point zero, one is the minimum.
> f double prime is positive everywhere, so the graph is a bowl the whole way. [pause]
> The sketch is a bowl with its lowest point at zero, one. [long pause]

## Example 4: Lines and a semicircle
**On screen:** "The graph of f′ on [−4, 6] consists of three line segments and a semicircle. Where is f increasing? Where does f have relative extrema? Where is the graph of f concave up? Find its inflection points." The figure: a segment from (−4, −2) up to (−2, 2), a segment down to (0, 0), a semicircle below the axis from (0, 0) to (4, 0) with its bottom at (2, −2), and a segment up to (6, 3). Board: f′ > 0 on (−3, 0) and (4, 6): f increasing; x = −3: − to +, rel. min; x = 0: + to −, rel. max; x = 4: − to +, rel. min; f′ increasing on (−4, −2) and (2, 6): concave up; f′ decreasing on (−2, 2): concave down; inflection points at x = −2 and x = 2.
> The graph of f prime is made of three line segments and a semicircle. Where is f increasing? Where are its relative extrema? Where is the graph of f concave up, and where are its inflection points? [try it]
> Start with the sign of f prime. It's above the axis from negative three to zero, and from four to six. So f is increasing there. [pause]
> Now the crossings. At negative three, f prime goes from negative to positive: a relative minimum. At zero, positive to negative: a relative maximum. At four, negative to positive: another relative minimum. [pause]
> For concavity, look at whether f prime is rising or falling, not its sign. It rises from negative four to negative two, and again from two to six, along the bottom of the semicircle and up the last segment. So f is concave up there. [pause]
> It falls from negative two to two: down the segment and down into the semicircle. So f is concave down there. [pause]
> f prime switches between rising and falling at negative two and at two. Those are the inflection points of f. [long pause]

## Example 5: Sketching from scratch
**On screen:** "Sketch the graph of f(x) = x³ − 6x² + 9x by hand. Find its intercepts, relative extrema, concavity and inflection point." On the right, empty axes, with number lines for f′ and f″ beneath. Board: f(x) = x(x − 3)², zero at x = 0, 3; f′(x) = 3x² − 12x + 9 = 3(x − 1)(x − 3); f′(0) = 9 > 0, f′(2) = −3 < 0, f′(4) = 9 > 0; rel. max (1, 4), rel. min (3, 0); f″(x) = 6x − 12: negative before 2, positive after; inflection point (2, 2); plot (0, 0), (1, 4), (2, 2), (3, 0); connect. The number lines fill in as the signs are found; the points appear on the axes; then the curve is drawn through them.
> Sketch the graph of x cubed minus six x squared plus nine x by hand. Find its intercepts, relative extrema, concavity, and inflection point. [pause]
> Start with the intercepts. Factor out x: x times x squared minus six x plus nine, which is x times x minus three squared. So f is zero at zero and at three. [pause]
> f prime is three x squared minus twelve x plus nine. Factor out three: three times x minus one, times x minus three. It's zero at one and three.
> Test zero, two, and four. f prime of zero is nine, positive. f prime of two is twelve minus twenty-four plus nine, negative three. f prime of four is nine, positive. [pause]
> So f has a relative maximum at one, where f of one is four, and a relative minimum at three, where f of three is zero. [pause]
> f double prime is six x minus twelve. It's zero at two, negative before, positive after. [pause]
> So the graph is concave down until two and concave up after, with an inflection point at two, where f of two is two. [pause]
> Now plot the points: zero, zero; the peak at one, four; the inflection point at two, two; and the valley at three, zero. [pause]
> Connect them: rising and bending down to the peak, falling through the inflection point, then bending up out of the valley and rising again. [long pause]
