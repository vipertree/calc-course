# 5.9 Connecting a Function, Its First Derivative, and Its Second Derivative

## Three graphs, one function
**On screen:** Three stacked graphs sharing an x-axis: f on top, f′ in the middle, f″ at the bottom. A vertical line sweeps across all three, and a readout shows the sign of each at the line's position.
> Here are three graphs stacked up: a function, its derivative, and its second derivative. [pause]
> Each one describes the one above it. f double prime tells how f prime is changing, and f prime tells how f is changing. [pause]
> Sweep across, and the signs below explain the shape above. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.9 · Connecting a function, its first derivative, and its second derivative." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## The chart
**On screen:** A three-column chart builds one group at a time, with a tinted header row: "Function, f" | "First derivative, f′" | "Second derivative, f″". Group 1, f only: positive; zero; negative. Group 2: increasing | positive; decreasing | negative; local max or min | zero, and changes sign; horizontal tangent | zero. Group 3: concave up | increasing | positive; concave down | decreasing | negative; inflection point | local max or min | zero, and changes sign. Rows are shaded alternately, with rules between groups. At the end, matching cells light up in pairs: "increasing" in the f column of group 2 with "increasing" in the f′ column of group 3; "positive" in the f′ column with "positive" in the f″ column; and so on.
> Here's the whole story in one chart. Three columns: the function, its first derivative, and its second derivative. [pause]
> The first rows are about f itself: positive, zero, or negative. That's just the height of the graph, and the derivatives can't tell you that. [pause]
> Next, direction. f is increasing where f prime is positive, and decreasing where f prime is negative. [pause]
> Where f prime is zero, the tangent is horizontal. Local maxima and minima live there, but only where f prime changes sign. [pause]
> Then the bend. f is concave up where f prime is increasing, which means f double prime is positive. Concave down: f prime decreasing, f double prime negative. [pause]
> And an inflection point of f is a local max or min of f prime: f double prime is zero and changes sign there. [pause]
> Notice the pattern. Each column is the derivative of the one before it, so the words slide one column to the right. Increasing for f becomes increasing for f prime one group down, and positive for f prime becomes positive for f double prime. [long pause]

## Zero is not enough
**On screen:** Four small number lines in a grid. Top row, for f′: "+ | −" at c, labeled "zero and changes sign: a max"; "+ | +" at c, labeled "zero, no change: just a flat spot". Bottom row, for f″: "− | +" labeled "zero and changes sign: inflection"; "+ | +" labeled "zero, no change: no inflection (like x⁴)".
> One thing to say twice, because it's where points get lost. A zero in the f prime column only gives a horizontal tangent. [pause]
> For a max or min, f prime has to change sign: plus to minus, or minus to plus. If it stays the same sign, f just flattens for a moment. [pause]
> The same goes for f double prime. A zero is only a candidate for an inflection point. It has to change sign. x to the fourth has f double prime zero at the origin, and it's a bowl on both sides. [long pause]

## Justify at the right level
**On screen:** "f is concave up on (3, ∞) because f″ > 0 there" (checked). "f is concave up because it curves upward" (crossed out).
> When you justify, give the reason from the level that decides it. [pause]
> f is concave up because f double prime is positive. Saying the graph curves up just restates the claim. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** The stacked graphs again, with "f′: direction and extrema" next to the middle graph and "f″: concavity and inflection" next to the bottom one.
> Direction and extrema come from f prime. Concavity and inflection come from f double prime. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: All the features
**On screen:** "f′(x) = (x − 1)²(x − 4). Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f." On the right, number lines for f′ and f″ that fill in as tested. Board: x − 1 = 0 or x − 4 = 0, so x = 1 or x = 4; f′(0) = (1)(−4) = −4 < 0, f′(2) = (1)(−2) = −2 < 0, f′(5) = (16)(1) = 16 > 0; x = 1: no sign change, no extremum; x = 4: − to +, relative min; f″(x) = 2(x − 1)(x − 4) + (x − 1)²; = (x − 1)(2x − 8 + x − 1) = 3(x − 1)(x − 3); f″(0) = 9 > 0, f″(2) = −3 < 0, f″(4) = 9 > 0; inflection points at x = 1 and x = 3.
> f prime of x is x minus one squared, times x minus four. Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f. [pause]
> f prime is zero when x minus one is zero or x minus four is zero: at x equals one and x equals four. [pause]
> Test zero: one times negative four, negative. Test two: one times negative two, negative. Test five: sixteen times one, positive. [pause]
> f prime doesn't change sign at one, so there's no extremum there. It goes from negative to positive at four: a relative minimum.
> For inflection points, find f double prime with the product rule: two times x minus one times x minus four, plus x minus one squared. [pause]
> Factor out x minus one: x minus one, times two x minus eight plus x minus one. That's three x minus nine, so f double prime is three times x minus one times x minus three.
> Test zero: three times negative one times negative three, nine, positive. Test two: three times one times negative one, negative. Test four: three times three times one, positive. [pause]
> f double prime changes sign at one and at three, so both are inflection points. Notice x equals one: no extremum, but an inflection point. [long pause]

## Example 2: A sign table
**On screen:** "On (−∞, −1): f′ > 0, f″ < 0. At −1: f′ = 0. On (−1, 2): f′ < 0, f″ < 0. At 2: f″ = 0. On (2, ∞): f′ < 0, f″ > 0. Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f." Work: relative max at x = −1 (f′ + to −); inflection at x = 2 (f″ − to +); no relative min.
> A twice-differentiable f has this sign table. Find its relative extrema and inflection points. [pause]
> f prime changes from positive to negative at negative one: a relative maximum.
> f double prime changes from negative to positive at two: a point of inflection. [pause]
> f prime never changes from negative to positive, so there's no relative minimum.

## Example 3: When the second derivative is zero too
**On screen:** "f′(2) = 0 and f″(x) = (x − 2)eˣ. Does f have a relative extremum at x = 2?" Work: f″(2) = 0: the Second Derivative Test is silent; f″ < 0 for x < 2 and f″ > 0 for x > 2, so f′ has a minimum value of 0 at x = 2; f′ ≥ 0 near 2: f keeps increasing; no extremum, but an inflection point.
> f prime of two is zero, and f double prime of x is x minus two times e to the x. Does f have a relative extremum at two? [pause]
> f double prime of two is zero, so the second derivative test says nothing.
> Look one level down instead. f double prime is negative before two and positive after, so f prime decreases to two and then increases. [pause]
> f prime bottoms out at zero. So f prime is never negative near two, and f keeps increasing. No extremum.
> But f double prime changes sign, so there is an inflection point at two. [long pause]

## Example 4: Every column from one graph
**On screen:** "The graph of f′ on [−3, 4] is three line segments. Where does f have relative extrema? Where is f concave up or down, and where are its inflection points? Find f″(2)." The figure: a segment from (−3, 0) up to (−1, 2), a segment down to (1, −2), and a segment up to (4, 1). Board: x = 0: f′ + to −, rel. max; x = 3: f′ − to +, rel. min; f′ rising on (−3, −1) and (1, 4): concave up; f′ falling on (−1, 1): concave down; inflection at x = −1 and x = 1; f″(2) = slope of f′ = (1 − (−2))/(4 − 1) = 1.
> The graph of f prime is three line segments. Use the chart: where does f have relative extrema? Where is f concave up or down, and where are its inflection points? And what is f double prime of two? [try it]
> Extrema come from the f prime column: look for sign changes. f prime goes from positive to negative at zero: a relative maximum. From negative to positive at three: a relative minimum. [pause]
> Concavity is the f double prime column, which here means: is the graph of f prime rising or falling? It rises from negative three to negative one, and from one to four. So f is concave up there. [pause]
> It falls from negative one to one, so f is concave down there. The inflection points are at negative one and one, where f prime turns around. [pause]
> And f double prime is the slope of the graph of f prime. At two, we're on the last segment, which rises three over a run of three. So f double prime of two is one. [long pause]
