# 5.9 Connecting a Function, Its First Derivative, and Its Second Derivative

## Three graphs, one function
**On screen:** Three stacked graphs sharing an x-axis: f on top, f′ in the middle, f″ at the bottom. A vertical line sweeps across all three, and a readout shows the sign of each at the line's position.
> Here are three graphs stacked up: a function, its derivative, and its second derivative. [pause]
> Each one describes the one above it. f double prime tells how f prime is changing, and f prime tells how f is changing. [pause]
> Sweep across, and the signs below explain the shape above. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 5.9 · Connecting a function, its first derivative, and its second derivative." Unit 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation.

## Which level decides
**On screen:** A three-column table: "f", "f′", "f″". Rows: increasing / f′ > 0; concave up / f′ increasing / f″ > 0; relative extremum / f′ changes sign; inflection point / f′ has an extremum / f″ changes sign.
> Every question about f is decided one or two levels down. [pause]
> Is f increasing? Ask f prime about its sign. Is there an extremum? Ask whether f prime changes sign. [pause]
> Is f concave up? Ask whether f prime is increasing, or equivalently whether f double prime is positive. [pause]
> Is there an inflection point? Ask whether f double prime changes sign, or whether f prime has a peak or a valley. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: All the features
**On screen:** "f′(x) = (x − 1)²(x − 4). Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f." Work: f′ signs −, −, +: no extremum at 1, relative min at 4; f″(x) = 3(x − 1)(x − 3); f″ signs +, −, +: inflection points at x = 1 and x = 3.
> f prime of x is x minus one squared, times x minus four. Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f. [pause]
> The signs of f prime: negative, negative, positive. So no extremum at one, and a relative minimum at four.
> For inflection points, find f double prime. The product rule gives three times x minus one times x minus three. [pause]
> Its signs: positive, negative, positive. So f has inflection points at one and at three.

## Example 2: A sign table
**On screen:** "On (−∞, −1): f′ > 0, f″ < 0. At −1: f′ = 0. On (−1, 2): f′ < 0, f″ < 0. At 2: f″ = 0. On (2, ∞): f′ < 0, f″ > 0. Find the relative extrema and inflection points of f." Work: relative max at x = −1 (f′ + to −); inflection at x = 2 (f″ − to +); no relative min.
> A twice-differentiable f has this sign table. Find its relative extrema and inflection points. [pause]
> f prime changes from positive to negative at negative one: a relative maximum.
> f double prime changes from negative to positive at two: a point of inflection. [pause]
> f prime never changes from negative to positive, so there's no relative minimum.

## Example 3: When the second derivative is zero too
**On screen:** "f′(2) = 0 and f″(x) = (x − 2)eˣ. Does f have a relative extremum at x = 2?" Work: f″(2) = 0: the Second Derivative Test is silent; f″ < 0 for x < 2 and f″ > 0 for x > 2, so f′ has a minimum value of 0 at x = 2; f′ ≥ 0 near 2: f keeps increasing; no extremum, but an inflection point.
> f prime of two is zero, and f double prime of x is x minus two times e to the x. Does f have a relative extremum at two? [pause]
> f double prime of two is zero, so the second derivative test says nothing.
> Look one level down instead. f double prime is negative before two and positive after, so f prime decreases to two and then increases. [pause]
> f prime bottoms out at zero. So f prime is never negative near two, and f keeps increasing. No extremum.
> But f double prime changes sign, so there is an inflection point at two. [long pause]
