# 6.1 Exploring Accumulations of Change

## Filling a tank
**On screen:** On the left, a glass water tank with a faucet above it. On the right, axes with "rate (gal/min)" up the side and "t (min)" along the bottom, and the flat line r = 3. As a time marker slides from 0 to 4, the region under the line fills in as a growing rectangle, and the water in the tank rises in step. A readout: "water added = 3 × t gallons". At t = 4 it reads 12.
> Water flows into a tank at three gallons per minute. After four minutes, how much has come in? [pause]
> Three gallons per minute, times four minutes: twelve gallons. [pause]
> Now look at the graph of the rate. Twelve is also the area of the rectangle under it: height three, width four. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.1 · Exploring accumulations of change." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Area means amount
**On screen:** The rectangle again, its height labeled "3 gal/min" and its width "4 min". Beneath: "(3 gal/min)(4 min) = 12 gal", with the two "min" units crossed out. Then a boxed rule: "area under a rate graph = amount of change". Units: "(rate units) × (time units)".
> That's not a coincidence. The height of the graph is a rate, gallons per minute. The width is a time, minutes. [pause]
> Multiply them, and the minutes cancel. The area is measured in gallons: it's the amount of water that came in. [pause]
> That's true for any rate. The area under the graph of a rate of change is the total change in the amount. [long pause]

## When the rate changes
**On screen:** The rate graph r(t) = 2 + t on 0 ≤ t ≤ 4, a slanted line from 2 up to 6. The region under it shades in as a trapezoid while the tank fills faster and faster. Labels: heights 2 and 6, width 4. Board: area = ((2 + 6)/2)(4) = 16 gallons.
> Usually the rate isn't constant. Here the flow speeds up: r of t equals two plus t. [pause]
> The water added is still the area under the rate graph. This region is a trapezoid, so its area is the average of the two heights, times the width. [pause]
> The heights are two and six, so the average is four. Four times four minutes: sixteen gallons. [long pause]

## Below the axis
**On screen:** A velocity graph: v = 2 m/s for 0 ≤ t ≤ 3, then v = −1 m/s for 3 ≤ t ≤ 7. The rectangle above the axis shades green with "+6", the one below shades red with "−4". Beneath, a walker dot on a number line moves 6 meters right, then 4 meters back. Then: "net change: 6 − 4 = 2 m" and "total distance: 6 + 4 = 10 m".
> A rate can be negative too. Here's the velocity of someone walking along a path: two meters per second for three seconds, then negative one for four seconds. [pause]
> Above the axis, the area is six: they walk six meters forward. Below the axis, the area is four: they walk four meters back. [pause]
> Area below the axis counts as negative change. So the net change in position is six minus four: two meters. [pause]
> But the total distance walked is six plus four: ten meters. Net change subtracts the part below the axis. Total distance adds it. [long pause]

## Reading the area
**On screen:** The problem on top. On the right, the graph of the rate of rainfall r(t), in inches per hour: up from 0 to 0.4 over the first 2 hours, flat at 0.4 until t = 6, then down to 0 at t = 8. The region splits into a triangle, a rectangle and a triangle, each shading as it is computed. Board: units: (in/hr)(hr) = in; triangle: ½(2)(0.4) = 0.4; rectangle: (4)(0.4) = 1.6; triangle: ½(2)(0.4) = 0.4; total: 0.4 + 1.6 + 0.4 = 2.4 inches.
> Rain falls at the rate shown, in inches per hour. How much rain fell from t equals zero to t equals eight hours? [try it]
> The rate is in inches per hour and time is in hours, so the area is in inches: the amount of rain. [pause]
> Split the region into simple shapes: a triangle, a rectangle, and another triangle.
> The first triangle: one half, times two hours, times point four. Point four inches.
> The rectangle: four hours times point four. One point six inches.
> The last triangle matches the first: point four inches. [pause]
> Add them up: point four plus one point six plus point four. Two point four inches of rain. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Area under a rate graph = amount of change." "Units: (rate units) × (time units)." "Above the axis adds; below the axis subtracts." "Total distance: add all the areas."
> The area under the graph of a rate of change is the amount of change. Its units are the rate's units times the time units. Area above the axis adds, area below subtracts. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: What the area means
**On screen:** "B(t) is the rate, in loaves per hour, at which a bakery sells bread, t hours after it opens. What does the area under the graph of B from t = 0 to t = 3 represent?" Board: height: loaves per hour; width: hours; (loaves/hour)(hours) = loaves; the total number of loaves sold in the first 3 hours after opening.
> B of t is the rate, in loaves per hour, at which a bakery sells bread, t hours after it opens. What does the area under the graph of B from t equals zero to three represent? [pause]
> The height is in loaves per hour, and the width is in hours. [pause]
> Multiply: loaves per hour, times hours, is loaves. The hours cancel.
> So the area is a number of loaves: the total number of loaves sold from t equals zero to t equals three, the first three hours after opening. [long pause]

## Example 2: Net change and distance
**On screen:** "A particle's velocity is v(t) = 4 − 2t meters per second for 0 ≤ t ≤ 4. Find its displacement and the total distance it travels." On the right, the line from (0, 4) down to (4, −4), with the triangle above the axis green and the one below red. Board: 4 − 2t = 0, t = 2; above: ½(2)(4) = 4; below: ½(2)(4) = 4, counts negative; displacement: 4 − 4 = 0 m; distance: 4 + 4 = 8 m.
> A particle's velocity is v of t equals four minus two t meters per second, for t from zero to four. Find its displacement and the total distance it travels. [pause]
> The graph is a line. It crosses zero when four minus two t is zero: t equals two. [pause]
> Above the axis, a triangle with base two and height four. Its area is one half times two times four: four.
> Below the axis, the same size triangle: area four, and it counts as negative. [pause]
> The displacement is four minus four: zero meters. The particle ends where it started.
> The total distance adds the two areas: four plus four, eight meters. [long pause]

## Example 3: Draining a tank
**On screen:** "Water drains from a tank at r(t) = 10 − 2t liters per minute for 0 ≤ t ≤ 5. The tank held 60 liters at t = 0. How much water is in the tank at t = 5?" On the right, the line from (0, 10) down to (5, 0), the triangle under it shaded. Board: area = amount drained; triangle: ½(5)(10) = 25 L; 60 − 25 = 35 L.
> Water drains from a tank at r of t equals ten minus two t liters per minute, for t from zero to five. The tank held sixty liters at the start. How much is in the tank at t equals five? [pause]
> r is the rate the water drains out, so the area under its graph is the amount that drains out. [pause]
> The graph is a line from ten at t equals zero down to zero at t equals five: a triangle.
> Its area is one half times five times ten: twenty-five liters. [pause]
> The tank started with sixty liters, and twenty-five drained out. Sixty minus twenty-five: thirty-five liters. [long pause]
