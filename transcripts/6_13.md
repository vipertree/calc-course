# 6.13 Evaluating Improper Integrals

## An endless region
**On screen:** The curves y = 1/x² and y = 1/x for x ≥ 1, on separate axes stacked. Under each, a region from 1 to a moving right edge b shades in as b slides to the right. Readouts: under 1/x², "area = 1 − 1/b" creeping toward 1; under 1/x, "area = ln b" climbing without end. A note: "BC only".
> Here are two regions that run forever to the right: under one over x squared, and under one over x, starting at one. This topic is for BC. [pause]
> Shade from one out to some edge b, and slide b to the right. Under one over x squared, the area creeps toward one and never passes it. [pause]
> Under one over x, the area keeps growing, past any number you name. One region has a finite area. The other doesn't. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.13 · Evaluating improper integrals." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Infinite limits of integration
**On screen:** "∫_a^∞ f(x) dx = lim_{b→∞} ∫_a^b f(x) dx". Labels: "limit exists (a number): converges"; "limit doesn't exist or is infinite: diverges". Then the worked pair: lim_{b→∞} ∫_1^b x⁻² dx = lim_{b→∞} [−1/x]_1^b = lim_{b→∞} (−1/b + 1) = 1, converges; lim_{b→∞} ∫_1^b (1/x) dx = lim_{b→∞} ln b = ∞, diverges.
> An integral with infinity as a limit is called improper. It means a limit: integrate up to b, then let b go to infinity. [pause]
> If that limit is a number, the integral converges to that number. If the limit is infinite or doesn't exist, the integral diverges. [pause]
> For one over x squared, the antiderivative is negative one over x. From one to b, that's negative one over b, plus one. As b goes to infinity, one over b goes to zero, so the integral converges to one. [pause]
> For one over x, the antiderivative is ln x. From one to b, that's ln b, and ln b goes to infinity. That integral diverges. [pause]
> On the AP exam, write the limit on every line. The infinity never gets plugged in directly. [long pause]

## A vertical asymptote
**On screen:** The graph of y = 1/√x on (0, 1], shooting up along the y-axis, with the region under it shaded from a to 1 as a slides toward 0. Board: ∫_0^1 x^{−1/2} dx = lim_{a→0⁺} ∫_a^1 x^{−1/2} dx; = lim_{a→0⁺} [2√x]_a^1; = lim_{a→0⁺} (2 − 2√a) = 2; converges.
> An integral is also improper when the function goes off to infinity inside the interval or at an end. Here, one over root x goes off to infinity at zero. [pause]
> Replace the bad endpoint with a, and let a approach zero from the right.
> The antiderivative of x to the negative one half is two root x. From a to one, that's two minus two root a. [pause]
> As a goes to zero, two root a goes to zero. So this integral converges to two, even though the region is infinitely tall. [long pause]

## p-integrals
**On screen:** A summary: "∫_1^∞ 1/xᵖ dx converges if p > 1, diverges if p ≤ 1." Beneath: p = 2: converges (to 1); p = 1: diverges; p = 1/2: diverges. Small graphs of 1/x², 1/x and 1/√x, colored green, red, red.
> A useful pattern: the integral from one to infinity of one over x to the p converges exactly when p is bigger than one. [pause]
> One over x squared converges. One over x, the borderline case, diverges. One over root x falls even more slowly, so it diverges too. [long pause]

## A hidden asymptote
**On screen:** The problem: "Evaluate ∫_{−1}^{1} 1/x² dx." First, the tempting wrong work, written in gray and crossed out: [−1/x]_{−1}^{1} = −1 − 1 = −2. Then: "1/x² > 0, so the integral can't be negative; and 1/x² → ∞ at x = 0, inside [−1, 1]." Board: split at 0; ∫_0^1 x⁻² dx = lim_{a→0⁺} [−1/x]_a^1 = lim_{a→0⁺} (−1 + 1/a) = ∞; diverges, so the whole integral diverges.
> Evaluate the integral from negative one to one of one over x squared. [try it]
> The tempting move is to plug in: negative one over x from negative one to one gives negative two. But one over x squared is positive everywhere, so its integral can't be negative. Something's wrong. [pause]
> The problem is at zero, inside the interval, where one over x squared goes off to infinity. The Fundamental Theorem needs a continuous function, so it doesn't apply across zero. [pause]
> Split at zero and look at one piece: the integral from zero to one is the limit as a approaches zero from the right of negative one plus one over a. [pause]
> One over a goes to infinity. That piece diverges, so the whole integral diverges. Always check for asymptotes inside the interval. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Infinite limit: ∫_a^∞ f dx = lim_{b→∞} ∫_a^b f dx." "Asymptote at c: replace c by a limit." "Number: converges. Infinite or no limit: diverges." "Write the limit on every line. Check inside the interval."
> Every improper integral is a limit. Replace the infinity or the asymptote with a variable, integrate, and take the limit. A number means converges. Infinite means diverges. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An exponential tail
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_0^∞ e^{−2x} dx." Board: = lim_{b→∞} ∫_0^b e^{−2x} dx; = lim_{b→∞} [−½e^{−2x}]_0^b; = lim_{b→∞} (−½e^{−2b} + ½); = 0 + ½ = ½; converges.
> Evaluate the integral from zero to infinity of e to the negative two x. [pause]
> Write it as the limit as b goes to infinity of the integral from zero to b. [pause]
> The antiderivative is negative one half e to the negative two x. From zero to b: negative one half e to the negative two b, plus one half.
> As b goes to infinity, e to the negative two b goes to zero. [pause]
> So the integral converges to one half. [long pause]

## Example 2: A slow tail
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_1^∞ x/(x² + 1) dx." Board: = lim_{b→∞} ∫_1^b x/(x² + 1) dx; u = x² + 1: = lim_{b→∞} [½ ln(x² + 1)]_1^b; = lim_{b→∞} (½ ln(b² + 1) − ½ ln 2) = ∞; diverges.
> Evaluate the integral from one to infinity of x over x squared plus one. [pause]
> Write the limit. A substitution, u equals x squared plus one, gives the antiderivative one half ln of x squared plus one. [pause]
> From one to b: one half ln of b squared plus one, minus one half ln two.
> As b goes to infinity, ln of b squared plus one goes to infinity. [pause]
> The integral diverges. For large x, x over x squared plus one behaves like one over x, the borderline case. [long pause]
