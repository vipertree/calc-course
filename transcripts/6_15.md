# 6.15 Selecting Techniques for Antidifferentiation

## A full toolkit
**On screen:** A toolkit tray with seven labeled tools laid out: "basic rules", "rewrite first", "substitution", "long division", "complete the square", "integration by parts", "partial fractions" (the last two in the accumulation color). Above the tray, a scattered pile of integrals.
> You now have every antiderivative technique in the course. On a test, the hard part is choosing which one to use. [pause]
> There's no fixed list of steps to march through. Look at the integral, notice its features, and pick the tool those features call for. [pause]
> If the first tool doesn't work, put it back and try another. That's normal. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "6.14 BC · Selecting techniques for antidifferentiation." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## What to look for
**On screen:** A two-column chart, built row by row: "a basic form (power, trig, eˣ, 1/x) | basic rules"; "a product, a power of a sum, or a sum over one term | rewrite first"; "an inside function whose derivative is a factor | substitution"; "a fraction, top degree ≥ bottom degree | long division"; "1 over a quadratic that won't factor | complete the square"; "a product of two kinds of functions, no inside derivative | integration by parts"; "a fraction whose bottom factors into different lines | partial fractions".
> Start with the features. If it's already a basic form, use the basic rules. [pause]
> If it's a product you can multiply out, a power of a sum, or a sum over a single term, rewrite it first. [pause]
> If there's an inside function and its derivative is a factor, up to a constant, use substitution. Check for this one early: it's quick to spot. [pause]
> If it's a fraction whose top has degree at least the bottom's, divide. If it's one over a quadratic that won't factor, complete the square. [pause]
> A product of two different kinds of functions, such as x times e to the x, with no inside derivative to match, calls for integration by parts. [pause]
> And a fraction whose bottom factors into different linear factors calls for partial fractions. [long pause]

## Choosing the tool
**On screen:** The problem: "Choose a technique for each: (a) ∫ x e^(2x) dx (b) ∫ x e^(x²) dx (c) ∫ (x + 5)/((x − 1)(x + 2)) dx (d) ∫ 1/(x² + 4x + 5) dx." Board: (a) no inside derivative, a product: parts, u = x, dv = e^(2x) dx; (b) u = x² (2x is a factor): substitution, ½ e^(x²) + C; (c) the bottom factors into lines: partial fractions, 2 ln|x − 1| − ln|x + 2| + C; (d) (x + 2)² + 1: arctan(x + 2) + C.
> For each of these four integrals, which tool? [pause]
> The first is x times e to the two x. There's no inside function whose derivative is a factor, so substitution won't finish it. It's a power of x times an exponential: integration by parts, with u equal to x. [pause]
> The second looks almost the same, but now x squared is inside, and its derivative, two x, is a factor up to a constant. Substitution, giving one half e to the x squared. [pause]
> The third is a fraction whose bottom is already factored into lines, and the top has the smaller degree. Partial fractions, which gives two ln of absolute x minus one, minus ln of absolute x plus two. [pause]
> The fourth is one over a quadratic that won't factor. Complete the square: x plus two, squared, plus one. That's arctangent of x plus two. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A product of two kinds
**On screen:** "Find ∫ x ln x dx." A four-box parts table on the right fills in as u, dv, du and v are chosen. Board: no inside function with its derivative as a factor: parts; u = ln x, dv = x dx; du = (1/x) dx, v = x²/2; ∫ x ln x dx = (x²/2) ln x − ∫ (x²/2)(1/x) dx; (x²/2)(1/x) = x/2; = (x²/2) ln x − x²/4 + C.
> Find the integral of x times ln x.
> Check substitution first. The derivative of ln x is one over x, and the other factor is x, not one over x. So substitution won't work. It's a product of two different kinds of functions: integration by parts.
> Which one is u? We want u to get simpler when we take its derivative, and ln x becomes one over x. So u is ln x, and d v is x d x. [pause]
> Then d u is one over x d x, and v is x squared over two.
> Parts gives u v minus the integral of v d u: x squared over two times ln x, minus the integral of x squared over two times one over x. [pause]
> One x cancels: x squared over two, times one over x, is x over two.
> The integral of x over two is x squared over four. So the answer is x squared over two ln x, minus x squared over four, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 2: The same pieces, rearranged
**On screen:** "Find ∫ (ln x)/x dx." Board: d/dx ln x = 1/x, a factor: substitution; u = ln x, du = (1/x) dx; ∫ (ln x)/x dx = ∫ u du = u²/2; = (ln x)²/2 + C; one piece is the other's derivative: substitution.
> Now the same pieces arranged differently: the integral of ln x over x.
> Check substitution first. The derivative of ln x is one over x, and one over x is right there as a factor. So this one is substitution. No parts needed. [pause]
> Let u be ln x. Then d u is one over x d x.
> The integral becomes the integral of u d u, which is u squared over two.
> Put x back: ln x, squared, over two, plus C. [pause]
> Same two pieces as the last example, but a different tool, because this time one piece is the derivative of the other. [long pause]

## Example 3: A bottom that factors
**On screen:** "Find ∫ 2/(x² − 1) dx." Board: no x on top for substitution, and the bottom factors: (x − 1)(x + 1); 2/((x − 1)(x + 1)) = A/(x − 1) + B/(x + 1); 2 = A(x + 1) + B(x − 1); Let x = 1: 2 = 2A, so A = 1; Let x = −1: 2 = −2B, so B = −1; ∫ (1/(x − 1) − 1/(x + 1)) dx; = ln|x − 1| − ln|x + 1| + C.
> Find the integral of two over x squared minus one.
> The top has no x, so there's no derivative of the bottom to match, and substitution is out. But the bottom factors: x minus one, times x plus one. That calls for partial fractions. [pause]
> Write two over x minus one times x plus one as A over x minus one, plus B over x plus one.
> Multiply both sides by x minus one times x plus one: two equals A times x plus one, plus B times x minus one.
> Let x equal one. The B term is zero: two equals two A, so A is one. [pause]
> Let x equal negative one. The A term is zero: two equals negative two B, so B is negative one. [pause]
> So the integral is the integral of one over x minus one, minus one over x plus one.
> That's ln of absolute x minus one, minus ln of absolute x plus one, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 4: Check substitution first
**On screen:** "Find ∫ 2x/(x² − 1) dx." Board: d/dx (x² − 1) = 2x, the top: substitution; u = x² − 1, du = 2x dx; ∫ 2x/(x² − 1) dx = ∫ (1/u) du = ln|u|; = ln|x² − 1| + C; partial fractions agrees: ln|x − 1| + ln|x + 1| = ln|x² − 1|.
> Last one: the integral of two x over x squared minus one.
> It's the same bottom, so partial fractions would work. But check substitution first: the derivative of x squared minus one is two x, which is exactly the top. [pause]
> Let u be x squared minus one. Then d u is two x d x.
> The integral becomes the integral of one over u, d u, which is ln of absolute u.
> So the answer is ln of absolute x squared minus one, plus C. [pause]
> Partial fractions gives ln of absolute x minus one, plus ln of absolute x plus one, which combine into the same answer. Substitution just gets there faster. [long pause]
