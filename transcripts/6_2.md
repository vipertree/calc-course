# 6.2 Approximating Areas with Riemann Sums

## Rectangles under a curve
**On screen:** The curve f(x) = 0.1x² + 1 on 0 ≤ x ≤ 8, the region under it shaded. Four rectangles appear under the curve, then eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two, hugging the curve more closely each time. A readout of the total rectangle area creeps toward 25.07.
> Here's a region under a curve. Its top isn't straight, so no simple shape has the same area. [pause]
> But rectangles are easy. Fill the region with four rectangles and add up their areas. [pause]
> Use more, thinner rectangles, and the total gets closer and closer to the true area. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.2 · Approximating areas with Riemann sums." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Left Riemann sum
**On screen:** f(x) = 0.1x² + 1 on [0, 8], cut at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 by dashed lines, with a double arrow "Δx = 2" under the first piece. One rectangle at a time: a dot marks where the piece starts on the axis and the point straight above it on the curve, the rectangle rises to that height, and its area is written under it: "2 · f(0) = 2 · 1 = 2", "2 · f(2) = 2 · 1.4 = 2.8", "2 · f(4) = 2 · 2.6 = 5.2", "2 · f(6) = 2 · 4.6 = 9.2". The dot for each rectangle stays on the curve while it is discussed. Then "2 + 2.8 + 5.2 + 9.2 = 19.2", and the name "left Riemann sum".
> Let's find the area under f of x equals point one x squared plus one, from zero to eight. Split it into four pieces, each two wide. The width of a piece is called delta x: here, delta x is two. [pause]
> Start with the first piece, from zero to two, and build a rectangle on it. How tall? Look: the height of this rectangle is just the function value where the piece starts. That's f of zero, which is one. [pause]
> Its area is width times height: two times f of zero, two times one. Two. [pause]
> The next piece starts at two, so its height is f of two, one point four. Two times one point four: two point eight. [pause]
> The third starts at four: two times f of four, two times two point six. Five point two.
> The last starts at six: two times f of six, two times four point six. Nine point two. [pause]
> Add the four areas: two, plus two point eight, plus five point two, plus nine point two. Nineteen point two. [pause]
> Every height came from the left end of its piece. That's why this is called a left Riemann sum. [long pause]

## Factoring out the width
**On screen:** The four left rectangles stay. Board: L = 2 · f(0) + 2 · f(2) + 2 · f(4) + 2 · f(6); = 2[f(0) + f(2) + f(4) + f(6)]; = 2[1 + 1.4 + 2.6 + 4.6]; = 2(9.6) = 19.2. Then: "Or add the rectangles one at a time: width × height."
> Every rectangle has the same width, two, so the two can be factored out: two times the quantity f of zero plus f of two plus f of four plus f of six. [pause]
> Now put in the values: two times the quantity one plus one point four plus two point six plus four point six. That's two times nine point six: nineteen point two, the same as before. [pause]
> That's the usual way to write it. But you can always get it by thinking about the rectangles one at a time: each one is its width times its height. [long pause]

## Right Riemann sum
**On screen:** The same graph. The left rectangles fade out and the dots move to the right end of each piece. One at a time, a rectangle rises to the height of the curve above its piece's right end, with its area written under it: "2 · f(2) = 2.8", "2 · f(4) = 5.2", "2 · f(6) = 9.2", "2 · f(8) = 2 · 7.4 = 14.8". Board: R = 2[f(2) + f(4) + f(6) + f(8)]; = 2[1.4 + 2.6 + 4.6 + 7.4]; = 2(16) = 32. Then the name "right Riemann sum".
> We could draw these rectangles differently. We made each height the function value at the left side of its piece. What if we used the value at the right side instead? [pause]
> The first piece ends at two, so its height is f of two: one point four. Two times one point four: two point eight.
> The second ends at four: two times f of four, five point two. The third ends at six: two times f of six, nine point two. [pause]
> And the last ends at eight: two times f of eight, two times seven point four. Fourteen point eight. [pause]
> Using the right ends is called a right Riemann sum. Factored, it's two times the quantity f of two plus f of four plus f of six plus f of eight. [pause]
> That's two times the quantity one point four plus two point six plus four point six plus seven point four: two times sixteen, thirty-two. [long pause]

## Midpoint Riemann sum
**On screen:** The same graph. A tick marks the middle of each piece, at 1, 3, 5 and 7, with a dashed line up to a dot on the curve. One at a time, a rectangle rises to the dot's height, with its area written under it: "2 · f(1) = 2 · 1.1 = 2.2", "2 · f(3) = 3.8", "2 · f(5) = 7", "2 · f(7) = 11.8". Board: M = 2[f(1) + f(3) + f(5) + f(7)]; = 2[1.1 + 1.9 + 3.5 + 5.9]; = 2(12.4) = 24.8. Then the name "midpoint Riemann sum".
> There's a third choice: the middle of each piece. The first piece runs from zero to two, so its middle is one. [pause]
> Go straight up from one to the curve. f of one is one point one, and that's the height of the first rectangle. Its area is two times one point one: two point two. [pause]
> The other middles are three, five, and seven. Two times f of three is three point eight. Two times f of five is seven. Two times f of seven is eleven point eight. [pause]
> Using the middles is called a midpoint Riemann sum. Factored, it's two times the quantity f of one plus f of three plus f of five plus f of seven. [pause]
> That's two times the quantity one point one plus one point nine plus three point five plus five point nine: two times twelve point four, twenty-four point eight. [long pause]

## Trapezoids
**On screen:** The same graph, now with trapezoids whose slanted tops connect the points on the curve at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8. Board: T = 2 · (f(0) + f(2))/2 + 2 · (f(2) + f(4))/2 + ...; = (2/2)[f(0) + 2f(2) + 2f(4) + 2f(6) + f(8)]; = 1[1 + 2.8 + 5.2 + 9.2 + 7.4] = 25.6. Then "T = (L + R)/2 = (19.2 + 32)/2 = 25.6" and "true area ≈ 25.07".
> A trapezoidal sum replaces the flat tops with slanted ones that connect the points on the curve. Each trapezoid's area is its width times the average of its two sides: two times f of zero plus f of two, over two, and so on. [pause]
> Every inside height gets used twice, once by each trapezoid next to it. So T is two over two, times the quantity f of zero, plus two f of two, plus two f of four, plus two f of six, plus f of eight. [pause]
> Put in the values: one plus two point eight plus five point two plus nine point two plus seven point four. Twenty-five point six. That's also the average of the left and right sums. [pause]
> The true area is about twenty-five point oh seven. The midpoint and trapezoidal sums are much closer than the left or right sums. [long pause]

## Over or under
**On screen:** The increasing curve with its left rectangles, and the question "f increasing, left sum: what can we expect?" Then the small gaps between each rectangle's top and the curve light up, and the answer: "underestimate: every rectangle is under the graph". Then the right rectangles, their corners above the curve lit up: "right sum: overestimate". Then a decreasing curve, g(x) = 0.1(8 − x)² + 1, with left rectangles poking above it ("left sum: overestimate") and right rectangles under it ("right sum: underestimate"). Then the rule: "increasing: left under, right over. Decreasing: left over, right under." Last, two small panels: a bowl with trapezoid tops above it ("concave up: trapezoid over") and a hill with tops below it ("concave down: trapezoid under").
> Is a sum too big or too small? Our function is increasing, and we're using a left Riemann sum. What can we expect? [long pause]
> The sum will be an underestimate. Each height comes from the left end, the lowest point of its piece, so every rectangle sits below the graph. These little gaps are area the sum misses. [pause]
> Now the right Riemann sum. What can we expect? [long pause]
> An overestimate. Each height comes from the right end, the highest point of its piece, so the rectangles poke up above the graph. [pause]
> For a decreasing function, it's the reverse. Going downhill, the left end is the high point, so a left sum is an overestimate. The right end is the low point, so a right sum is an underestimate. [pause]
> So: increasing, left under and right over. Decreasing, left over and right under. [pause]
> For trapezoids, look at the bend. When the graph is concave up, a bowl, the slanted tops sit above the curve: the trapezoidal sum is too big. When it's concave down, a hill, the tops sit below the curve: too small. [long pause]

## Uneven widths
**On screen:** The problem on top: a table, t (minutes) = 0, 2, 5, 9, 10; r(t) (gallons per minute) = 4, 6, 7, 5, 3. Each subinterval's width is written above the table: 2, 3, 4, 1. Board: widths: 2, 3, 4, 1; 2 · r(0) + 3 · r(2) + 4 · r(5) + 1 · r(9); = 2(4) + 3(6) + 4(7) + 1(5); = 8 + 18 + 28 + 5; = 59 gallons. On the right, the four rectangles of different widths, each shading as it is added.
> Water flows into a tank at the rate r of t, given in this table. Use a left Riemann sum with the four subintervals in the table to approximate the total water that flows in from t equals zero to ten. [pause]
> The widths aren't equal. From zero to two is two minutes, two to five is three, five to nine is four, and nine to ten is one. Each rectangle gets its own width, so there's no width to factor out. [pause]
> Left sum, so each height is the rate at the left end: two times r of zero, plus three times r of two, plus four times r of five, plus one times r of nine.
> Put in the values from the table: two times four, plus three times six, plus four times seven, plus one times five.
> That's eight plus eighteen plus twenty-eight plus five. [pause]
> Fifty-nine gallons. Gallons per minute times minutes gives gallons. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Riemann sum: add up (width) × (height)." "Left, right, midpoint: where the height comes from." "Write Δx[f(x₁) + f(x₂) + ...], then the numbers." "Trapezoid: average the two sides." "Increasing: left under, right over. Concave up: trapezoid over."
> A Riemann sum adds up widths times heights. Left, right, and midpoint say where each height comes from. Write the function values first, then the numbers. Trapezoids average the two sides. And the shape of the graph says if the sum is too big or too small. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A right sum
**On screen:** "Use a right Riemann sum with 4 equal subintervals to approximate the area under f(x) = 1/x on [1, 3]. Is it an overestimate or an underestimate?" Board: Δx = (3 − 1)/4 = 0.5; right ends 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3; R = 0.5[f(1.5) + f(2) + f(2.5) + f(3)]; = 0.5[2/3 + 1/2 + 2/5 + 1/3]; = 0.5(19/10) = 19/20 = 0.95; f is decreasing: right sum underestimates.
> Use a right Riemann sum with four equal subintervals to approximate the area under one over x from one to three. Is it an overestimate or an underestimate? [pause]
> The width of each piece is three minus one, over four: point five. [pause]
> The right ends are one point five, two, two point five, and three. So R is point five times the quantity f of one point five plus f of two plus f of two point five plus f of three.
> Put in the values: two thirds, one half, two fifths, and one third.
> Those add to nineteen tenths. Times the width, point five: nineteen twentieths, point nine five. [pause]
> One over x is decreasing, so each right end is the low point of its piece. The right sum is an underestimate. [long pause]

## Example 2: Trapezoids from a table
**On screen:** "Use a trapezoidal sum with the subintervals in the table to approximate the area under f from x = 0 to x = 4." Table: x = 0, 1, 3, 4; f(x) = 2, 5, 7, 4. Board: widths 1, 2, 1; (1)(f(0) + f(1))/2 = (1)(2 + 5)/2 = 3.5; (2)(f(1) + f(3))/2 = (2)(5 + 7)/2 = 12; (1)(f(3) + f(4))/2 = (1)(7 + 4)/2 = 5.5; T = 3.5 + 12 + 5.5 = 21.
> Use a trapezoidal sum with the subintervals in the table to approximate the area under f from zero to four. [pause]
> The widths are one, two, and one. Each trapezoid is its width times the average of its two heights. [pause]
> First: one times f of zero plus f of one, over two. That's one times two plus five over two, three point five.
> Second: two times f of one plus f of three, over two. Two times five plus seven over two, twelve.
> Third: one times f of three plus f of four, over two. One times seven plus four over two, five point five. [pause]
> Add them: three point five plus twelve plus five point five, twenty-one. [long pause]

## Example 3: Putting them in order
**On screen:** "f is positive, increasing and concave down on [a, b]. Put the left sum L, the right sum R, the trapezoidal sum T, and the exact area A in order." On the right, a hill-shaped increasing curve with a left rectangle below it, a right rectangle above it, and a trapezoid whose top sits just under the curve. Board: increasing: L < A < R; concave down: T < A; T = (L + R)/2 > L; L < T < A < R.
> f is positive, increasing, and concave down. Put the left sum, the right sum, the trapezoidal sum, and the exact area in order. [pause]
> f is increasing, so the left sum is too small and the right sum is too big. [pause]
> f is concave down, so the trapezoid tops sit under the curve: the trapezoidal sum is too small too.
> But the trapezoidal sum is the average of the left and right sums, so it's bigger than the left sum. [pause]
> So the order is: left, then trapezoid, then the exact area, then right. [long pause]
