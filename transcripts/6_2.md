# 6.2 Approximating Areas with Riemann Sums

## Rectangles under a curve
**On screen:** The curve y = ¼x² + 1 on 0 ≤ x ≤ 4, the region under it shaded. Four rectangles appear under the curve, then eight, then sixteen, then thirty-two, hugging the curve more closely each time. A readout of the total rectangle area creeps toward 9.33.
> Here's a region under a curve. Its top isn't straight, so no simple shape has the same area. [pause]
> But rectangles are easy. Fill the region with four rectangles and add up their areas. [pause]
> Use more, thinner rectangles, and the total gets closer and closer to the true area. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.2 · Approximating areas with Riemann sums." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Left and right sums
**On screen:** f(x) = ¼x² + 1 on [0, 4], four subintervals of width Δx = 1. First the left sum: each rectangle's height is f at the left end, computed on the board: f(0) = 1, f(1) = 1.25, f(2) = 2, f(3) = 3.25; L = 1(1 + 1.25 + 2 + 3.25) = 7.5. The rectangles sit below the curve. Then the right sum: f(1) = 1.25, f(2) = 2, f(3) = 3.25, f(4) = 5; R = 1(1.25 + 2 + 3.25 + 5) = 11.5. These rectangles poke above the curve.
> Split zero to four into four pieces, each one wide. The width of each piece is called delta x. [pause]
> For a left sum, each rectangle's height is the function's value at the left end of its piece. f of zero is one, f of one is one point two five, f of two is two, and f of three is three point two five.
> Each width is one, so the left sum is one times the total of those heights: seven point five. [pause]
> For a right sum, use the right ends: f of one, two, three, and four. That's one point two five, two, three point two five, and five.
> The right sum is eleven point five. [pause]
> This function is increasing, so the left ends are the low points of each piece and the right ends are the high points. The left sum is too small and the right sum is too big. [long pause]

## Midpoints and trapezoids
**On screen:** The same region. First, rectangles whose heights are f at the midpoints 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5: M = 1(1.0625 + 1.5625 + 2.5625 + 4.0625) = 9.25. Then trapezoids whose slanted tops connect the points on the curve: T = (1/2)(1)(1 + 2(1.25) + 2(2) + 2(3.25) + 5) = 9.5, and beside it "T = (L + R)/2 = (7.5 + 11.5)/2 = 9.5". The true area, 9.33, is shown for comparison.
> A midpoint sum uses the value in the middle of each piece. Here the midpoints are point five, one point five, two point five, and three point five. [pause]
> Their heights add to nine point two five, and each width is one, so the midpoint sum is nine point two five.
> A trapezoidal sum replaces the flat tops with slanted ones that connect the points on the curve. Each trapezoid's area is the average of its two sides, times its width. [pause]
> Every inside height gets used twice, once by each trapezoid next to it. The total is nine point five, which is also the average of the left and right sums. [pause]
> The true area is about nine point three three. Both the midpoint and trapezoidal sums are much closer than left or right. [long pause]

## Over or under
**On screen:** Four small panels. "f increasing: left under, right over." "f decreasing: left over, right under." "f concave up: trapezoid over." "f concave down: trapezoid under." In each panel the shapes are drawn so the gap or overhang is visible.
> Whether a sum is too big or too small depends on the shape of the graph. [pause]
> If f is increasing, a left sum underestimates and a right sum overestimates. If f is decreasing, it's the other way around. [pause]
> For trapezoids, look at the bend. When the graph is concave up, a bowl, the slanted tops sit above the curve: the trapezoidal sum is too big. [pause]
> When it's concave down, a hill, the tops sit below the curve: the trapezoidal sum is too small. [long pause]

## Uneven widths
**On screen:** The problem on top: a table, t (minutes) = 0, 2, 5, 9, 10; r(t) (gallons per minute) = 4, 6, 7, 5, 3. Each subinterval's width is written above the table: 2, 3, 4, 1. Board: left sum: 2(4) + 3(6) + 4(7) + 1(5); = 8 + 18 + 28 + 5 = 59 gallons. On the right, the four rectangles of different widths, each shading as it is added.
> Water flows into a tank at the rate r of t, given in this table. Use a left Riemann sum with the four subintervals in the table to approximate the total water that flows in from t equals zero to ten. [try it]
> The widths aren't equal. From zero to two is two minutes, two to five is three, five to nine is four, and nine to ten is one. Each rectangle gets its own width. [pause]
> Left sum, so each height is the rate at the left end. Two times four, plus three times six, plus four times seven, plus one times five.
> That's eight plus eighteen plus twenty-eight plus five. [pause]
> Fifty-nine gallons. Gallons per minute times minutes gives gallons. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Riemann sum: add (height) × (width)." "Left, right, midpoint: where the height comes from." "Trapezoid: average the two sides." "Increasing: left under, right over. Concave up: trapezoid over."
> A Riemann sum adds up heights times widths. Left, right, and midpoint say where each height comes from. Trapezoids average the two sides. And the shape of the graph says if the sum is too big or too small. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A right sum
**On screen:** "Use a right Riemann sum with 4 equal subintervals to approximate the area under f(x) = 1/x on [1, 3]. Is it an overestimate or an underestimate?" Board: Δx = (3 − 1)/4 = 0.5; right ends 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3; f: 2/3, 1/2, 2/5, 1/3; R = 0.5(2/3 + 1/2 + 2/5 + 1/3); = 0.5(19/10) = 19/20 = 0.95; f is decreasing: right sum underestimates.
> Use a right Riemann sum with four equal subintervals to approximate the area under one over x from one to three. Is it an overestimate or an underestimate? [pause]
> The width of each piece is three minus one, over four: point five. [pause]
> The right ends are one point five, two, two point five, and three. Their heights are two thirds, one half, two fifths, and one third.
> Those add to nineteen tenths. Times the width, point five: nineteen twentieths, point nine five. [pause]
> One over x is decreasing, so each right end is the low point of its piece. The right sum is an underestimate. [long pause]

## Example 2: Trapezoids from a table
**On screen:** "Use a trapezoidal sum with the subintervals in the table to approximate the area under f from x = 0 to x = 4." Table: x = 0, 1, 3, 4; f(x) = 2, 5, 7, 4. Board: widths 1, 2, 1; (1)(2 + 5)/2 = 3.5; (2)(5 + 7)/2 = 12; (1)(7 + 4)/2 = 5.5; T = 3.5 + 12 + 5.5 = 21.
> Use a trapezoidal sum with the subintervals in the table to approximate the area under f from zero to four. [pause]
> The widths are one, two, and one. Each trapezoid is its width times the average of its two heights. [pause]
> First: one times two plus five over two, three point five.
> Second: two times five plus seven over two, twelve.
> Third: one times seven plus four over two, five point five. [pause]
> Add them: three point five plus twelve plus five point five, twenty-one. [long pause]

## Example 3: Putting them in order
**On screen:** "f is positive, increasing and concave down on [a, b]. Put the left sum L, the right sum R, the trapezoidal sum T, and the exact area A in order." On the right, a hill-shaped increasing curve with a left rectangle below it, a right rectangle above it, and a trapezoid whose top sits just under the curve. Board: increasing: L < A < R; concave down: T < A; T = (L + R)/2 > L; L < T < A < R.
> f is positive, increasing, and concave down. Put the left sum, the right sum, the trapezoidal sum, and the exact area in order. [pause]
> f is increasing, so the left sum is too small and the right sum is too big. [pause]
> f is concave down, so the trapezoid tops sit under the curve: the trapezoidal sum is too small too.
> But the trapezoidal sum is the average of the left and right sums, so it's bigger than the left sum. [pause]
> So the order is: left, then trapezoid, then the exact area, then right. [long pause]
