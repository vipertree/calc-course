# 6.4 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and Accumulation Functions

## A running total
**On screen:** Two graphs on the same x-scale, stacked. Below: the graph of f, labeled "f(t)" in large type next to the curve, with the tag "graph of f" at its left. Above: an empty pair of axes tagged "graph of F". The definition "F(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt" sits at the top right and stays there. The traced curve is labeled "F(x)" in large type.
> Down on the bottom graph is a function, f of t. Shade the area under it from zero up to some point x. [pause]
> As x slides to the right, the shaded area grows. On the top graph, plot that area against x, and you get a new function: big F of x, a running total of the area. [pause]
> It's called an accumulation function. Today's question: how fast does it grow? [long pause]

## Accumulation functions
**On screen:** "F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt", with labels: "a: a fixed start", "x: the moving end, the input of F", "t: runs from a to x". Below, a small graph of f with the region from a to x shaded and "f(t)" labeled on the curve. Then "F(a) = ∫_a^a f(t) dt = 0".
> In symbols, big F of x is the integral from a to x of f of t, d t. We integrate f of t, starting at a and going up to x. [pause]
> The bottom limit, a, is fixed: it's where the total starts. The top limit, x, moves: it's the input of the new function F. [pause]
> Inside, the variable is t, so it doesn't get mixed up with the x on top. t runs from a up to x. [pause]
> And when x equals a, there's no region at all. F of a is zero. [long pause]

## Two values of F
**On screen:** The definition "F(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt with f(t) = 2t" at the top. The graph of f(t) = 2t, labeled "f(t) = 2t" on the line, with a tag "graph of f". First the triangle under it from 0 to 1 shades, with base 1 and height 2 marked: "F(1) = ∫_0^1 2t dt = ½(1)(2) = 1". Then the triangle from 0 to 2, base 2 and height 4: "F(2) = ∫_0^2 2t dt = ½(2)(4) = 4".
> Take a function we can work with: f of t equals two t, starting at zero. So F of x is the integral from zero to x of two t. [pause]
> F of one is the area under f from zero to one. That's a triangle with base one and height two: one half times one times two, which is one. [pause]
> F of two is the area from zero to two: a triangle with base two and height four. One half times two times four: four. [pause]
> Each input x gives one area, so each input gives one output. [long pause]

## F is a function
**On screen:** The graph of f stays on the bottom, tagged "graph of f". Above it, new axes tagged "graph of F". The points (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 4) and (3, 9) are plotted one at a time, each with a dashed line down to the triangle it came from. A curve through them is labeled "F(x) = x²" in large type.
> So F is a function of its own, with its own graph. Put it on top, and plot the values: F of zero is zero, F of one is one, F of two is four. [pause]
> F of three is a triangle with base three and height six: nine. [pause]
> These are the squares. For this f, big F of x is x squared. [pause]
> F has a graph, so it has slopes, so it has a derivative. What is F prime? [long pause]

## How fast F grows
**On screen:** Back to the first pair of graphs. On top, the graph of F, tagged "graph of F" and labeled "F(x)". Below, the graph of f, tagged "graph of f" and labeled "f(t)", shaded from 0 to x. A nudge: x moves to x + Δx, with a little double arrow labeled Δx under each graph. On F, the rise from F(x) to F(x + Δx) is marked ΔF. On f, the thin sliver from x to x + Δx shades in a second color, with its height marked f(x). Board, beside the graphs: "ΔF = area of the sliver"; "ΔF ≈ f(x) · Δx"; "ΔF/Δx ≈ f(x)"; "F′(x) = lim_{Δx→0} ΔF/Δx = f(x)".
> Back to the running total. On top is F. On the bottom is f. Nudge x a tiny bit to the right, by delta x. [pause]
> On the top graph, F rises by some amount, delta F. [pause]
> On the bottom graph, where does that change come from? F is the shaded area, so delta F is exactly the new sliver of area, from x to x plus delta x. [pause]
> For a small nudge, the sliver is almost a rectangle. Its height is f of x, and its width is delta x. So delta F is about f of x times delta x. [pause]
> Divide by delta x: the rise over the run of F is about f of x. [pause]
> As delta x goes to zero, the sliver gets thinner and the approximation becomes exact. The limit of delta F over delta x is F prime of x, and it equals f of x. [long pause]

## The Fundamental Theorem
**On screen:** A large boxed statement with a heading, "The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus": "If F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt, then F′(x) = f(x)." (f continuous.) A short fanfare plays. Underneath, in plain words: "The rate a running total of area grows is the height of the graph being added up." Then the check on the triangle example: "F(x) = x², F′(x) = 2x = f(x)."
> This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. It's called fundamental because the whole course rests on it. [pause]
> If big F of x is the integral from a to x of f of t, then F prime of x is f of x. [long pause]
> In words: a running total of area grows at a rate equal to the height of the graph you're adding up. [pause]
> Check it on the triangles. F of x was x squared, and the derivative of x squared is two x. That's f. [long pause]

## Two halves, joined
**On screen:** Two cards side by side. Left: "Derivatives (Units 2 to 5): slopes, rates of change." Right: "Integrals (Units 6.1 to 6.3): Riemann sums, areas, accumulation." Between them, two curved arrows: "integrate up to x" from left to right is drawn first, then "take the derivative" back again. Under them: "f → F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt → F′(x) = f(x)".
> Look at what the theorem says. Start with a function f. Integrate it up to x, then take the derivative. You get f back. [pause]
> So integrating and taking a derivative undo each other. They are inverse operations, the way adding three and subtracting three are. [pause]
> Until now, this course has had two halves. Derivatives: slopes and rates of change. And Riemann sums and integrals: areas and running totals. [pause]
> They looked like separate subjects. The Fundamental Theorem joins them into one. [long pause]

## Just substitute
**On screen:** The problem: "F(x) = ∫_1^x √(1 + t³) dt. Find F′(x) and F′(2)." The theorem stays in the corner: "F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt, so F′(x) = f(x)". Board: the top limit is just x; F′(x) = √(1 + x³); F′(2) = √(1 + 8) = √9 = 3.
> Big F of x is the integral from one to x of the square root of one plus t cubed. Find F prime of x, and F prime of two. [pause]
> The top limit is just x. So the theorem says F prime of x is the integrand with x in place of t. [pause]
> F prime of x is the square root of one plus x cubed. That's the whole job. [pause]
> F prime of two is the square root of one plus eight, the square root of nine: three. [long pause]

## Using the theorem
**On screen:** The problem: "Let g(x) = ∫_2^x (t² − 3t) dt. Find g′(x), g′(4) and g(2)." The theorem stays in the corner. Board: g′(x) = x² − 3x (FTC); g′(4) = 16 − 12 = 4; g(2) = ∫_2^2 (t² − 3t) dt = 0.
> The function doesn't have to be called F. Let g of x be the integral from two to x of t squared minus three t. Find g prime of x, g prime of four, and g of two.
> By the Fundamental Theorem, g prime of x is the integrand with x in place of t: x squared minus three x. [pause]
> So g prime of four is sixteen minus twelve: four.
> g of two is the integral from two to two. There's no region, so it's zero. [long pause]

## With the chain rule
**On screen:** The problem: "h(x) = ∫_1^{x²} cos t dt. Find h′(x)." The chain rule stays in the corner: "d/dx F(g(x)) = F′(g(x)) · g′(x)". Board, one part at a time: F(x) = ∫_1^x cos t dt, so F′(x) = cos x; the inner function g(x) = x², g′(x) = 2x; h(x) = F(g(x)); h′(x) = F′(g(x)) · g′(x); F′(g(x)) = cos(x²); h′(x) = cos(x²) · 2x = 2x cos(x²).
> Now the top limit is a function of x. h of x is the integral from one to x squared of cosine t. Find h prime of x. [pause]
> Name the pieces. Let big F of x be the integral from one to x of cosine t, with plain x on top. By the Fundamental Theorem, F prime of x is cosine x. [pause]
> The top limit, x squared, is the inner function. Call it g of x. g prime of x is two x. [pause]
> Then h of x is F of g of x: put g of x in as the top limit. It's a composition. [pause]
> The chain rule says h prime of x is F prime of g of x, times g prime of x. [pause]
> F prime of g of x: put x squared into cosine. Cosine of x squared. [pause]
> Times g prime, two x. h prime of x is two x cosine of x squared. [long pause]

## A function on top, in one step
**On screen:** A boxed formula: d/dx ∫_a^{u(x)} f(t) dt = f(u(x)) · u′(x). Beneath it, in words: "Put the top limit into f, then multiply by the top limit's derivative." Then the last example, checked against it: f(t) = cos t, u(x) = x², u′(x) = 2x, so h′(x) = cos(x²) · 2x.
> Every one of these goes the same way, so here's the Fundamental Theorem with a function on top. Call the top limit u of x. [pause]
> The derivative of the integral from a to u of x, of f of t, is f of u of x, times u prime of x. [pause]
> In words: put the top limit into f, then multiply by the top limit's derivative. [pause]
> Check it on the last example. f is cosine, and u of x is x squared. Cosine of x squared, times two x. The same answer. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt: a running total of area." The theorem: "If F(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt, then F′(x) = f(x)." "A function on top: d/dx ∫_a^{u(x)} f(t) dt = f(u(x)) · u′(x)." "F(a) = 0."
> An accumulation function, big F of x, is a running total of the area under f. The Fundamental Theorem says its derivative is f of x. If the top limit is a function u of x, put u into f and multiply by u prime. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A function on top
**On screen:** "k(x) = ∫_3^{sin x} e^{t²} dt. Find k′(x)." The function-on-top formula stays in the corner. Board: f(t) = e^{t²}, u(x) = sin x, u′(x) = cos x; f(u(x)) = e^{(sin x)²}; k′(x) = e^{sin² x} · cos x.
> k of x is the integral from three to sine x of e to the t squared. Find k prime of x. [pause]
> Name the pieces. f of t is e to the t squared. The top limit is u of x equals sine x, and u prime of x is cosine x. [pause]
> f of u of x: put sine x into f. e to the sine x, squared.
> Multiply by u prime: k prime of x is e to the sine squared x, times cosine x. [long pause]

## Example 2: x on the bottom
**On screen:** "m(x) = ∫_x^5 (t⁴ + 1) dt. Find m′(x)." The theorem stays in the corner. Board: m(x) = −∫_5^x (t⁴ + 1) dt; m′(x) = −(x⁴ + 1).
> m of x is the integral from x to five of t to the fourth plus one. Find m prime of x. [pause]
> The theorem wants x on top. Swapping the limits changes the sign: m of x is negative the integral from five to x. [pause]
> Now the theorem applies: m prime of x is negative x to the fourth plus one. [long pause]

## Example 3: x at both ends
**On screen:** "R(x) = ∫_{3x}^{x²} √(t⁴ + 1) dt. Find R′(x)." The function-on-top formula stays in the corner. Board: R(x) = ∫_{3x}^0 √(t⁴ + 1) dt + ∫_0^{x²} √(t⁴ + 1) dt; = −∫_0^{3x} √(t⁴ + 1) dt + ∫_0^{x²} √(t⁴ + 1) dt; first piece: −√((3x)⁴ + 1) · 3 = −3√(81x⁴ + 1); second piece: √((x²)⁴ + 1) · 2x = 2x√(x⁸ + 1); R′(x) = 2x√(x⁸ + 1) − 3√(81x⁴ + 1).
> R of x is the integral from three x to x squared of the square root of t to the fourth plus one. Find R prime of x. [pause]
> Both limits move. Split the integral at a fixed number, such as zero: from three x to zero, plus from zero to x squared. [pause]
> Flip the first piece so x is on top: negative the integral from zero to three x. [pause]
> The first piece's derivative: the integrand at three x, times the derivative of three x. Negative three times the square root of eighty-one x to the fourth plus one.
> The second piece: the integrand at x squared, times two x. Two x times the square root of x to the eighth plus one. [pause]
> Add them: R prime of x is two x root x to the eighth plus one, minus three root eighty-one x to the fourth plus one. [long pause]
