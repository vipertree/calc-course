# 6.4 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and Accumulation Functions

## A running total
**On screen:** The graph of a positive rate f(t) on top. A vertical line at t = x slides to the right, and the area under f from 0 to x shades in as it goes. Below, a second graph is drawn point by point: its height at each x is the shaded area so far. It's labeled A(x).
> Here's a rate, f of t. Shade the area under it from zero up to some point x. [pause]
> As x slides to the right, the shaded area grows. Plot that area against x, and you get a new function: a running total. [pause]
> It's called an accumulation function. Today's question: how fast does it grow? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.4 · The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and accumulation functions." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Accumulation functions
**On screen:** "A(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt", with labels: "a: a fixed starting point", "x: the moving end, the input", "t: the variable you integrate over". Below, a small graph shows the region from a to x shaded. Then "A(a) = ∫_a^a f(t) dt = 0".
> In symbols, the accumulation function is the integral from a to x of f of t, d t. [pause]
> The bottom limit, a, is fixed: it's where the total starts. The top limit, x, moves: it's the input of the new function. [pause]
> Inside, the variable is t, so it doesn't get mixed up with the x on top. t just runs from a to x. [pause]
> And when x equals a, there's no region at all. A of a is zero. [long pause]

## How fast the area grows
**On screen:** The shaded region from a to x, then a thin sliver added from x to x + h, outlined. The sliver is nearly a rectangle with height f(x) and width h. Board: A(x + h) − A(x) ≈ f(x) · h; (A(x + h) − A(x))/h ≈ f(x); as h → 0: A′(x) = f(x). Then the boxed theorem: "If f is continuous, then d/dx ∫_a^x f(t) dt = f(x)."
> Move x a tiny bit to the right, to x plus h. The area grows by a thin sliver. [pause]
> The sliver is almost a rectangle: its height is f of x and its width is h. So the change in area is about f of x times h.
> Divide by h: the average rate the area grows is about f of x. [pause]
> Let h go to zero, and that becomes exact. The derivative of the accumulation function is f of x. [pause]
> That's the first part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. The rate a running total grows is the height of the graph you're adding up. [long pause]

## With the chain rule
**On screen:** "d/dx ∫_1^{x²} cos t dt". The top limit x² is boxed and labeled "inside function u = x²". Board: = cos(x²) · d/dx(x²); = cos(x²) · 2x = 2x cos(x²). Then the general rule: "d/dx ∫_a^{u(x)} f(t) dt = f(u(x)) · u′(x)".
> Now let the top limit be a function of x. Take the integral from one to x squared of cosine t. [pause]
> The top limit is an inside function, u equals x squared. So this is a composition, and the chain rule applies.
> Put the top limit into the integrand: cosine of x squared. Then multiply by the derivative of the top limit, two x. [pause]
> The derivative is two x cosine of x squared. In general: f of the top limit, times the derivative of the top limit. [long pause]

## Using the theorem
**On screen:** The problem: "Let g(x) = ∫_2^x (t² − 3t) dt. Find g′(4) and g(2)." Board: g′(x) = x² − 3x (FTC); g′(4) = 16 − 12 = 4; g(2) = ∫_2^2 (t² − 3t) dt = 0.
> Let g of x be the integral from two to x of t squared minus three t. Find g prime of four, and g of two. [try it]
> By the Fundamental Theorem, g prime of x is the integrand with x in place of t: x squared minus three x. [pause]
> So g prime of four is sixteen minus twelve: four.
> g of two is the integral from two to two. There's no region, so it's zero. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "A(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt: a running total." "A′(x) = f(x)." "Top limit u(x): f(u(x)) · u′(x)." "A(a) = 0."
> An accumulation function is a running total of area. Its derivative is the function you're adding up. If the top limit is a function of x, multiply by its derivative. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Straight from the theorem
**On screen:** "Find d/dx ∫_0^x √(1 + t³) dt." Board: the top limit is x; the integrand at x: √(1 + x³); answer √(1 + x³).
> Find the derivative of the integral from zero to x of the square root of one plus t cubed. [pause]
> The top limit is just x, so there's no chain rule. [pause]
> Put x into the integrand: the square root of one plus x cubed. That's the derivative. [long pause]

## Example 2: A function on top
**On screen:** "Find d/dx ∫_3^{sin x} e^{t²} dt." Board: u = sin x, u′ = cos x; integrand at u: e^{(sin x)²}; answer e^{sin² x} cos x.
> Find the derivative of the integral from three to sine x of e to the t squared. [pause]
> The top limit is u equals sine x, and u prime is cosine x. [pause]
> Put the top limit into the integrand: e to the sine x squared.
> Multiply by u prime: e to the sine squared x, times cosine x. [long pause]

## Example 3: x on the bottom
**On screen:** "Find d/dx ∫_x^5 (t⁴ + 1) dt." Board: ∫_x^5 (t⁴ + 1) dt = −∫_5^x (t⁴ + 1) dt; d/dx: −(x⁴ + 1).
> Find the derivative of the integral from x to five of t to the fourth plus one. [pause]
> The theorem wants x on top. Swapping the limits changes the sign: this is negative the integral from five to x. [pause]
> Now the theorem applies: the derivative is negative x to the fourth plus one. [long pause]
