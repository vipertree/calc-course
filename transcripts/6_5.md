# 6.5 Interpreting the Behavior of Accumulation Functions Involving Area

## Rising and falling totals
**On screen:** Stacked graphs on one x-scale, each tagged with which graph it is and labeled in large type next to its curve. On the bottom, "graph of f": a rate f(t) that is positive, then negative, then positive again; the region between f and the axis is shaded as a marker x slides right, green above the axis and red below. On top, "graph of g": g(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt is traced: it climbs while the shading is green and falls while it is red. The definition "g(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt" stays at the top right. Dashed lines join each zero of f to a peak or valley of g.
> On the bottom is a rate, f. On top, its running total, g of x, the integral from zero to x of f. [pause]
> While f is above the axis, each new sliver of area is positive, so the total climbs. While f is below, the slivers are negative, so the total falls. [pause]
> Where f crosses the axis, the total turns around. [long pause]

## Read g from f
**On screen:** "g(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt, so g′ = f and g″ = f′" at the top, where it stays. Below it, a two-column chart in the same order as Topic 5.9's: the function g on the left, the graph of f (which is g′) on the right. Built row by row: "g increasing | f positive"; "g decreasing | f negative"; "g has a relative max | f changes sign, + to −"; "g has a relative min | f changes sign, − to +"; "g concave up | f increasing"; "g concave down | f decreasing"; "g has an inflection point | f has a max or min". At the bottom, a note: "Topic 5.9's chart, one level up: g plays f, and f plays f′."
> Because g prime is f, everything we learned about reading a derivative applies here. f plays the role of g prime, and the slope of f is g double prime. [pause]
> Where f is positive, g is increasing. Where f is negative, g is decreasing. [pause]
> Where f changes sign, g has a relative extremum: plus to minus is a max, minus to plus is a min. And it has to be a change of sign, not just a touch. [pause]
> Where f is increasing, g is concave up. Where f is decreasing, g is concave down. And where f has a peak or a valley, g has an inflection point. [pause]
> It's the same chart as topic five point nine, one level up. [long pause]

## Reading the graph of f
**On screen:** The problem: "The graph of f on [−4, 6] is a semicircle and two line segments. Let g(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt." The figure: the upper semicircle of radius 2 centered at (−2, 0); a segment from (0, 0) down to (2, −2); a segment from (2, −2) up to (6, 2). Parts appear one at a time. (a) Find g(2), g(4), g(6) and g(−4). Board: g(2) = −½(2)(2) = −2; g(4) = −2 − 2 = −4; g(6) = −4 + 2 = −2; g(−4) = −∫_{−4}^{0} f(t) dt = −½π(2)² = −2π. (b) Where does g have relative extrema? Board: relative extrema of g: where g′ = f changes sign; f changes + to − at x = 0: rel. max; − to + at x = 4: rel. min. (c) Absolute max and min of g on [−4, 6]? Board: candidates g(−4) = −2π ≈ −6.28, g(0) = 0, g(4) = −4, g(6) = −2; abs max 0 at x = 0; abs min −2π at x = −4. (d) Inflection points of g? Board: inflection points of g: where g″ = f′ changes sign; f changes from increasing to decreasing at x = −2 and from decreasing to increasing at x = 2: inflection at x = −2 and x = 2. On the graph, each region shades as its area is used, and the turning points are marked.
> The graph of f is a semicircle and two line segments. g of x is the integral from zero to x of f. First: find g of two, four, six, and negative four. [pause]
> g of two is the area from zero to two, and it's below the axis, so it counts as negative: a triangle, one half times two times two. Negative two. [pause]
> From two to four is another triangle below the axis, area two. So g of four is negative four.
> From four to six, a triangle above the axis, area two. So g of six is negative two. [pause]
> g of negative four runs backward, from zero to negative four. Swapping the limits makes it the negative of the semicircle's area, one half pi times two squared. So g of negative four is negative two pi. [pause]
> Next: where does g have relative extrema? [try it]
> g prime is f. f changes from positive to negative at zero, so g has a relative maximum there. It changes from negative to positive at four, so g has a relative minimum there. [pause]
> Now the absolute maximum and minimum on the whole interval. [try it]
> Use the candidates test: the endpoints and the critical points. g of negative four is about negative six point two eight, g of zero is zero, g of four is negative four, and g of six is negative two. [pause]
> The absolute maximum is zero, at x equals zero. The absolute minimum is negative two pi, at the endpoint x equals negative four. Not at the relative minimum. [pause]
> Last: the inflection points of g. [try it]
> g double prime is the slope of f. f rises to its peak at negative two, then falls until two, then rises again. So the concavity of g changes at negative two and at two. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "g(x) = ∫_a^x f(t) dt, so g′ = f and g″ = f′." "Increasing, decreasing, relative extrema of g: the sign of f." "Concavity and inflection points of g: f rising or falling." "Values of g: areas, with area below the axis counted as negative. Backward limits flip the sign."
> When g of x is the integral from a to x of f, g prime is f and g double prime is f prime. Where g increases and where it has extrema come from the sign of f. Its concavity comes from whether f rises or falls. And its values are areas, with area below the axis counted as negative. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A formula for f
**On screen:** "g(x) = ∫_0^x (t² − 4) dt. Find where g is increasing and where it has relative extrema." On the right, a number line for g′ with −2 and 2 marked, filling in as tested. Board: increasing: g′(x) > 0; relative extrema: g′ changes sign; g′(x) = x² − 4 (FTC); x² − 4 = 0, (x − 2)(x + 2) = 0, x = ±2; g′(−3) = 9 − 4 = 5 > 0; g′(0) = −4 < 0; g′(3) = 5 > 0; increasing on (−∞, −2) and (2, ∞); rel. max at x = −2, rel. min at x = 2.
> g of x is the integral from zero to x of t squared minus four. Where is g increasing, and where does it have relative extrema? [pause]
> Before computing anything, stop and ask what we're looking for. g is increasing where g prime is positive. g has a relative extremum where g prime changes sign. So the job is to find g prime and its signs. [pause]
> By the Fundamental Theorem, g prime of x is x squared minus four. [pause]
> Set it equal to zero: x squared minus four factors as x minus two times x plus two, so x is two or negative two.
> Test negative three: nine minus four, five. Positive.
> Test zero: negative four. Negative.
> Test three: five again. Positive. [pause]
> So g is increasing for x less than negative two and for x greater than two. g prime goes from positive to negative at negative two, a relative maximum, and from negative to positive at two, a relative minimum. [long pause]

## Example 2: A tangent line to g
**On screen:** "g(x) = ∫_1^x √(t + 3) dt. Write an equation for the line tangent to the graph of g at x = 1." Board: point: g(1) = ∫_1^1 √(t + 3) dt = 0, so the point is (1, 0); g′(x) = √(x + 3); slope: m = g′(1) = √4 = 2; then, set aside and labeled, "point (1, 0), slope m = 2"; y − 0 = 2(x − 1); y = 2(x − 1).
> g of x is the integral from one to x of the square root of t plus three. Write an equation for the line tangent to the graph of g at x equals one. [pause]
> A tangent line needs a point and a slope. The point: g of one is the integral from one to one, zero. So the point is one, zero. [pause]
> The slope: g prime of x is the square root of x plus three, so the slope m is g prime of one, the square root of four, two. [pause]
> Set them aside: the point is one, zero, and the slope is m equals two. [pause]
> Put them into point-slope form: y minus zero equals two times x minus one. So y equals two times x minus one. [long pause]

## Example 3: Concavity from a graph
**On screen:** "The graph of f is shown: it rises from (0, −2) to a peak at (3, 4), then falls to (6, 1). Let g(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt. On what intervals is g concave up? Concave down?" The figure: two line segments, (0, −2) to (3, 4) and (3, 4) to (6, 1). Board: concave up: g″ > 0; concave down: g″ < 0; g′ = f (FTC), so g″ = f′; look at the slope of f; f increasing on (0, 3): g concave up; f decreasing on (3, 6): g concave down; inflection at x = 3.
> The graph of f rises from zero, negative two up to a peak at three, four, then falls to six, one. g is the integral from zero to x of f. Where is g concave up, and where concave down? [pause]
> First, stop and ask what we're looking for. g is concave up where g double prime is positive, and concave down where g double prime is negative. [pause]
> By the Fundamental Theorem, g prime is just f. So g double prime is f prime. [pause]
> In other words, look at the slope of f. Ignore whether f is above or below the axis: only whether it's rising or falling matters. [pause]
> f rises from zero to three, so g is concave up there.
> f falls from three to six, so g is concave down there, with an inflection point at three. [long pause]
