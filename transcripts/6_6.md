# 6.6 Applying Properties of Definite Integrals

## Pieces of area
**On screen:** A curve over [a, c] with a point b in between. The region from a to b shades blue, the region from b to c shades orange, and together they fill the whole region from a to c. Labels: "∫_a^b f dx + ∫_b^c f dx = ∫_a^c f dx".
> Integrals are signed areas, so they follow the rules that areas follow. [pause]
> The area from a to b, plus the area from b to c, is the area from a to c. [pause]
> A handful of rules like that let you work with integrals you can't compute directly, as long as you know some pieces. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.6 · Applying properties of definite integrals." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## The properties
**On screen:** A list builds, each line with a tiny picture: "∫_a^a f(x) dx = 0" (a region with no width); "∫_b^a f(x) dx = −∫_a^b f(x) dx" (an arrow running backward); "∫_a^b k f(x) dx = k ∫_a^b f(x) dx" (the region stretched taller); "∫_a^b [f(x) ± g(x)] dx = ∫_a^b f(x) dx ± ∫_a^b g(x) dx"; "∫_a^b f dx + ∫_b^c f dx = ∫_a^c f dx" (two pieces joined).
> First, an integral from a to a has no width, so it's zero. [pause]
> Running the limits backward, from b down to a, flips the sign. [pause]
> A constant multiple can come out front: stretching the graph by k stretches the area by k. [pause]
> The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals, and the same for a difference. [pause]
> And integrals over neighboring intervals add up to the integral over the whole interval. [long pause]

## Using given values
**On screen:** Given: "∫_1^4 f(x) dx = 6" and "∫_1^4 g(x) dx = −2". Board: ∫_1^4 [3f(x) − 2g(x)] dx = 3∫_1^4 f(x) dx − 2∫_1^4 g(x) dx; = 3(6) − 2(−2); = 18 + 4 = 22. Then: ∫_4^1 f(x) dx = −6.
> Suppose the integral of f from one to four is six, and the integral of g over the same interval is negative two. Find the integral of three f minus two g. [pause]
> Split the difference, and pull out the constants: three times the integral of f, minus two times the integral of g.
> That's three times six, minus two times negative two. [pause]
> Eighteen plus four: twenty-two.
> And the integral of f from four down to one? Backward limits flip the sign: negative six. [long pause]

## Splitting an interval
**On screen:** The problem: "∫_0^5 f(x) dx = 10 and ∫_3^5 f(x) dx = 4. Find (a) ∫_0^3 f(x) dx, (b) ∫_3^0 2f(x) dx, (c) ∫_0^3 [f(x) + 1] dx." A number line from 0 to 5 shows the pieces [0, 3] and [3, 5]. Board: (a) ∫_0^3 f + ∫_3^5 f = ∫_0^5 f, so ∫_0^3 f + 4 = 10, ∫_0^3 f = 6; (b) ∫_3^0 2f = −2∫_0^3 f = −2(6) = −12; (c) ∫_0^3 f dx + ∫_0^3 1 dx = 6 + 3 = 9.
> The integral of f from zero to five is ten, and from three to five it's four. Find the three integrals shown. [pause]
> For the first, split zero to five at three. The piece from zero to three plus the piece from three to five is the whole: the integral from zero to three plus four equals ten. [pause]
> So the integral from zero to three is six.
> For the second, the limits run backward, which flips the sign, and the two comes out front. Negative two times six: negative twelve. [pause]
> For the third, split the sum. The integral of f from zero to three is six. The integral of one from zero to three is a rectangle of height one and width three: three. [pause]
> Six plus three: nine. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "∫_a^a = 0. Backward limits flip the sign. Constants come out. Sums split. Neighboring intervals add."
> Same interval: zero. Backward: flip the sign. Constants come out, sums split, and neighboring intervals add. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: An absolute value
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_{−2}^{4} |x| dx." On the right, the V-shaped graph of y = |x| with two triangles shaded: one over [−2, 0], one over [0, 4]. Board: split at 0: ∫_{−2}^{0} |x| dx + ∫_0^4 |x| dx; left triangle: ½(2)(2) = 2; right triangle: ½(4)(4) = 8; total 2 + 8 = 10.
> Evaluate the integral from negative two to four of the absolute value of x. [pause]
> The graph has a corner at zero, so split the interval there. [pause]
> From negative two to zero, the region is a triangle with base two and height two: area two.
> From zero to four, a triangle with base four and height four: area eight. [pause]
> Both are above the axis, so the integral is two plus eight: ten. [long pause]

## Example 2: A piecewise function
**On screen:** "f(x) = 2 for x < 1 and f(x) = x + 1 for x ≥ 1. Evaluate ∫_{−1}^{3} f(x) dx." On the right, the graph: flat at 2 from −1 to 1, then the line y = x + 1 from (1, 2) to (3, 4); a rectangle and a trapezoid shaded. Board: split at 1; ∫_{−1}^{1} 2 dx = 2(2) = 4; ∫_1^3 (x + 1) dx = ((2 + 4)/2)(2) = 6; total 4 + 6 = 10.
> f is two for x less than one, and x plus one for x at least one. Evaluate the integral of f from negative one to three. [pause]
> The rule changes at one, so split there. [pause]
> From negative one to one, f is two: a rectangle, two tall and two wide. Area four.
> From one to three, f is x plus one, which runs from two up to four: a trapezoid. The average height is three, times the width two: six. [pause]
> The total is four plus six: ten. [long pause]

## Example 3: Find the constant
**On screen:** "∫_2^5 f(x) dx = 7 and ∫_2^5 [f(x) + k] dx = 16. Find k." Board: ∫_2^5 f(x) dx + ∫_2^5 k dx = 16; 7 + k(5 − 2) = 16; 3k = 9; k = 3.
> The integral of f from two to five is seven, and the integral of f plus k over the same interval is sixteen. Find k. [pause]
> Split the sum: the integral of f, plus the integral of the constant k. [pause]
> The integral of k from two to five is a rectangle, k tall and three wide: three k. So seven plus three k equals sixteen.
> Subtract seven: three k equals nine. [pause]
> So k is three. [long pause]
