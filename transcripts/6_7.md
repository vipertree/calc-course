# 6.7 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and Definite Integrals

## The area we want
**On screen:** The graph of y = x² from 0 to 3, labeled "f(x) = x²" on the curve. Right-endpoint rectangles under it, first 6, then 24, then 60, getting thinner. Beside the graph: "area = lim_{n→∞} Σ_{k=1}^{n} (3k/n)² · (3/n)", then under it in gray: "hard to compute". Then the rectangles fade into a solid shaded region, and above the area formula the words "the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: area as another function, F".
> Here's the area under x squared, from zero to three. We'd like its exact value. [pause]
> Riemann sums get us there: make the rectangles thinner and thinner, and take the limit of the sums. [pause]
> Writing that limit down is easy. Actually computing it is hard. [pause]
> Fortunately, there's another way to think about area under the curve: as the values of another function, big F. We have that thanks to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. [long pause]

**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.7 · The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and definite integrals." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Big F, again
**On screen:** Two graphs on one x-scale, as in topic 6.4. Below: the graph of f, tagged "graph of f", labeled "f(t)". Above: the graph of F, tagged "graph of F", labeled "F(x)". The definition "F(x) = ∫_0^x f(t) dt" sits at the top right, then "F′(x) = f(x)" under it. The area under f from 0 to x is shaded. A thin sliver under f, from x to x + Δx, shades in a second color, with a matching small rise ΔF on the graph of F.
> Remember big F from topic six point four. It's the accumulation function. Here F of x is the integral from zero to x of f of t, a running total of the area under f. [pause]
> The Fundamental Theorem said F prime of x is f of x. Each small change in F is a thin sliver of area under f. [long pause]

## Where F starts and ends
**On screen:** The same two graphs. On the bottom graph, the region under f from a to b shades in, labeled "∫_a^b f(t) dt". On the top graph, a dot at x = a with a dashed level line labeled "F(a): where F starts", a dot at x = b with a dashed level line labeled "F(b): where F ends", and a vertical bracket between the levels labeled "F(b) − F(a): how much F changed". Board: "the change in F = the area that f accumulates"; "∫_a^b f(t) dt = F(b) − F(a)".
> Now go all the way from a to b. Down on f, the slivers pile up into the whole area from a to b. [pause]
> Up on F, all those small changes add up to F's total change. Look where F starts, at a, and where F ends, at b. [pause]
> The change in F, F of b minus F of a, is the net area that f accumulates from a to b. [long pause]

## Side by side
**On screen:** Two columns. Left, headed "In general": "the integrand f(x)"; "F: a function with F′(x) = f(x)"; "where F starts: F(a)"; "where F ends: F(b)"; "∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)". Right, headed "For ∫_0^3 x² dx": "the integrand x²"; "F(x) = x³/3, since d/dx (x³/3) = x²"; "F(0) = 0"; "F(3) = 27/3 = 9"; "∫_0^3 x² dx = 9 − 0 = 9". Each right-hand line appears next to its left-hand line.
> So here's the question to ask about any integral. What is big F here? That is, what is this integrand the derivative of? [pause]
> Take the area under x squared from zero to three. What has derivative x squared? Pause and think. [try it]
> x cubed over three. Its derivative is three x squared over three, which is x squared. [pause]
> Where does F start? F of zero is zero. Where does F end? F of three is twenty-seven over three, which is nine. [pause]
> The difference is nine minus zero: nine. That's the exact area we wanted, with no limit of sums. [long pause]

## Where plus C comes from
**On screen:** Axes with four curves: y = x³/3, y = x³/3 + 2, y = x³/3 − 1 and y = x³/3 + 5, each labeled with its C on its flat left end. Short tangent lines at x = 2 on every curve, all parallel: "every one has derivative x²". Beside them a table with columns C, F(3), F(0), F(3) − F(0): rows 0 | 9 | 0 | 9; 2 | 11 | 2 | 9; −1 | 8 | −1 | 9; 5 | 14 | 5 | 9. The last column is highlighted. Then, beneath: "(F(b) + C) − (F(a) + C) = F(b) − F(a)".
> x cubed over three isn't the only choice. Add any constant: plus two, minus one, plus five. [pause]
> Adding a constant shifts the graph up or down without changing its slope. So every one of these has derivative x squared. Any of them could be our F, so we write F of x as x cubed over three, plus C. [pause]
> Now find the area with each one. With plus two, F of three is eleven and F of zero is two. The difference is nine. [pause]
> With minus one: eight minus negative one, nine. With plus five: fourteen minus five, nine. [pause]
> The C is in both values, so it cancels when we subtract. Any C gives the same change, so any F with the right derivative works. [long pause]

## The Fundamental Theorem, part 2
**On screen:** A large boxed statement with a heading, "The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, part 2": "If F′(x) = f(x) on [a, b], then ∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)." (f continuous.) A short fanfare plays. Under it, in plain words: "The area under f from a to b is how much F changes from a to b."
> This is the second part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. [pause]
> If F prime of x is f of x on the interval from a to b, then the integral from a to b of f of x is F of b minus F of a. [long pause]
> In words: the area under f from a to b is how much F changes from a to b. [long pause]

## Why it matters
**On screen:** Left, grayed out: the limit of Riemann sums from the opening, with "a limit of sums". Right, three numbered steps: "1. Find an antiderivative F (F′ = f)." "2. Evaluate F at the two endpoints." "3. Subtract: F(b) − F(a)." Under the steps: "∫_0^3 x² dx = 9 − 0 = 9".
> This is a huge shortcut. To find the integral of a function, we don't need a limit of sums anymore. [pause]
> Find an antiderivative, a function whose derivative is the integrand. Evaluate it at the two endpoints. Subtract. [pause]
> So the whole job comes down to one skill: running derivative rules backward. [long pause]

## Reversing the power rule
**On screen:** Left: "Power rule: d/dx xⁿ = n xⁿ⁻¹", with two numbered steps under it: "1. multiply by the power"; "2. subtract 1 from the exponent". An example underneath: x³ → 3x³ → 3x². Right, built after a pause: "Reversed: ∫ xⁿ dx", with "1. add 1 to the exponent"; "2. divide by the new exponent". Its example: x² → x³ → x³/3. Then the rule: "xⁿ has antiderivative xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1), n ≠ −1". A check line: "d/dx (x³/3) = 3x²/3 = x²". Then the prompt "an antiderivative of x⁴?" and, after the pause, "x⁵/5".
> Start with the power rule. It has two steps: multiply by the power out front, then subtract one from the exponent. x cubed becomes three x squared. [pause]
> Before going on, pause and think: how would you run those steps backward? [try it]
> Undo them in reverse order. Add one to the exponent, then divide by the new exponent. x squared becomes x cubed, then x cubed over three. [pause]
> Check it with the power rule: the derivative of x cubed over three is three x squared over three, which is x squared. [pause]
> In general, x to the n has antiderivative x to the n plus one, over n plus one. That works for every power except negative one, where we'd divide by zero. [pause]
> Your turn: what's an antiderivative of x to the fourth? [try it]
> Add one to get x to the fifth, then divide by five: x to the fifth over five. [long pause]

## More rules backward
**On screen:** A two-column chart with plenty of room between rows: "f(x)" and "an antiderivative F(x)". Rows: xⁿ (n ≠ −1) | xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1); cos x | sin x; sin x | −cos x; eˣ | eˣ; 1/x | ln|x|; sec²x | tan x. Each right-hand cell shows up only after a pause, and the derivative facts it comes from show in the corner: "d/dx sin x = cos x", "d/dx cos x = −sin x", "d/dx eˣ = eˣ", "d/dx ln|x| = 1/x", "d/dx tan x = sec²x".
> Every derivative rule you know runs backward the same way. Pause and think about each one before it appears. [pause]
> What has derivative cosine x? [try it]
> Sine x. [pause]
> What has derivative sine x? Careful with the sign. [try it]
> The derivative of cosine is negative sine, so the derivative of negative cosine is sine. The answer is negative cosine x. [pause]
> e to the x is its own derivative, so it's its own antiderivative. [pause]
> One over x comes from the natural log of the absolute value of x. That's the power the power rule skips. [pause]
> And secant squared comes from tangent. [long pause]

## Evaluating an integral
**On screen:** The problem: "Evaluate ∫_1^4 (3x² − 2x) dx." The theorem stays in the corner: "∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F′ = f". Board: F(x) = x³ − x² (check: F′(x) = 3x² − 2x); F(4) = 4³ − 4² = 64 − 16 = 48; F(1) = 1³ − 1² = 0; ∫_1^4 (3x² − 2x) dx = F(4) − F(1) = 48 − 0 = 48. Then: "from now on: [x³ − x²]_1^4"; = (4³ − 4²) − (1³ − 1²) = 48 − 0 = 48.
> Evaluate the integral from one to four of three x squared minus two x. [pause]
> First, what's big F? Reverse the power rule on each term. Three x squared comes from x cubed, and two x comes from x squared. So F of x is x cubed minus x squared. [pause]
> Where F ends: F of four is four cubed minus four squared, sixty-four minus sixteen. Forty-eight. [pause]
> Where F starts: F of one is one cubed minus one squared, which is zero. [pause]
> The integral is F of four minus F of one: forty-eight minus zero. Forty-eight. [long pause]
> Writing out F of four and F of one every time gets long, so there's shorter notation. Put the antiderivative in square brackets, with the limits on the bracket: one at the bottom, four at the top. [pause]
> It means the same thing: the top value minus the bottom value. Four cubed minus four squared, minus one cubed minus one squared. [pause]
> Forty-eight minus zero, forty-eight. This is how we'll write it from now on. [long pause]

## Net change
**On screen:** "∫_a^b f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a)": "the integral of a rate of change is the net change." Then "f(b) = f(a) + ∫_a^b f′(x) dx": "where it ends = where it starts + how much it changed". A small picture: a level starting at f(a), with the area under f′ added on top to reach f(b).
> The theorem works for any function and its derivative. With f prime as the integrand, the F is f itself: the integral of f prime from a to b is f of b minus f of a. [pause]
> In words: the integral of a rate of change is the net change in the amount. That's what we saw with areas in topic six point one, and now we can compute it. [pause]
> Rearranged, it says where you end equals where you start, plus how much you changed: f of b equals f of a plus the integral of f prime. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "∫_a^b f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F′ = f." "[F(x)]_a^b = F(b) − F(a)." "Reverse the power rule: add 1 to the exponent, then divide by the new exponent." "∫_a^b f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a): net change."
> To evaluate a definite integral, find an antiderivative, plug in the top and the bottom, and subtract. Reverse the power rule by adding one to the exponent and dividing by the new exponent. And the integral of a rate is the net change. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A trig integral
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_0^π sin x dx." The theorem stays in the corner. Board: F(x) = −cos x (check: F′(x) = sin x); [−cos x]_0^π = −cos π − (−cos 0); = −(−1) − (−1); = 1 + 1 = 2.
> Evaluate the integral from zero to pi of sine x. [pause]
> What has derivative sine x? Negative cosine x. [pause]
> At the top: negative cosine of pi. Cosine of pi is negative one, so that's positive one.
> At the bottom: negative cosine of zero, which is negative one. [pause]
> Top minus bottom: one minus negative one. Two. [long pause]

## Example 2: A root
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_1^9 √x dx." The theorem stays in the corner. Board: √x = x^{1/2}; add 1: x^{3/2}, divide by 3/2: x^{3/2}/(3/2) = (2/3)x^{3/2}; [(2/3)x^{3/2}]_1^9 = (2/3)(9^{3/2}) − (2/3)(1^{3/2}); = (2/3)(27) − 2/3 = 18 − 2/3 = 52/3.
> Evaluate the integral from one to nine of the square root of x. [pause]
> Write the root as a power: x to the one half. [pause]
> Add one to the exponent: x to the three halves. Then divide by three halves, which is the same as multiplying by two thirds. [pause]
> At the top: two thirds times nine to the three halves. The square root of nine is three, cubed is twenty-seven. Two thirds of twenty-seven is eighteen.
> At the bottom: two thirds times one, two thirds. [pause]
> Eighteen minus two thirds: fifty-two thirds. [long pause]

## Example 3: Net change
**On screen:** "A plant is 10 cm tall at t = 1 week and grows at h′(t) = 6t − 2 cm per week. How tall is it at t = 3?" Board: h(3) = h(1) + ∫_1^3 h′(t) dt; ∫_1^3 (6t − 2) dt = [3t² − 2t]_1^3; = (27 − 6) − (3 − 2) = 21 − 1 = 20; h(3) = 10 + 20 = 30 cm.
> A plant is ten centimeters tall at week one and grows at h prime of t equals six t minus two centimeters per week. How tall is it at week three? [pause]
> Where it ends is where it starts plus how much it grew: h of three equals h of one plus the integral of h prime from one to three. [pause]
> An antiderivative of six t minus two is three t squared minus two t.
> At three: twenty-seven minus six, twenty-one. At one: three minus two, one. The plant grew twenty centimeters. [pause]
> So h of three is ten plus twenty: thirty centimeters. [long pause]
