# 6.9 Integrating Using Substitution

## The chain rule, backward
**On screen:** "d/dx sin(x²) = cos(x²) · 2x" with the inside x² underbraced and labeled "inside". Then the same line read right to left: "∫ 2x cos(x²) dx = sin(x²) + C". The factor 2x is highlighted in both lines: "the derivative of the inside". Then, under both: "essentially a reverse chain rule".
> By the chain rule, the derivative of sine of x squared is cosine of x squared, times two x: the derivative of the inside. [pause]
> Read that backward. The integral of two x cosine of x squared, d x, is sine of x squared, plus C. [pause]
> Notice the inside function, x squared, and its derivative, two x, sitting right there as a factor. [pause]
> In this lesson, we'll learn what is essentially a reverse chain rule. It isn't as simple as running the chain rule backward, though. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.9 · Integrating using substitution." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## Substitution
**On screen:** Heading "Substitution", with "(often called u-substitution)" under it. Then the goal, boxed: "Goal: rewrite the integral as a simple function of u, one we know how to integrate." Then the integral "∫ 2x cos(x²) dx", with its dx circled: "every integral has its dx".
> Here's the method. We call it substitution, or often u-substitution, because u is the letter we usually use. [pause]
> The goal is to rewrite the integral as a simple function of u, something we know how to integrate. [pause]
> Take the integral of two x cosine of x squared, d x. Every integral ends with its d x, and here that matters a lot: the d x gets substituted too. [long pause]

## Call the inside u
**On screen:** "∫ 2x cos(x²) dx" at the top, the x² underbraced "inside". Beside it: "u = x²". Under it, the x² is replaced right away: "∫ 2x cos(u) dx", the u in color. Labels point at the 2x and the dx ("still x") and at the u ("u"): "a mix of x's and u's". Then: "We want the whole integral in u, even the dx." Then: "dx becomes du: accumulate change in u", and "so: take the derivative of u".
> Start by calling the inside function u. Let u equal x squared. [pause]
> Substitute it right away: the x squared becomes u. Now we have the integral of two x cosine u, d x. [pause]
> That's a mix of x's and u's, so we're not done. We want the whole integral in terms of u, so the two x has to go, and even the d x. [pause]
> Think of the d x turning into a d u: instead of accumulating change in x, we'll accumulate change in u. That's why we take the derivative of u. [long pause]

## Finding du
**On screen:** "u = x²" at the top. Then "du/dx = 2x", with the words "a slight change in u for every slight change in x". Then both sides times dx: "du = 2x · dx", with the words "a slight change in u is 2x times a slight change in x". The two forms sit side by side, the second boxed: "du = 2x dx". In gray: "You could work with du/dx = 2x, but du = 2x dx lines up with the pieces of the integral."
> Take the derivative of u. d u d x equals two x. [pause]
> d u d x means a slight change in u for every slight change in x. Here that ratio is two x. [pause]
> So a slight change in u is two x times the slight change in x. Multiply both sides by d x: d u equals two x d x. [pause]
> You could keep d u d x equals two x and solve it for what you need. Writing d u equals two x d x is usually nicer, because it lines up with the pieces of the integral. [long pause]

## Rewrite in u
**On screen:** The original "∫ 2x cos(x²) dx" again, large. On the left, the side work: "u = x²" and "du = 2x dx". The 2x and the dx are underlined in one color, with arrows to a "du"; the x² is boxed in another color, with an arrow to a "u". Under it: "∫ cos u du". Then "= sin u + C", then "= sin(x²) + C", with "put x back: u = x²" beside the last line.
> Now go back to the original integral and replace. The two x and the d x together are exactly d u. [pause]
> And the x squared is u. [pause]
> What's left is the integral of cosine u, d u. It's all in u, and we know how to integrate it. [pause]
> The integral of cosine u is sine u, plus C. [pause]
> Last, put x back. u is x squared, so the answer is sine of x squared, plus C. [long pause]

## The steps
**On screen:** Four numbered steps. "1. Choose u: an inside function." "2. Find du = u′(x) dx." "3. Rewrite the whole integral in u, including the dx." "4. Integrate, then put x back."
> So here are the steps. Choose u, an inside function. Find d u. Rewrite the whole integral in u, including the d x. Then integrate, and put x back. [long pause]

## Fixing a constant
**On screen:** The problem: "Find ∫ x√(x² + 4) dx." Side work on the left: "u = x² + 4"; "du = 2x dx"; then, as the x and the dx in the problem are underlined, "we have x dx, not 2x dx"; "divide both sides by 2"; "½ du = x dx", boxed. The main column on the right: the x² + 4 boxed and the x dx underlined in the problem, with arrows to u and ½ du; "∫ √u · ½ du"; "= ½ ∫ u^{1/2} du"; "= ½ · u^{3/2}/(3/2) + C = ⅓ u^{3/2} + C"; "= ⅓ (x² + 4)^{3/2} + C".
> Find the integral of x times the square root of x squared plus four, d x. [pause]
> The inside is x squared plus four, so let u be x squared plus four. [pause]
> Then d u d x is two x, so d u equals two x d x. [pause]
> Look back at the integral. We don't have a two x d x to swap out. We have only an x and a d x. [pause]
> So divide both sides by two. One half d u equals x d x. Now x d x is something we can replace. [pause]
> Substitute. The x squared plus four becomes u, and x d x becomes one half d u. That's the integral of root u times one half d u. [pause]
> Pull the one half out front, and write root u as u to the one half. [pause]
> Integrate: u to the three halves over three halves. One half times that is one third u to the three halves, plus C. [pause]
> Put x back: one third times x squared plus four, to the three halves, plus C. [long pause]

## Definite integrals: change the limits
**On screen:** The problem: "Evaluate ∫_0^2 x(x² + 1)³ dx." Side work on the left: "u = x² + 1"; "du = 2x dx"; "½ du = x dx". The limits 0 and 2 in the problem are circled: "limits of integration (endpoints)". Main column: "½ ∫_{x=0}^{x=2} u³ du", the limits written as "x = 0" and "x = 2". Then the long way, in gray: "= ⅛ u⁴", "= [⅛ (x² + 1)⁴]_0^2", "= ⅛(625) − ⅛(1) = 78". Then those three lines are crossed out: "we never do this". The side work adds "When x = 0" with an arrow to "u = 0² + 1 = 1" and "When x = 2" with an arrow to "u = 2² + 1 = 5". The main column becomes "½ ∫_1^5 u³ du"; "= ½ [u⁴/4]_1^5"; "= ⅛(625 − 1)"; "= 624/8 = 78".
> Now a definite integral. Evaluate the integral from zero to two of x times x squared plus one, cubed, d x. [pause]
> Same start. Let u be x squared plus one. d u is two x d x, so one half d u is x d x. [pause]
> Notice the limits of integration, zero and two: the endpoints of the integral. They're x values. x runs from zero to two. [pause]
> For now, substitute and leave the limits where they are, labeled as x values: from x equals zero to x equals two. That's one half times the integral of u cubed, d u. [pause]
> From here you could finish in u. One half times u to the fourth over four is one eighth u to the fourth. [pause]
> Then put x back, one eighth times x squared plus one, to the fourth, and evaluate from zero to two. One eighth of six twenty-five, minus one eighth of one: seventy-eight. [pause]
> That works, but we never do definite integrals that way. There's no need to switch back to x. [pause]
> Instead, change the limits of integration from x values to u values. Plug each x into the definition of u: u equals x squared plus one. [pause]
> When x is zero, u is zero squared plus one, which is one. When x is two, u is two squared plus one, which is five. [pause]
> So the integral is one half times the integral from one to five of u cubed, d u. Now we can integrate in u and stay in u. [pause]
> One half times u to the fourth over four, from one to five: one eighth times six twenty-five minus one. [pause]
> Six twenty-four over eight: seventy-eight. Same answer, with no trip back to x. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "u: an inside function whose derivative is a factor (up to a constant)." "du = u′(x) dx; divide to fix a constant." "Rewrite everything in u, even the dx. Integrate, then put x back." "Definite: plug the x limits into u to get u limits."
> Choose u as an inside function whose derivative is there as a factor, up to a constant. Rewrite everything in u, even the d x, integrate, and put x back. For definite integrals, change the limits to u values instead. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A linear inside
**On screen:** "Find ∫ e^{3x} dx." Board: u = 3x; du = 3 dx; ⅓ du = dx; ∫ eᵘ · ⅓ du = ⅓ eᵘ + C; = ⅓ e^{3x} + C.
> Find the integral of e to the three x, d x. [pause]
> The inside is three x, so let u be three x. [pause]
> Then d u is three d x. Divide by three: one third d u is d x. [pause]
> The integral becomes one third times the integral of e to the u, d u. That's one third e to the u, plus C.
> Put x back: one third e to the three x, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 2: A logarithm inside
**On screen:** "Find ∫ (ln x)/x dx." Board: u = ln x; du = (1/x) dx; ∫ u du = u²/2 + C; = (ln x)²/2 + C.
> Find the integral of natural log x over x, d x. [pause]
> The derivative of ln x is one over x, and one over x is right there. So let u be ln x. [pause]
> Then d u is one over x d x, and the integral becomes the integral of u d u. [pause]
> That's u squared over two, plus C. Put x back: ln x squared, over two, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 3: Trig with new limits
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_0^{π/2} cos²x sin x dx." On the right, the choice of u: "u = sin x?" and "u = cos x?", each with its derivative; then "cos x is inside the square, and its derivative matches the sin x dx", and "u = cos x" is boxed. Board: du = −sin x dx, so −du = sin x dx; "When x = 0" → u = cos 0 = 1; "When x = π/2" → u = cos(π/2) = 0; −∫_1^0 u² du = ∫_0^1 u² du; = [u³/3]_0^1 = ⅓.
> Evaluate the integral from zero to pi over two of cosine squared x, sine x, d x. [try it]
> What should u be? Cosine x and sine x are both candidates, and each one's derivative is the other, up to a sign. [pause]
> The cosine is inside something more complicated: it's being squared. And its derivative, negative sine x, can be swapped for the sine x d x that's sitting there. So let u be cosine x. [pause]
> Then d u is negative sine x d x. Multiply both sides by negative one: negative d u is sine x d x. [pause]
> Change the limits by plugging each x into u equals cosine x. When x is zero, u is cosine zero, which is one. [pause]
> When x is pi over two, u is cosine of pi over two, which is zero. [pause]
> Substitute: negative the integral from one to zero of u squared, d u. Flipping the limits flips the sign back: the integral from zero to one of u squared. [pause]
> That's u cubed over three, from zero to one: one third. [long pause]
