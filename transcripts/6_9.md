# 6.9 Integrating Using Substitution

## The chain rule, backward
**On screen:** "d/dx sin(x²) = cos(x²) · 2x" with the inside x² underbraced and labeled "inside". Then the same line read right to left: "∫ 2x cos(x²) dx = sin(x²) + C". The factor 2x is highlighted in both lines: "the derivative of the inside".
> By the chain rule, the derivative of sine of x squared is cosine of x squared, times two x: the derivative of the inside. [pause]
> Read that backward. The integral of two x cosine of x squared is sine of x squared, plus C. [pause]
> The clue is that the integrand has an inside function, and its derivative is sitting right there as a factor. Substitution is the method for spotting that. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 6.9 · Integrating using substitution." Unit 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change.

## The method
**On screen:** Four numbered steps, each shown on ∫ 2x cos(x²) dx. 1. "Choose u: an inside function. u = x²." 2. "Find du: du = 2x dx." 3. "Rewrite everything in u: ∫ cos u du." 4. "Integrate, then put x back: sin u + C = sin(x²) + C." The underbrace marks x² as u; the factor 2x dx is boxed and relabeled du.
> Here's the method. Step one: choose u to be an inside function, here x squared. [pause]
> Step two: find d u. The derivative of u is two x, so d u equals two x d x. [pause]
> Step three: rewrite the whole integral in u. The x squared becomes u, and two x d x becomes d u. What's left is the integral of cosine u, d u. [pause]
> Step four: integrate, and put x back. Sine u plus C becomes sine of x squared plus C. [long pause]

## Fixing a constant
**On screen:** The problem: "Find ∫ x√(x² + 4) dx." Board: the inside x² + 4 underbraced; u = x² + 4; du = 2x dx; x dx = ½ du; ∫ √u · ½ du = ½ ∫ u^{1/2} du; = ½ · u^{3/2}/(3/2) = ⅓ u^{3/2}; = ⅓ (x² + 4)^{3/2} + C.
> Find the integral of x times the square root of x squared plus four. [try it]
> The inside is x squared plus four, so let u be x squared plus four. [pause]
> Then d u is two x d x. The integrand only has x d x, not two x d x, so divide by two: x d x is one half d u.
> Rewrite: the integral of root u times one half d u. Pull out the one half. [pause]
> Integrate u to the one half: u to the three halves over three halves. One half times that is one third u to the three halves.
> Put x back: one third times x squared plus four, to the three halves, plus C. [long pause]

## Definite integrals: change the limits
**On screen:** The problem: "Evaluate ∫_0^2 x(x² + 1)³ dx." Board: u = x² + 1, du = 2x dx, x dx = ½ du; x = 0: u = 1; x = 2: u = 5; ½ ∫_1^5 u³ du; = ½ [u⁴/4]_1^5; = ⅛(625 − 1) = 624/8 = 78. The new limits are written in a different color with arrows from the old ones.
> For a definite integral, there's one more step. Evaluate the integral from zero to two of x times x squared plus one, cubed. [pause]
> Let u be x squared plus one. Then d u is two x d x, so x d x is one half d u.
> The limits are x values, so change them to u values. When x is zero, u is one. When x is two, u is five. [pause]
> Now the integral is one half times the integral from one to five of u cubed. No need to go back to x.
> That's one half times u to the fourth over four, from one to five: one eighth of six twenty-five minus one. [pause]
> Six twenty-four over eight: seventy-eight. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "u: an inside function whose derivative is a factor." "du = u′ dx; fix constants by dividing." "Rewrite everything in u, integrate, put x back." "Definite: change the limits to u values."
> Choose u as an inside function whose derivative is there as a factor, up to a constant. Rewrite everything in u, integrate, and put x back. For definite integrals, change the limits instead. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A linear inside
**On screen:** "Find ∫ e^{3x} dx." Board: u = 3x; du = 3 dx, dx = ⅓ du; ∫ eᵘ · ⅓ du = ⅓ eᵘ; = ⅓ e^{3x} + C.
> Find the integral of e to the three x. [pause]
> The inside is three x. Let u be three x, so d u is three d x, and d x is one third d u. [pause]
> The integral becomes one third times the integral of e to the u: one third e to the u.
> Put x back: one third e to the three x, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 2: A logarithm inside
**On screen:** "Find ∫ (ln x)/x dx." Board: u = ln x; du = (1/x) dx; ∫ u du = u²/2; = (ln x)²/2 + C.
> Find the integral of natural log x over x. [pause]
> The derivative of ln x is one over x, and one over x is right there. So let u be ln x. [pause]
> Then d u is one over x d x, and the integral becomes the integral of u d u.
> That's u squared over two. Put x back: ln x squared, over two, plus C. [long pause]

## Example 3: Trig with new limits
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫_0^{π/2} cos²x sin x dx." Board: u = cos x; du = −sin x dx, sin x dx = −du; x = 0: u = 1; x = π/2: u = 0; −∫_1^0 u² du = ∫_0^1 u² du; = [u³/3]_0^1 = ⅓.
> Evaluate the integral from zero to pi over two of cosine squared x, sine x. [pause]
> Let u be cosine x. Then d u is negative sine x d x, so sine x d x is negative d u. [pause]
> Change the limits. At zero, u is cosine zero, one. At pi over two, u is cosine pi over two, zero.
> The integral is negative the integral from one to zero of u squared. Flipping the limits flips the sign back: the integral from zero to one of u squared. [pause]
> That's u cubed over three from zero to one: one third. [long pause]
