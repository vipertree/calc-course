# 7.2 Verifying Solutions for Differential Equations

## Does it fit?
**On screen:** "dy/dx = 3y" on top. Below, a candidate: "y = 5e^{3x}?". Its derivative is computed, "dy/dx = 15e^{3x}", and the right side, "3y = 3(5e^{3x}) = 15e^{3x}". The two sides match and a check mark appears.
> To check whether a function solves a differential equation, you don't have to solve anything. Just test it. [pause]
> Is y equals five e to the three x a solution of d y d x equals three y? The left side, its derivative, is fifteen e to the three x. [pause]
> The right side, three y, is three times five e to the three x: also fifteen e to the three x. They match for every x, so it's a solution. [long pause]

## The test
**On screen:** Three steps: "1. Differentiate the candidate as many times as the equation needs." "2. Substitute y, y′ (and y″) into both sides." "3. Simplify: a solution makes the two sides equal for every x." Then "y = Ce^{3x}: y′ = 3Ce^{3x} = 3y for every C", with a family of curves.
> The test has three steps. Differentiate the candidate as many times as the equation needs. [pause]
> Substitute into both sides of the equation. [pause]
> Simplify. If the two sides are equal for every x, the function is a solution. If they differ anywhere, it isn't. [pause]
> In fact, every function C e to the three x works: its derivative is three times itself. A differential equation usually has a whole family of solutions. [long pause]

## One that fails
**On screen:** The problem: "Is y = e^{2x} a solution of y″ − y = 0?" Board: y′ = 2e^{2x}; y″ = 4e^{2x}; y″ − y = 4e^{2x} − e^{2x} = 3e^{2x}; 3e^{2x} ≠ 0: not a solution.
> Is y equals e to the two x a solution of y double prime minus y equals zero? [try it]
> Differentiate twice: y prime is two e to the two x, and y double prime is four e to the two x. [pause]
> Substitute: four e to the two x minus e to the two x is three e to the two x.
> That's never zero, so the two sides don't match. It isn't a solution. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Differentiate, substitute, simplify." "Equal for every x: a solution." "Solutions usually come in families."
> To verify a solution: differentiate, substitute, and simplify. If both sides agree for every x, it's a solution. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A first-order equation
**On screen:** "Show that y = x² + 2x is a solution of xy′ − y = x²." Board: y′ = 2x + 2; xy′ − y = x(2x + 2) − (x² + 2x); = 2x² + 2x − x² − 2x = x²; matches the right side, so it's a solution.
> Show that y equals x squared plus two x is a solution of x y prime minus y equals x squared. [pause]
> y prime is two x plus two. [pause]
> Substitute into the left side: x times two x plus two, minus x squared plus two x.
> That's two x squared plus two x, minus x squared, minus two x: just x squared. [pause]
> It matches the right side, so it's a solution. [long pause]

## Example 2: A second-order equation
**On screen:** "Show that y = sin 2x is a solution of y″ + 4y = 0." Board: y′ = 2cos 2x; y″ = −4sin 2x; y″ + 4y = −4sin 2x + 4sin 2x = 0. Matches.
> Show that y equals sine two x is a solution of y double prime plus four y equals zero. [pause]
> By the chain rule, y prime is two cosine two x, and y double prime is negative four sine two x. [pause]
> Substitute: negative four sine two x, plus four sine two x. That's zero.
> It matches, so sine two x is a solution. [long pause]

## Example 3: Finding the constant
**On screen:** "y = 1/(x + C) solves dy/dx = −y². Find C so that y(0) = ½." Board: check: y′ = −1/(x + C)² = −y²; y(0) = 1/C = ½; C = 2; y = 1/(x + 2).
> y equals one over x plus C solves d y d x equals negative y squared. Find the value of C for which y of zero is one half. [pause]
> First, a quick check: the derivative of one over x plus C is negative one over x plus C squared, which is negative y squared. Good. [pause]
> Now use the condition. y of zero is one over C, and that equals one half.
> So C is two, and the solution is y equals one over x plus two. [long pause]
