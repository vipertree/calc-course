# 7.8 Exponential Models with Differential Equations

## Growth proportional to size
**On screen:** A dish of bacteria: dots multiplying, the count doubling at equal time steps (100, 200, 400, 800). Beside it, the graph of the count bending upward. Then "the more there are, the faster they grow: dP/dt = kP".
> A colony of bacteria grows faster when there are more bacteria to divide. Its rate of growth is proportional to its size. [pause]
> That's the differential equation d P d t equals k P. It shows up everywhere: populations, money in an account, radioactive decay, medicine leaving the bloodstream. [pause]
> And we can solve it once and for all. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 7.8 · Exponential models with differential equations." Unit 7 · Differential Equations.

## The solution
**On screen:** Board: dy/dt = ky; dy/y = k dt; ln|y| = kt + C; y = Ae^{kt}; at t = 0, y = A, so A = y₀; boxed: "dy/dt = ky has solution y = y₀e^{kt}". Two small graphs: k > 0 growth curving up, k < 0 decay curving down toward 0.
> Separate: d y over y equals k d t. Integrate: ln of absolute y equals k t plus C. [pause]
> Exponentiate: y equals A e to the k t. At time zero, e to the zero is one, so A is the starting amount, y sub zero. [pause]
> So any quantity whose rate of change is proportional to itself is y sub zero times e to the k t. Positive k means growth. Negative k means decay. [long pause]

## Finding k from data
**On screen:** The problem: "A population grows at a rate proportional to its size. P(0) = 500 and P(3) = 800. Find P(6)." Board: P = 500e^{kt}; 800 = 500e^{3k}; e^{3k} = 8/5; k = ⅓ ln(8/5) ≈ 0.157; P(6) = 500e^{6k} = 500(e^{3k})² = 500(8/5)² = 1280.
> A population grows at a rate proportional to its size. It's five hundred at time zero and eight hundred at time three. Find the population at time six. [pause]
> The model is P equals five hundred e to the k t. Use the second point to find k: eight hundred equals five hundred e to the three k. [pause]
> Divide: e to the three k is eight fifths. So k is one third ln of eight fifths, about point one five seven.
> Now P of six is five hundred e to the six k. And e to the six k is e to the three k, squared. [pause]
> That's five hundred times eight fifths squared: twelve eighty. [long pause]

## Half-life and doubling time
**On screen:** "Decay (k < 0): half-life T with e^{kT} = ½, so T = ln 2/|k|." "Growth (k > 0): doubling time T with e^{kT} = 2, so T = ln 2/k." Then a decay curve with the half-lives marked: the amount halves every T.
> For decay, the half-life is how long it takes to lose half. Set e to the k T equal to one half, and the half-life is ln two over the absolute value of k. [pause]
> For growth, the doubling time comes from e to the k T equal to two: ln two over k. [pause]
> Either way, the time to halve or double doesn't depend on how much you start with. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "dy/dt = ky" and beneath it "y = y₀e^{kt}". "Find k from a second data point." "Half-life or doubling time: ln 2/|k|."
> A rate proportional to the amount means exponential change: y sub zero e to the k t. Use a second data point to find k, and ln two over k gives the doubling time or half-life. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Half-life
**On screen:** "A substance has a half-life of 10 years. There are 80 g now. How much remains after 25 years?" Board: y = 80e^{kt}; ½ = e^{10k}, k = −ln 2/10; y(25) = 80e^{25k} = 80(e^{10k})^{2.5} = 80(½)^{2.5}; ≈ 14.14 g.
> A substance has a half-life of ten years, and there are eighty grams now. How much remains after twenty-five years? [pause]
> The model is y equals eighty e to the k t. In ten years, half remains: e to the ten k is one half, so k is negative ln two over ten. [pause]
> At twenty-five years: eighty e to the twenty-five k. Twenty-five is two and a half half-lives, so that's eighty times one half to the two point five.
> About fourteen point one four grams. [long pause]

## Example 2: When will it reach a target?
**On screen:** "A bacteria count triples every 4 hours. When is it 10 times its starting size?" Board: e^{4k} = 3, k = ln 3/4; 10 = e^{kt}; t = ln 10/k = 4 ln 10/ln 3 ≈ 8.38 hours.
> A bacteria count triples every four hours. When will it be ten times its starting size? [pause]
> Tripling in four hours means e to the four k is three, so k is ln three over four. [pause]
> Ten times the start means e to the k t equals ten, so t is ln ten over k.
> That's four ln ten over ln three: about eight point three eight hours. [long pause]
