# 8.1 Finding the Average Value of a Function on an Interval

## Averaging infinitely many numbers
**On screen:** A thermometer beside a graph of temperature over one day, rising from a cool morning to a warm afternoon and falling at night. A list of five readings appears under it, "average = sum ÷ 5". Then the question: "What about every instant of the day?"
> To average five temperatures, you add them and divide by five. [pause]
> But the temperature has a value at every instant of the day, infinitely many of them. How do you average infinitely many numbers? [pause]
> Calculus has an answer, and it's built from two things you already know: a Riemann sum and the definite integral. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.1 · Finding the average value of a function on an interval." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## From a list to an integral
**On screen:** A curve y = f(x) over [a, b], cut into n equal pieces of width Δx with a sample point dot in each. Board: average ≈ (f(x₁) + ... + f(xₙ))/n; n = (b − a)/Δx; average ≈ 1/(b − a) · Σ f(xₖ)Δx; as n → ∞: f_avg = 1/(b − a) ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx. Boxed with the plain-words version: "total accumulated, divided by the length of the interval".
> Take n samples, equally spaced. Their average is the sum of the samples over n. [pause]
> Now a trick. The pieces have width delta x, so n is b minus a over delta x. Dividing by n is the same as multiplying by delta x over b minus a. [pause]
> So the average of the samples is one over b minus a, times a Riemann sum. [pause]
> Take more and more samples, and the Riemann sum becomes the integral. The average value of f on the interval from a to b is one over b minus a times the integral of f from a to b. [pause]
> In plain words: add up everything with the integral, then divide by the length of the interval. [long pause]

## Leveling the area
**On screen:** The region under the curve is shaded. A horizontal line at height f_avg rises into place, and the region "pours" like water into a rectangle of the same width; the bumps above the line fill the dips below. Labels: "height f_avg", "width b − a", "same area". Then a dot slides along the curve to a point c where the curve crosses the line, labeled "f(c) = f_avg".
> Here's the picture. Multiply both sides by b minus a, and the average value times the width equals the area. [pause]
> So the average value is the height of the rectangle with the same width and the same area. Picture the region as water, and let it level out. The water line is the average value. [pause]
> If f is continuous, the curve has to cross that water line somewhere. So there's at least one c in the interval where f of c equals the average value. That's the Mean Value Theorem for integrals. [long pause]

## Average value of x squared
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the average value of f(x) = x² on [1, 4]. Then find where f equals its average value." Board, one step per line: f_avg = 1/(4 − 1) ∫₁⁴ x² dx; = ⅓ [x³/3]₁⁴; = ⅓ (64/3 − 1/3); = ⅓ · 21; = 7; x² = 7; x = ±√7; √7 ≈ 2.65 is in [1, 4], −√7 is not. Then the graph with the line y = 7 and the point (√7, 7).
> Find the average value of x squared on the interval from one to four. Then find where f equals its average value. [try it]
> The interval has length four minus one, three. So the average value is one third times the integral of x squared from one to four. [pause]
> The antiderivative is x cubed over three. At four that's sixty-four thirds; at one it's one third. The difference is sixty-three thirds, which is twenty-one. [pause]
> One third of twenty-one is seven. The average value is seven. [pause]
> Now where does f equal seven? Set x squared equal to seven: x is plus or minus root seven. [pause]
> Root seven is about two point six five, inside the interval. Negative root seven is not. So the average value is seven, and f reaches it at x equals root seven. [pause]
> Keep the two straight: seven is the output, the average value. Root seven is the input where it happens. [long pause]

## Average value is not average rate of change
**On screen:** Side by side for x² on [1, 4]. Left, "average rate of change": the secant line from (1, 1) to (4, 16), slope (16 − 1)/(4 − 1) = 5. Right, "average value": the water line at height 7. Captions: "a slope: (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)" and "a height: 1/(b − a) ∫ f(x) dx".
> Don't mix this up with the average rate of change. That's a slope: the change in f over the change in x. For x squared on this interval it's fifteen over three, five. [pause]
> The average value is a height: seven. Same function, same interval, different questions. [pause]
> Here's a way to remember: if the question is about f prime, average rate of change of f and average value of f prime are the same number, because the integral of f prime from a to b is f of b minus f of a. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "f_avg = 1/(b − a) ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx" boxed; "the height of the rectangle with the same area"; "continuous f reaches its average at some c".
> The average value of f is the integral divided by the length of the interval: the height of the rectangle with the same area. And a continuous function reaches its average value somewhere in the interval. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: From a graph
**On screen:** The graph of f on [0, 6]: a horizontal segment at height 2 from x = 0 to x = 2, then the upper half of the circle of radius 2 centered at (4, 0). The problem: "Find the average value of f on [0, 6]." Board: ∫₀⁶ f = (rectangle) 2 · 2 + (semicircle) ½π(2)²; = 4 + 2π; f_avg = (4 + 2π)/6; = (2 + π)/3 ≈ 1.71.
> The graph of f is a segment at height two from zero to two, then a semicircle of radius two. Find the average value of f on the interval from zero to six. [pause]
> The integral is the area. The rectangle is two by two: four. The semicircle is half of pi r squared: half of four pi, two pi. [pause]
> So the integral is four plus two pi. Divide by the length of the interval, six. [pause]
> That's two plus pi over three, about one point seven one. [long pause]

## Example 2: Average of sine
**On screen:** "Find the average value of sin x on [0, π]." Board: f_avg = 1/π ∫₀^π sin x dx; = 1/π [−cos x]₀^π; = 1/π (1 + 1); = 2/π ≈ 0.637. A graph of one arch of sine with the water line at 2/π.
> Find the average value of sine x from zero to pi. [pause]
> One over pi, times the integral of sine x from zero to pi. [pause]
> The antiderivative is negative cosine x. At pi, negative cosine pi is one; at zero, negative cosine zero is negative one. The difference is two. [pause]
> So the average value is two over pi, about point six three seven. The arch reaches up to one, but it averages a bit less than two thirds. [long pause]

## Example 3: Average temperature from a table
**On screen:** A table: t (hours) 0, 2, 4, 6, 8; T(t) (°F) 60, 64, 70, 68, 62. The problem: "Use a trapezoidal sum with four subintervals to approximate the average temperature over 0 ≤ t ≤ 8." Board: ∫₀⁸ T dt ≈ 2·(60 + 64)/2 + 2·(64 + 70)/2 + 2·(70 + 68)/2 + 2·(68 + 62)/2; = 124 + 134 + 138 + 130; = 526; average ≈ 526/8 = 65.75 °F.
> Here's a table of temperatures over eight hours. Use a trapezoidal sum with four subintervals to approximate the average temperature. [pause]
> First the integral. Each subinterval has width two, so each trapezoid is two times the average of its two heights. [pause]
> That's one twenty-four, plus one thirty-four, plus one thirty-eight, plus one thirty: five twenty-six degree-hours. [pause]
> Divide by the length of the interval, eight hours. The average temperature is about sixty-five point seven five degrees. [long pause]
