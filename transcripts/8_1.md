# 8.1 Finding the Average Value of a Function on an Interval

## Averaging infinitely many numbers
**On screen:** A thermometer beside a graph of the temperature T(t) from 6 a.m. (t = 6) to 10 p.m. (t = 22), rising from a cool morning to a warm afternoon and falling at night. Five readings appear as dots, each labeled with its value: 52° at t = 6, 61° at 10, 70° at 14, 66° at 18, 56° at 22. Under the graph: "52 + 61 + 70 + 66 + 56 = 305", then "305 ÷ 5 = 61°", and a dashed line at 61 across the graph. Then the question: "What about every instant of the day?"
> Here's the temperature outside over one day, from six in the morning until ten at night. [pause]
> Take five readings: fifty-two degrees, sixty-one, seventy, sixty-six, and fifty-six. [pause]
> To average them, add them up: three hundred five. Then divide by how many there are, five. The average is sixty-one degrees. [pause]
> But the temperature has a value at every instant of the day, infinitely many of them. How do you average infinitely many numbers? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.1 · Finding the average value of a function on an interval." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Four meanings, plus one
**On screen:** The 2x2 card. Columns "Analytically" and "Graphically"; rows "Derivative" and "Integral". The derivative row lights up: "Instantaneous rate of change" and "Slope of the tangent line" (the slope at a point). Then the integral row: "Accumulation" and "Area under the curve". Then a bonus card under the grid: "Bonus: derivatives and integrals are inverses", with "d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt = f(x)".
> Unit eight is all about applying integrals. So start with what derivatives and integrals mean. [pause]
> A derivative, analytically, is an instantaneous rate of change. Graphically, it's the slope at a point. [pause]
> An integral, analytically, is an accumulation: a total of everything that has built up. Graphically, it's the area under the curve. [pause]
> And one more thing to keep in mind for applications: derivatives and integrals are inverses of each other. Take the derivative of an accumulation function, and you get back the function you accumulated. [long pause]

## Summing infinitely thin slivers
**On screen:** The temperature graph again. Many thin vertical lines fill in under the curve from t = 6 to t = 22, each a sliver from the axis up to the curve, until the region is solid. Board: "list: average = (sum of the values) ÷ (how many)"; "continuous: average = (total accumulation) ÷ (width of the interval)"; then "f_avg = 1/(b − a) ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx", boxed with the plain words "total accumulated, divided by the width of the interval". Then for the temperatures: "1/(22 − 6) ∫₆²² T(t) dt ≈ 62.9°", next to the five-reading average 61°.
> For a list, the average is the sum of the values divided by how many there are. [pause]
> For a continuous function, the sum is the accumulation. The integral adds up infinitely many infinitely thin slivers, one for every instant, each as tall as the function there. [pause]
> And instead of dividing by how many values there are, divide by the width of the interval. [pause]
> So the average value of f on the interval from a to b is one over b minus a, times the integral of f from a to b. [pause]
> In plain words: add up everything with the integral, then divide by the width of the interval. [pause]
> For the temperatures, that works out to about sixty-two point nine degrees. Close to the sixty-one from five readings, but the five readings couldn't see every moment of the day. [long pause]

## Leveling the area
**On screen:** A curve over [a, b] with the area under it shaded in the usual area color: "∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx". Thin walls go up at a and b. The shading turns blue, water held between the walls. Then the water settles: the surface sloshes a little and flattens out to a level line, with the same amount of water the whole time. A dashed line marks the level. Labels: "height f_avg", "width b − a", "same area".
> Here's the integral of f from a to b: the area under the curve. [pause]
> Now picture that area turning into water, held between two walls at a and b. [pause]
> Let the water settle. The high parts flow down into the low parts, and the surface levels out. [pause]
> No water was added or lost, so the area is the same. The water's level is the average value: the height of a rectangle with width b minus a and the same area as the region under the curve. [long pause]

## The Mean Value Theorem for integrals
**On screen:** The curve and the water line at height f_avg stay on the left. Board on the right: "F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, so F′(x) = f(x)"; "MVT for F: F′(c) = (F(b) − F(a))/(b − a)"; "F(b) − F(a) = ∫ₐᵇ f(t) dt"; "so f(c) = 1/(b − a) ∫ₐᵇ f(t) dt". A dot slides along the curve to a point c where the curve crosses the water line, labeled "f(c) = f_avg". Boxed: "If f is continuous on [a, b], then f(c) = f_avg for some c in [a, b]."
> If f is continuous, the curve has to cross the water line somewhere. That's the Mean Value Theorem for integrals, and it follows straight from the Mean Value Theorem you already know. [pause]
> Let big F of x be the accumulation function, the integral from a to x of f of t. By the Fundamental Theorem, F prime is f. So the curve we're looking at is the derivative of F. [pause]
> Apply the Mean Value Theorem to big F. Somewhere between a and b, there's a c where F prime of c equals the average rate of change of F: F of b minus F of a, over b minus a. [pause]
> F of b minus F of a is the integral of f from a to b. And F prime of c is f of c. [pause]
> So f of c equals one over b minus a times the integral: f reaches its average value at c. The Mean Value Theorem for integrals is the Mean Value Theorem, applied to the accumulation function. [long pause]

## Average value of x squared
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the average value of f(x) = x² on [1, 4]. Then find where f equals its average value." Board, one step per line: f_avg = 1/(4 − 1) ∫₁⁴ x² dx; = ⅓ [x³/3]₁⁴; = ⅓ (64/3 − 1/3); = ⅓ · 21; = 7; x² = 7; x = ±√7; √7 ≈ 2.65 is in [1, 4], −√7 is not. Then the graph with the line y = 7 and the point (√7, 7).
> Find the average value of x squared on the interval from one to four. Then find where f equals its average value. [pause]
> The interval has length four minus one, three. So the average value is one third times the integral of x squared from one to four. [pause]
> The antiderivative is x cubed over three. At four that's sixty-four thirds; at one it's one third. The difference is sixty-three thirds, which is twenty-one. [pause]
> One third of twenty-one is seven. The average value is seven. [pause]
> Now where does f equal seven? Set x squared equal to seven: x is plus or minus root seven. [pause]
> Root seven is about two point six five, inside the interval. Negative root seven is not. So the average value is seven, and f reaches it at x equals root seven. [pause]
> Keep the two straight: seven is the output, the average value. Root seven is the input where it happens. [long pause]

## Average value is not average rate of change
**On screen:** Side by side for x² on [1, 4]. Left, "average rate of change": the secant line from (1, 1) to (4, 16), slope (16 − 1)/(4 − 1) = 5. Right, "average value": the water line at height 7. Captions: "a slope: (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a)" and "a height: 1/(b − a) ∫ f(x) dx".
> Don't mix this up with the average rate of change. That's a slope: the change in f over the change in x. For x squared on this interval it's fifteen over three, five. [pause]
> The average value is a height: seven. Same function, same interval, different questions. [pause]
> Here's a way to remember: if the question is about f prime, average rate of change of f and average value of f prime are the same number, because the integral of f prime from a to b is f of b minus f of a. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "f_avg = 1/(b − a) ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx" boxed; "the height of the rectangle with the same area"; "continuous f reaches its average at some c".
> The average value of f is the integral divided by the length of the interval: the height of the rectangle with the same area. And a continuous function reaches its average value somewhere in the interval. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: From a graph
**On screen:** The graph of f on [0, 6]: a horizontal segment at height 2 from x = 0 to x = 2, then the upper half of the circle of radius 2 centered at (4, 0). The problem: "Find the average value of f on [0, 6]." Board: ∫₀⁶ f = (rectangle) 2 · 2 + (semicircle) ½π(2)²; = 4 + 2π; f_avg = (4 + 2π)/6; = (2 + π)/3 ≈ 1.71.
> The graph of f is a segment at height two from zero to two, then a semicircle of radius two. Find the average value of f on the interval from zero to six. [pause]
> The integral is the area. The rectangle is two by two: four. The semicircle is half of pi r squared: half of four pi, two pi. [pause]
> So the integral is four plus two pi. Divide by the length of the interval, six. [pause]
> That's two plus pi over three, about one point seven one. [long pause]

## Example 2: Average of sine
**On screen:** "Find the average value of sin x on [0, π]." Board: f_avg = 1/π ∫₀^π sin x dx; = 1/π [−cos x]₀^π; = 1/π (1 + 1); = 2/π ≈ 0.637. A graph of one arch of sine with the water line at 2/π.
> Find the average value of sine x from zero to pi. [pause]
> One over pi, times the integral of sine x from zero to pi. [pause]
> The antiderivative is negative cosine x. At pi, negative cosine pi is one; at zero, negative cosine zero is negative one. The difference is two. [pause]
> So the average value is two over pi, about point six three seven. The arch reaches up to one, but it averages a bit less than two thirds. [long pause]

## Example 3: Average temperature from a table
**On screen:** A table: t (hours) 0, 2, 4, 6, 8; T(t) (°F) 60, 64, 70, 68, 62. The problem: "Use a trapezoidal sum with four subintervals to approximate the average temperature over 0 ≤ t ≤ 8." Board: ∫₀⁸ T dt ≈ (2/2)[T(0) + 2T(2) + 2T(4) + 2T(6) + T(8)]; = 1[60 + 2(64) + 2(70) + 2(68) + 62]; = 60 + 128 + 140 + 136 + 62; = 526; average ≈ 526/8 = 65.75 °F.
> Here's a table of temperatures over eight hours. Use a trapezoidal sum with four subintervals to approximate the average temperature. [pause]
> First the integral. Each subinterval has width two, so each trapezoid is two times the average of its two heights. The inside heights are each used twice: two over two, times the quantity T of zero, plus two T of two, plus two T of four, plus two T of six, plus T of eight. [pause]
> Put in the temperatures: sixty, plus two times sixty-four, plus two times seventy, plus two times sixty-eight, plus sixty-two.
> That's sixty plus one twenty-eight plus one forty plus one thirty-six plus sixty-two: five twenty-six degree-hours. [pause]
> Divide by the length of the interval, eight hours. The average temperature is about sixty-five point seven five degrees. [long pause]
