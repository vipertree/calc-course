# 8.13 The Arc Length of a Smooth, Planar Curve and Distance Traveled

## How long is a curve?
**On screen:** A curving road on a map from point A to point B. A straight dashed line joins A to B ("too short"). Then the road is approximated by 4, then 8, then 16 straight segments, each set hugging the curve more closely, with the total length shown each time.
> How long is this road from A to B? The straight line between them is too short: the road bends. [pause]
> Approximate the road with straight segments. Each one is easy to measure with the distance formula. [pause]
> More segments hug the curve more closely, and in the limit the sum becomes an integral. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.13 · The arc length of a smooth, planar curve and distance traveled." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration. A small "BC" tag.

## Pythagoras on a tiny piece
**On screen:** A zoom on one short piece of the curve: a tiny right triangle with horizontal leg dx, vertical leg dy, and hypotenuse ds along the curve. Board: ds² = dx² + dy²; ds = √(dx² + dy²); factor out dx: ds = √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx; L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + [f′(x)]²) dx, boxed, with the plain words "add up the tiny hypotenuses".
> Zoom in on one tiny piece of the curve. It's almost straight: the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs d x and d y. [pause]
> By Pythagoras, d s squared equals d x squared plus d y squared. [pause]
> Factor a d x squared out from under the root: d s equals the square root of one plus d y d x squared, times d x. [pause]
> Add up all the tiny hypotenuses: the arc length is the integral from a to b of the square root of one plus f prime of x squared. [pause]
> f needs a continuous derivative on the interval: a smooth curve. [long pause]

## A check with a line
**On screen:** The line y = 2x from x = 0 to x = 3, from (0, 0) to (3, 6). Board: f′ = 2; L = ∫₀³ √(1 + 4) dx = 3√5; distance formula: √(3² + 6²) = √45 = 3√5. A check mark.
> Test it on a line, where we already know the answer. For y equals two x from zero to three, f prime is two. [pause]
> The integral of root five from zero to three is three root five. [pause]
> The distance formula from the origin to three, six gives root forty-five, which is also three root five. [long pause]

## A curve with an exact length
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the length of y = ⅔x^{3/2} from x = 0 to x = 3." Board, one step per line: f′(x) = ⅔ · (3/2)x^{1/2} = √x; 1 + [f′(x)]² = 1 + x; L = ∫₀³ √(1 + x) dx; = [⅔(1 + x)^{3/2}]₀³; = ⅔(4^{3/2} − 1^{3/2}); = ⅔(8 − 1); = 14/3 ≈ 4.67. The graph shows the curve with its length labeled.
> Find the length of the curve y equals two thirds x to the three halves, from zero to three. [try it]
> The derivative: two thirds times three halves x to the one half. That's just root x. [pause]
> Square it and add one: one plus x. [pause]
> So the length is the integral of root of one plus x, from zero to three. [pause]
> The antiderivative is two thirds, one plus x, to the three halves. At three: four to the three halves is eight. At zero: one. [pause]
> Two thirds of seven: fourteen thirds, about four point six seven. [long pause]

## Most arc lengths need a calculator
**On screen:** "L for y = x² on [0, 1]: ∫₀¹ √(1 + 4x²) dx ≈ 1.479 (calculator)". Then a short note: "for x = g(y): L = ∫ √(1 + [g′(y)]²) dy". And a link to motion: "speed = |v|; distance traveled = ∫ |v| dt" with a forward reference "parametric motion: Unit 9".
> Square roots of one plus something squared rarely have nice antiderivatives. Even the parabola y equals x squared from zero to one needs a calculator: about one point four seven nine. [pause]
> On the exam, most arc length questions are calculator questions. Set up the integral carefully, then evaluate it numerically. [pause]
> If the curve is given as x in terms of y, swap the roles: integrate the square root of one plus g prime of y squared, d y. [pause]
> And when a particle moves along a curve, the distance it travels is the arc length of its path. You'll see that with parametric motion in Unit 9. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + [f′(x)]²) dx"; "ds² = dx² + dy²"; "check: a line gives the distance formula"; "usually: set up, then calculator".
> Arc length adds up tiny hypotenuses: the integral of the square root of one plus f prime squared. It comes from Pythagoras, it agrees with the distance formula on lines, and most of the time you'll set it up and use a calculator. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A designed perfect square
**On screen:** "Find the length of y = x²/2 − (ln x)/4 from x = 1 to x = 2." Board: f′ = x − 1/(4x); [f′]² = x² − ½ + 1/(16x²); 1 + [f′]² = x² + ½ + 1/(16x²) = (x + 1/(4x))²; L = ∫₁² (x + 1/(4x)) dx; = [x²/2 + (ln x)/4]₁²; = (2 + (ln 2)/4) − ½; = 3/2 + (ln 2)/4 ≈ 1.67.
> Find the length of y equals x squared over two, minus ln x over four, from one to two. [pause]
> The derivative is x minus one over four x. Squared: x squared minus one half plus one over sixteen x squared. [pause]
> Add one, and the minus one half becomes plus one half. That's a perfect square: x plus one over four x, squared. So the square root just undoes it. [pause]
> Integrate x plus one over four x: x squared over two plus ln x over four. From one to two, three halves plus ln two over four, about one point six seven. [long pause]

## Example 2: The parabola, by calculator
**On screen:** "Set up and evaluate the length of y = x² from x = 0 to x = 1." Board: f′ = 2x; L = ∫₀¹ √(1 + 4x²) dx; ≈ 1.479 (calculator). A comparison: straight-line distance from (0, 0) to (1, 1) = √2 ≈ 1.414, a bit shorter.
> Set up and evaluate the length of y equals x squared from zero to one. [pause]
> f prime is two x, so the length is the integral of root of one plus four x squared, from zero to one. [pause]
> A calculator gives about one point four seven nine. That's a bit more than root two, the straight line between the endpoints, as it should be. [long pause]

## Example 3: A log of cosine
**On screen:** "Find the length of y = ln(cos x) from x = 0 to x = π/4." Board: f′ = −sin x / cos x = −tan x; 1 + tan²x = sec²x; L = ∫₀^{π/4} sec x dx; = [ln|sec x + tan x|]₀^{π/4}; = ln(√2 + 1) − ln(1); = ln(1 + √2) ≈ 0.881.
> Find the length of y equals ln of cosine x, from zero to pi over four. [pause]
> The derivative is negative sine over cosine: negative tangent x. Squared, plus one: one plus tangent squared, which is secant squared, by the Pythagorean identity. [pause]
> The square root of secant squared is secant, since secant is positive here. The integral of secant x is ln of secant x plus tangent x. [pause]
> At pi over four: ln of root two plus one. At zero: ln of one, zero. The length is ln of one plus root two, about point eight eight one. [long pause]
