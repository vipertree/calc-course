# 8.2 Connecting Position, Velocity, and Acceleration of Functions Using Integrals

## Out and back
**On screen:** A car on a straight road with a mile marker at 0, facing right. It drives 30 miles east, then turns around to face left and drives 10 miles back west. A trip odometer counts 30, then 40. A "position" arrow from 0 ends at 20. Labels: "displacement 20 miles", "distance 40 miles".
> A car drives thirty miles east, then turns around and drives ten miles back. [pause]
> Where did it end up? Twenty miles east of where it started. That's the displacement. [pause]
> How far did it drive? Forty miles. That's the total distance. Integrals can give us both, but they're different integrals. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.2 · Connecting position, velocity, and acceleration using integrals." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Displacement is the integral of velocity
**On screen:** First, a reminder card: "integrating a rate of change yields net change", with "∫ₐᵇ f′(t) dt = f(b) − f(a)". Under it: "velocity = rate of change of position", "v(t) = s′(t)". Then a velocity graph v(t) on [a, b], positive then negative. Board: ∫ₐᵇ v(t) dt = s(b) − s(a); "displacement: net change in position". Plain words: "where you end up, relative to where you started". Area above the axis shaded and marked "+", area below marked "−".
> Remember what integrals of rates do: when you integrate a rate of change, you get the net change. The integral of f prime from a to b is f of b minus f of a. [pause]
> Velocity is the rate of change of position. v is s prime. [pause]
> So when you integrate velocity, you get the change in position: the integral of v from a to b is s of b minus s of a. [pause]
> That's the displacement: the net change in position, where you end up relative to where you started. [pause]
> On the graph, area above the axis is forward motion, which counts as positive. Area below the axis is backward motion, which counts as negative, so the two cancel. Thirty forward, ten back: twenty. [long pause]

## Position from a starting point
**On screen:** Board: s(t) = s(0) + ∫₀ᵗ v(u) du; "where you start" under s(0), "how far you've moved since" under the integral. Then the same pattern with velocity: v(t) = v(0) + ∫₀ᵗ a(u) du.
> To find position, not just change in position, you need a starting point. [pause]
> Position at time t is where you started, plus the net change since: s of zero plus the integral from zero to t of v. [pause]
> The same pattern works one level down. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, so velocity at time t is v of zero plus the integral of acceleration. [long pause]

## Total distance
**On screen:** A car odometer reading 30, then rolling on to 40 as the car drives back west. Then the velocity graph from before, area above the axis shaded. Board: "total distance: travel in either direction adds". The part of the curve below the axis flips up above it (the graph of |v|) and its area turns positive, the same color as the rest. Board: "total distance = ∫ₐᵇ |v(t)| dt".
> Now consider total distance. Remember, total distance means something different from displacement. [pause]
> Travel in either direction still adds to your total distance, the same way a car's odometer keeps counting up whether you drive east or west. [pause]
> So all we're doing is integrating the absolute value of v. Total distance is the integral from a to b of the absolute value of v of t. [pause]
> Graphically, the absolute value turns everything positive. The part of the graph below the axis flips up above it, and all of that area counts toward the total. [long pause]

## Total distance by hand
**On screen:** Left: "On a calculator (Desmos)": a Desmos-style entry line "∫₀⁵ |v(t)| dt" with the absolute value bars. Right: "By hand": a sign chart for v with its zero at c, "+" then "−". Board: ∫ₐᵇ |v(t)| dt = ∫ₐᶜ v(t) dt + (−∫_cᵇ v(t) dt), with "the opposite of the negative piece" under the second term.
> On a calculator, you can enter the absolute value right into the integral, bars and all. [pause]
> By hand, you can't integrate an absolute value directly. Instead, find where v becomes negative. A sign chart for v shows you, every time. [pause]
> Split the interval there. The integral over the piece where v is negative comes out negative, so take its opposite to turn it positive. [pause]
> Then add the pieces. [long pause]

## Not the absolute value of the integral
**On screen:** The velocity graph from the car trip, area above the axis labeled "30", area below labeled "−10". Left column: "|∫ v dt| = |30 + (−10)| = |20| = 20", tagged "the size of the displacement". Right column: "∫ |v| dt = 30 + 10 = 40", tagged "total distance". Between them: "≠".
> Be careful where the absolute value goes. The absolute value of the integral is very different. [pause]
> Take the car trip. The velocity graph has thirty miles of area above the axis and ten below. [pause]
> The integral of v is thirty plus negative ten, twenty. Its absolute value is still twenty. That's only the size of the displacement. [pause]
> The integral of the absolute value of v is thirty plus ten, forty: the total distance. [pause]
> Taking the absolute value at the end only drops the direction of the final answer. Putting it inside the integral counts every mile. [long pause]

## Displacement and distance
**On screen:** The problem: "A particle moves along a line with v(t) = t² − 6t + 8 for 0 ≤ t ≤ 5, and s(0) = 1. Find (a) the displacement, (b) the position at t = 5, (c) the total distance." Board, one step per line: (a) ∫₀⁵ (t² − 6t + 8) dt; = [t³/3 − 3t² + 8t]₀⁵; = 125/3 − 75 + 40; = 20/3; (b) s(5) = 1 + 20/3 = 23/3; (c) v = (t − 2)(t − 4); zeros t = 2, t = 4; sign chart; ∫₀² v dt = 20/3; ∫₂⁴ v dt = −4/3; ∫₄⁵ v dt = 4/3; −(−4/3) = 4/3; distance = 20/3 + 4/3 + 4/3 = 28/3.
> A particle moves along a line with velocity t squared minus six t plus eight, from time zero to five, starting at position one. Find the displacement, the position at time five, and the total distance. [pause]
> Displacement first: the integral of v from zero to five. The antiderivative is t cubed over three, minus three t squared, plus eight t. [pause]
> At five: one twenty-five thirds, minus seventy-five, plus forty. That's twenty thirds. At zero it's zero. So the displacement is twenty thirds. [pause]
> Position at five is the start plus the displacement: one plus twenty thirds, twenty-three thirds. [pause]
> For total distance, find where v changes sign. Factor: t minus two, times t minus four. The zeros are two and four. [pause]
> Test a value in each piece. At one: one minus six plus eight, three, positive. At three: nine minus eighteen plus eight, negative one. At five: twenty-five minus thirty plus eight, three, positive. [pause]
> Now integrate each piece. From zero to two: twenty thirds. From two to four: negative four thirds. From four to five: four thirds. [pause]
> The piece from two to four came out negative, so take its opposite: four thirds. [pause]
> Add them up: twenty thirds plus four thirds plus four thirds, twenty-eight thirds. More than the displacement, because the particle backed up for a while. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "displacement = ∫ v dt (can cancel)"; "total distance = ∫ |v| dt (never cancels): split where v changes sign"; "s(t) = s(0) + ∫₀ᵗ v"; "v(t) = v(0) + ∫₀ᵗ a".
> Integrate velocity for displacement, and absolute velocity for total distance, splitting where v changes sign. Position is the starting position plus the integral of velocity, and velocity is the starting velocity plus the integral of acceleration. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: From a velocity graph
**On screen:** A velocity graph on [0, 8] made of segments: from (0, 0) up to (2, 4), level to (4, 4), down to (5, 0), down to (6, −2), level to (8, −2). The shading follows the segments exactly. The problem: "Find the displacement and the total distance on [0, 8]." Board: area above = ½(2)(4) + 2(4) + ½(1)(4) = 4 + 8 + 2 = 14; area below = ½(1)(2) + 2(2) = 1 + 4 = 5; displacement = 14 − 5 = 9; distance = 14 + 5 = 19.
> Here's a velocity graph made of line segments. Find the displacement and the total distance from zero to eight. [pause]
> Above the axis: a triangle of area four, a rectangle of area eight, and a triangle of area two. Fourteen. [pause]
> Below the axis: a triangle of area one and a rectangle of area four. Five. [pause]
> Displacement subtracts: fourteen minus five, nine. Total distance adds: nineteen. [long pause]

## Example 2: From acceleration
**On screen:** "A particle has a(t) = 6t and v(0) = −3. Find v(t), then the total distance on [0, 2]." Board: v(t) = −3 + ∫₀ᵗ 6u du; = 3t² − 3; zero at t = 1 (t = −1 is outside); sign chart: − on (0, 1), + on (1, 2); ∫₀¹ v = [t³ − 3t]₀¹ = −2; ∫₁² v = 2 − (−2) = 4; distance = 2 + 4 = 6.
> A particle has acceleration six t and starting velocity negative three. Find the velocity, then the total distance from zero to two. [pause]
> Velocity is the start plus the integral of acceleration: negative three plus three t squared. [pause]
> It's zero when t squared is one. t equals one is in the interval; negative one is not. Before one, v is negative; after one, positive. [pause]
> From zero to one, the integral is negative two. From one to two, it's four. Total distance: two plus four, six. [long pause]

## Example 3: Working backward
**On screen:** "A particle has v(t) = 3t² − 2, and s(2) = 5. Find s(0)." Board: s(2) = s(0) + ∫₀² (3t² − 2) dt; 5 = s(0) + [t³ − 2t]₀²; 5 = s(0) + 4; s(0) = 1.
> A particle has velocity three t squared minus two, and at time two it's at position five. Where was it at time zero? [pause]
> Position at two equals position at zero plus the integral from zero to two. [pause]
> The integral is eight minus four, four. So five equals s of zero plus four. [pause]
> It started at position one. [long pause]
