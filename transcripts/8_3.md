# 8.3 Using Accumulation Functions and Definite Integrals in Applied Contexts

## In and out
**On screen:** A water tank with a faucet pouring in at the top (label "in: E(t) liters per hour") and a drain at the bottom (label "out: D(t) liters per hour"). The water level rises, then falls as the drain overtakes the faucet. A gauge reads the amount A(t).
> Water pours into a tank through a faucet and leaves through a drain. Both rates change over time. [pause]
> When more comes in than goes out, the level rises. When more goes out, it falls. [pause]
> The question is always the same: how much is in the tank at a given time, and when is there the most? [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.3 · Using accumulation functions and definite integrals in applied contexts." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Amount equals start plus net change
**On screen:** Board: A′(t) = E(t) − D(t), labeled "rate in minus rate out"; A(t) = A(0) + ∫₀ᵗ (E(u) − D(u)) du; "start + net change". Under it, the plain-words version: "what you had, plus what came in, minus what went out".
> The rate of change of the amount is the rate in minus the rate out. [pause]
> So the amount at time t is the starting amount plus the integral of that net rate from zero to t. [pause]
> In plain words: what you had, plus what came in, minus what went out. It's the same idea as position from velocity, in a new costume. [long pause]

## Units of an integral
**On screen:** "E(t): liters per hour" times "dt: hours" gives "∫ E(t) dt: liters", with the hours canceling. Then a sentence template: "∫₂⁵ E(t) dt is the total number of liters that flow in from t = 2 to t = 5 hours." The parts of the sentence are color-coded: what (liters that flow in), how much (total), when (from t = 2 to t = 5).
> An integral's units are the rate's units times the time units. Liters per hour, times hours, is liters. [pause]
> So the integral of a rate is a total amount. When the AP exam asks you to interpret one, say what accumulates, that it's a total, and over which interval, with units. [pause]
> For example: the integral of E from two to five is the total number of liters that flow into the tank from time two to time five hours. [long pause]

## When is the amount greatest?
**On screen:** Graphs of E and D on the same axes, crossing at t = c. The gap shaded green where E > D ("rising") and red where D > E ("falling"). A number line for A′ = E − D: + then −, with "max" at c. Then the candidates list: "t = 0, t = c, and the right endpoint".
> The amount is increasing when the rate in beats the rate out, and decreasing when it's the other way. [pause]
> So the amount can only peak where E minus D changes sign: where the curves cross. That's a critical point of A, and the number line tells you whether it's a max. [pause]
> For the absolute maximum on a closed interval, compare the amount at the critical points and at both endpoints. The answer to "how much" is an amount; the answer to "when" is a time. [long pause]

## Water in a tank
**On screen:** The problem: "Water flows into a tank at E(t) = 8 + 2t liters per hour and drains at D(t) = t² liters per hour, 0 ≤ t ≤ 6. The tank holds 50 liters at t = 0. (a) Is the amount increasing or decreasing at t = 5? (b) How much water is in the tank at t = 6? (c) When is the amount greatest, and what is it?" Board, one step per line: (a) A′(5) = E(5) − D(5) = 18 − 25 = −7 < 0: decreasing; (b) A(6) = 50 + ∫₀⁶ (8 + 2t − t²) dt; = 50 + [8t + t² − t³/3]₀⁶; = 50 + (48 + 36 − 72); = 62 liters; (c) 8 + 2t − t² = 0; t² − 2t − 8 = 0; (t − 4)(t + 2) = 0; t = 4 (t = −2 is outside); sign chart: + on (0, 4), − on (4, 6); A(4) = 50 + 32 + 16 − 64/3 = 230/3; candidates: A(0) = 50, A(4) = 230/3 ≈ 76.7, A(6) = 62; greatest: 230/3 liters at t = 4.
> Water flows into a tank at eight plus two t liters per hour and drains at t squared liters per hour, for the first six hours. It starts with fifty liters. Is the amount increasing or decreasing at time five? How much is there at time six? And when is the amount greatest? [try it]
> At time five, the rate in is eighteen and the rate out is twenty-five. The net rate is negative seven: decreasing. [pause]
> For the amount at six: fifty plus the integral from zero to six of eight plus two t minus t squared. [pause]
> The antiderivative is eight t, plus t squared, minus t cubed over three. At six: forty-eight plus thirty-six minus seventy-two, which is twelve. So the tank holds sixty-two liters. [pause]
> For the greatest amount, set the net rate equal to zero. Multiply by negative one to get t squared minus two t minus eight equals zero. [pause]
> That factors as t minus four, times t plus two. So t is four; negative two isn't in the interval. [pause]
> Test values: at one, the net rate is eight plus two minus one, nine, positive. At five, it's negative seven. So the amount rises until four and falls after. [pause]
> Compare the candidates. At zero, fifty. At four: fifty plus thirty-two plus sixteen minus sixty-four thirds, which is two hundred thirty thirds, about seventy-six point seven. At six, sixty-two. [pause]
> The greatest amount is two hundred thirty thirds liters, at t equals four hours. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "A(t) = A(0) + ∫₀ᵗ (rate in − rate out)"; "units: rate units × time units"; "greatest: where rate in − rate out changes from + to −, checked against the endpoints".
> The amount is the start plus the integral of rate in minus rate out. The integral's units are the rate's units times time. And the amount peaks where the net rate changes from positive to negative, checked against the endpoints. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Interpret in context
**On screen:** "Oil leaks from a tanker at a rate of R(t) gallons per hour, t in hours. Interpret (a) ∫₂⁵ R(t) dt = 140 and (b) R′(3) = −4, with units." Board: (a) "A total of 140 gallons leak from t = 2 to t = 5 hours."; (b) "At t = 3 hours, the leak rate is decreasing at 4 gallons per hour per hour."
> Oil leaks from a tanker at R of t gallons per hour. Interpret the integral of R from two to five equals one forty, and R prime of three equals negative four. [pause]
> The integral is a total: one hundred forty gallons leak out between time two and time five hours. [pause]
> R prime is the rate of change of the leak rate. At time three hours, the leak rate is decreasing by four gallons per hour, per hour. [long pause]

## Example 2: From a table of rates
**On screen:** A table: t (years) 0, 2, 5, 8; P′(t) (people per year) 30, 40, 25, 10. The problem: "A town has 1200 people at t = 0. Use a trapezoidal sum to approximate P(8)." Board: ∫₀⁸ P′(t) dt ≈ 2·(30 + 40)/2 + 3·(40 + 25)/2 + 3·(25 + 10)/2; = 70 + 97.5 + 52.5; = 220; P(8) ≈ 1200 + 220 = 1420 people.
> A town's population changes at the rates in the table, and it starts with twelve hundred people. Use a trapezoidal sum to approximate the population at year eight. [pause]
> The integral of P prime is the net change. The trapezoids: two times the average of thirty and forty, seventy. Three times the average of forty and twenty-five, ninety-seven point five. Three times the average of twenty-five and ten, fifty-two point five. [pause]
> The net change is about two hundred twenty people. [pause]
> So P of eight is about twelve hundred plus two twenty: fourteen twenty people. [long pause]

## Example 3: The warmest moment
**On screen:** "A room's temperature changes at H′(t) = 4 − t degrees per minute for 0 ≤ t ≤ 6, and H(0) = 60. Find the maximum temperature and when it occurs." Board: 4 − t = 0, t = 4; sign chart: + on (0, 4), − on (4, 6); H(4) = 60 + ∫₀⁴ (4 − t) dt = 60 + 8 = 68; H(6) = 60 + (24 − 18) = 66; maximum 68 degrees at t = 4 minutes.
> A room's temperature changes at four minus t degrees per minute for six minutes, starting at sixty degrees. Find the maximum temperature and when it occurs. [pause]
> The rate is zero at t equals four. Before four it's positive, after four negative, so the temperature peaks at four. [pause]
> H of four is sixty plus the integral of four minus t from zero to four: sixty plus eight, sixty-eight. Check the end: H of six is sixty plus six, sixty-six. [pause]
> The maximum temperature is sixty-eight degrees, and it happens at t equals four minutes. [long pause]
