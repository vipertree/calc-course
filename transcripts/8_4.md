# 8.4 Finding the Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of x

## Between two curves
**On screen:** Two curves, an upper one and a lower one, crossing at two points. The area under the upper curve is shaded, then the area under the lower curve is shaded in another color and "removed", leaving the region between them highlighted.
> You know how to find the area under a curve. Now take two curves. What's the area of the region trapped between them? [pause]
> One way to see it: take the area under the top curve, and remove the area under the bottom curve. What's left is the region between. [pause]
> That gives a single integral: the top function minus the bottom function. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.4 · Finding the area between curves expressed as functions of x." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Top minus bottom
**On screen:** The region between y = f(x) (top) and y = g(x) (bottom) on [a, b]. One thin vertical rectangle at x, its height marked "f(x) − g(x)" and its width marked with a small double arrow "dx". Then many rectangles filling the region, and the board: area ≈ Σ (f(x) − g(x)) Δx; area = ∫ₐᵇ (f(x) − g(x)) dx; boxed with "∫ (top − bottom) dx".
> Slice the region into thin vertical rectangles. A rectangle at x reaches from the bottom curve up to the top curve, so its height is f of x minus g of x. Its width is d x. [pause]
> Add up all the rectangles, and let them get thin. The area is the integral from a to b of f of x minus g of x, d x. [pause]
> In words: integrate top minus bottom. [long pause]

## Even below the axis
**On screen:** A region that dips below the x-axis. A rectangle's height is shown as "top − bottom" with numbers: top = 1, bottom = −2, height = 1 − (−2) = 3. Then both curves slide up together by 3 units; the region keeps its shape and the height label stays 3.
> This works even when part of the region is below the x-axis. [pause]
> If the top curve is at one and the bottom curve is at negative two, the rectangle's height is one minus negative two: three. The subtraction handles the signs for you. [pause]
> Slide both curves up and the region doesn't change. Neither does top minus bottom. [long pause]

## Finding the limits
**On screen:** The two curves crossing at two points, each marked with a dot and a dashed line down to the x-axis labeled "a" and "b". Board: "set f(x) = g(x) and solve". Then a test point between them, with "which is on top? plug in a number".
> When the region is bounded only by the two curves, the limits of integration are where the curves meet. Set them equal and solve. [pause]
> Then decide which curve is on top by plugging in any x between the limits. [long pause]

## Area between a line and a parabola
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the area of the region bounded by y = x and y = x² − 2." Board, one step per line: x² − 2 = x; x² − x − 2 = 0; (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0; x = −1, x = 2; test x = 0: line 0, parabola −2, so the line is on top; A = ∫₋₁² (x − (x² − 2)) dx; = ∫₋₁² (x − x² + 2) dx; = [x²/2 − x³/3 + 2x]₋₁²; = (2 − 8/3 + 4) − (1/2 + 1/3 − 2); = 10/3 − (−7/6); = 9/2. The graph shows the region, partly below the axis, with a representative rectangle.
> Find the area of the region bounded by the line y equals x and the parabola y equals x squared minus two. [pause]
> First, where do they meet? Set x squared minus two equal to x. [pause]
> Move everything to one side: x squared minus x minus two equals zero. Factor: x minus two, times x plus one. So x is negative one or two. [pause]
> Which is on top? Test zero: the line gives zero, the parabola gives negative two. The line is on top. [pause]
> So the area is the integral from negative one to two of x minus the quantity x squared minus two. Distribute the minus sign: x minus x squared plus two. [pause]
> The antiderivative is x squared over two, minus x cubed over three, plus two x. [pause]
> At two: two minus eight thirds plus four, which is ten thirds. At negative one: one half plus one third minus two, which is negative seven sixths. [pause]
> Ten thirds minus negative seven sixths is twenty sixths plus seven sixths: twenty-seven sixths, which is nine halves. [pause]
> Notice that part of the region is below the x-axis, and we never had to treat it separately. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Area = ∫ₐᵇ (top − bottom) dx"; "limits: where the curves meet"; "top: test a point".
> The area between two curves is the integral of top minus bottom. The limits are where the curves meet, and a test point tells you which is on top. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Root and line
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = √x and y = x/2." Board: √x = x/2; x = x²/4; x² − 4x = 0; x(x − 4) = 0, so x = 0, x = 4; test x = 1: √1 = 1 > ½, root on top; A = ∫₀⁴ (√x − x/2) dx; = [⅔x^{3/2} − x²/4]₀⁴; = 16/3 − 4; = 4/3.
> Find the area between the curves y equals root x and y equals x over two. [pause]
> Set them equal and square both sides: x equals x squared over four. So x squared minus four x is zero, and x is zero or four. [pause]
> Test one: root one is one, and one over two is a half. The root is on top. [pause]
> Integrate root x minus x over two from zero to four: two thirds x to the three halves, minus x squared over four. At four: sixteen thirds minus four, four thirds. [long pause]

## Example 2: Sine and cosine
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = cos x and y = sin x for 0 ≤ x ≤ π/4." Board: on [0, π/4], cos x ≥ sin x; A = ∫₀^{π/4} (cos x − sin x) dx; = [sin x + cos x]₀^{π/4}; = (√2/2 + √2/2) − (0 + 1); = √2 − 1 ≈ 0.414.
> Find the area between cosine x and sine x, from zero to pi over four. [pause]
> At zero, cosine is one and sine is zero, and they meet at pi over four. So cosine is on top. [pause]
> The antiderivative of cosine minus sine is sine plus cosine. [pause]
> At pi over four: root two over two plus root two over two, root two. At zero: zero plus one. The area is root two minus one, about point four one four. [long pause]

## Example 3: Three boundaries
**On screen:** "Find the area of the region bounded by y = eˣ, y = 1, and x = 2." Board: eˣ = 1 at x = 0, so the region runs from x = 0 to x = 2; top eˣ, bottom 1; A = ∫₀² (eˣ − 1) dx; = [eˣ − x]₀²; = (e² − 2) − (1 − 0); = e² − 3 ≈ 4.39.
> Find the area of the region bounded by y equals e to the x, the line y equals one, and the line x equals two. [pause]
> The curve meets y equals one at x equals zero, so the region runs from zero to two, with e to the x on top. [pause]
> Integrate e to the x minus one: e to the x minus x. At two, e squared minus two. At zero, one. [pause]
> The area is e squared minus three, about four point three nine. [long pause]
