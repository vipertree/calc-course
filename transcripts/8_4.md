# 8.4 Finding the Area Between Curves Expressed as Functions of x

## Between two curves
**On screen:** Two curves, an upper one f and a lower one g, crossing at two points. The whole area under the top curve between the crossings shades in, labeled "area under the top". Then the smaller area under the bottom curve shades in a second color, labeled "area under the bottom", and is cut out: it slides away, leaving only the region between the curves. Board: "∫ f(x) dx − ∫ g(x) dx". Then, in the same picture, thin vertical slivers (lines) fill the region from the bottom curve to the top curve; one is highlighted with "height: top − bottom". Board: "∫ (top − bottom) dx".
> You know how to find the area under a curve. Now take two curves, and find the area of the region trapped between them. [pause]
> One way to see it: take the whole area under the top curve. [pause]
> Then cut out the smaller area under the bottom curve. What's left is the region between the curves. [pause]
> Or think of the region as a bunch of thin slivers, added up. Each sliver reaches from the bottom curve up to the top curve, so its height is the difference between the two: top minus bottom. [pause]
> Either way, you get one integral: the top function minus the bottom function. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.4 · Finding the area between curves expressed as functions of x." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Top minus bottom
**On screen:** The region between y = f(x) (top) and y = g(x) (bottom) on [a, b]. One thin vertical sliver at x, drawn as a line from the bottom curve to the top curve, with "height: f(x) − g(x)" above the graph. Then many slivers filling the region, and the board: area = ∫ₐᵇ (f(x) − g(x)) dx; boxed with "∫ (top − bottom) dx".
> Take one thin sliver of the region at x. It reaches from the bottom curve up to the top curve, so its height is f of x minus g of x. [pause]
> Add up all the slivers from a to b. The area is the integral from a to b of f of x minus g of x, d x. [pause]
> In words: integrate top minus bottom. [long pause]

## Even below the axis
**On screen:** A region that dips below the x-axis, with numbered axes. At x = 2, short vertical lines on the graph: one from the axis up to the top curve, labeled "top = 1"; one from the axis down to the bottom curve, labeled "bottom = −2". Then the whole sliver at x = 2, from the bottom curve to the top curve, labeled "height = 1 − (−2) = 3". Then both curves slide up together by 3 units; the region and the sliver keep their shape, and the height label stays 3.
> This works even when part of the region is below the x-axis. [pause]
> Look at x equals two. The top curve is at one, one unit above the axis. The bottom curve is at negative two, two units below it. [pause]
> The sliver at x equals two reaches from negative two up to one, so its height is one minus negative two: three. The subtraction handles the signs for you. [pause]
> Slide both curves up and the region doesn't change. Neither does top minus bottom. [long pause]

## Sketch first
**On screen:** Blank axes. Two curves are sketched one at a time. The picture shows which one is on top between the crossings. Board: "1. sketch: which is on top?"; "2. set f(x) = g(x) and solve"; "3. label the intersection points". The crossings get dots and labels a and b on the sketch.
> For every bounded region, start with a sketch. Think about what each graph looks like, and draw it. [pause]
> The sketch shows which curve is on top. When the region is bounded only by the two curves, the limits of integration are where they meet, so set the functions equal and solve. [pause]
> Then label the intersection points on your sketch. If the picture leaves any doubt about which is on top, plug in an x between the limits. [long pause]

## Area between a line and a parabola
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the area of the region bounded by y = x and y = x² − 2." Empty axes on the right. Board, one step per line: "y = x: a line through the origin, slope 1" (the line is drawn and labeled); "y = x² − 2: the parabola y = x², shifted down 2" (a faint y = x² appears and slides down 2 units into place, labeled); "The line is on top." (the region between them shades in); x² − 2 = x; x² − x − 2 = 0; (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0; x = −1, x = 2; "intersections: (−1, −1) and (2, 2)" (dots and labels appear on the sketch); "check x = 0: line 0, parabola −2"; "sliver height: top − bottom = x − (x² − 2)" (one sliver at x = 0.6 is drawn as a line); A = ∫₋₁² (x − (x² − 2)) dx; = ∫₋₁² (x − x² + 2) dx; = [x²/2 − x³/3 + 2x]₋₁²; = (2 − 8/3 + 4) − (1/2 + 1/3 − 2); = 10/3 − (−7/6); = 9/2.
> Find the area of the region bounded by the line y equals x and the parabola y equals x squared minus two. [pause]
> Start with a sketch. y equals x is just a line through the origin with slope one. [pause]
> y equals x squared minus two: that's the regular parabola, shifted down two. [pause]
> Now we can see very clearly which function is on top. Between the crossings, the line is above the parabola. [pause]
> Next, where do they meet? Set x squared minus two equal to x. [pause]
> Move everything to one side: x squared minus x minus two equals zero. Factor: x minus two, times x plus one. So x is negative one or two. [pause]
> Label those intersection points on the sketch: negative one, negative one, and two, two. [pause]
> A quick check at x equals zero: the line gives zero, the parabola gives negative two. The line is on top, as the sketch shows. [pause]
> Each sliver reaches from the parabola up to the line, so its height is x minus the quantity x squared minus two. Integrate from negative one to two, and distribute the minus sign: x minus x squared plus two. [pause]
> The antiderivative is x squared over two, minus x cubed over three, plus two x. [pause]
> At two: two minus eight thirds plus four, which is ten thirds. At negative one: one half plus one third minus two, which is negative seven sixths. [pause]
> Ten thirds minus negative seven sixths is twenty sixths plus seven sixths: twenty-seven sixths, which is nine halves. [pause]
> Notice that part of the region is below the x-axis, and we never had to treat it separately. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "Area = ∫ₐᵇ (top − bottom) dx"; "limits: where the curves meet"; "top: test a point".
> The area between two curves is the integral of top minus bottom. The limits are where the curves meet, and a test point tells you which is on top. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Root and line
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = √x and y = x/2." Empty axes. Board: "y = √x: the top half of a sideways parabola, from the origin" (drawn, labeled); "y = x/2: a line through the origin, slope ½" (drawn, labeled); "The root is on top, then they cross." (region shaded); √x = x/2; x = x²/4; x² − 4x = 0; x(x − 4) = 0, so x = 0, x = 4; "intersections: (0, 0) and (4, 2)" (dots and labels on the sketch); "sliver height: √x − x/2" (a sliver drawn as a line); A = ∫₀⁴ (√x − x/2) dx; = [⅔x^{3/2} − x²/4]₀⁴; = 16/3 − 4; = 4/3.
> Find the area between the curves y equals root x and y equals x over two. [try it]
> Sketch first. Root x starts at the origin and rises more and more slowly: the top half of a parabola lying on its side. [pause]
> x over two is a line through the origin with slope one half. [pause]
> Near the origin the root climbs faster, so it's on top until the line catches up. [pause]
> Set them equal and square both sides: x equals x squared over four. So x squared minus four x is zero, and x is zero or four. Label the intersection points: zero, zero and four, two. [pause]
> Each sliver's height is root x minus x over two. Integrate from zero to four: two thirds x to the three halves, minus x squared over four. At four: sixteen thirds minus four, four thirds. [long pause]

## Example 2: Sine and cosine
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = cos x and y = sin x for 0 ≤ x ≤ π/4." Empty axes. Board: "y = cos x starts at 1 and falls; y = sin x starts at 0 and rises" (both drawn, labeled); "They meet at x = π/4: (π/4, √2/2)." (dot and label on the sketch); "cos x is on top." (region shaded, a sliver drawn as a line); A = ∫₀^{π/4} (cos x − sin x) dx; = [sin x + cos x]₀^{π/4}; = (√2/2 + √2/2) − (0 + 1); = √2 − 1 ≈ 0.414.
> Find the area between cosine x and sine x, from zero to pi over four. [try it]
> Sketch first. Cosine starts at one and falls. Sine starts at zero and rises. [pause]
> They meet at pi over four, where both equal root two over two. Label that point. Until then, cosine is on top. [pause]
> So integrate cosine minus sine. The antiderivative is sine plus cosine. [pause]
> At pi over four: root two over two plus root two over two, root two. At zero: zero plus one. The area is root two minus one, about point four one four. [long pause]

## Example 3: Three boundaries
**On screen:** "Find the area of the region bounded by y = eˣ, y = 1, and x = 2." Empty axes. Board: "y = eˣ: through (0, 1), rising fast"; "y = 1: a horizontal line; x = 2: a vertical line" (all three drawn); "eˣ = 1 at x = 0: (0, 1)" (dot and label on the sketch); "eˣ is on top, from x = 0 to x = 2." (region shaded, a sliver drawn as a line); A = ∫₀² (eˣ − 1) dx; = [eˣ − x]₀²; = (e² − 2) − (1 − 0); = e² − 3 ≈ 4.39.
> Find the area of the region bounded by y equals e to the x, the line y equals one, and the line x equals two. [try it]
> Sketch first. e to the x passes through zero, one and rises fast. y equals one is a horizontal line, and x equals two is a vertical line. [pause]
> The curve meets y equals one at x equals zero. Label that point. So the region runs from zero to two, with e to the x on top. [pause]
> Integrate e to the x minus one: e to the x minus x. At two, e squared minus two. At zero, one. [pause]
> The area is e squared minus three, about four point three nine. [long pause]
