# 8.6 Finding the Area Between Curves That Intersect at More Than Two Points

## Trading places
**On screen:** Two curves that cross three times, enclosing two regions. In the left region one curve is on top; in the right region the other one is. The "top" label jumps from one curve to the other at the middle crossing.
> These two curves cross three times, so they trap two regions. [pause]
> In the left region, one curve is on top. In the right region, the other one is. [pause]
> Top minus bottom still works, but top and bottom trade places, so the integral has to be split. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.6 · Finding the area between curves that intersect at more than two points." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## A sign chart for f minus g
**On screen:** Board: "f(x) − g(x) > 0: f on top", "f(x) − g(x) < 0: g on top". A number line for f − g with the crossing points marked and the signs + and − in the pieces. Under each piece, the integral it gets: "∫ (f − g)" under +, "∫ (g − f)" under −. Then: "area = ∫ₐᵇ |f(x) − g(x)| dx".
> Which curve is on top is a sign question: where f minus g is positive, f is on top. Where it's negative, g is on top. [pause]
> So make a sign chart for f minus g. The crossing points are its zeros, and they cut the interval into pieces. [pause]
> On each piece, integrate top minus bottom. Together, that's the integral of the absolute value of f minus g. [long pause]

## Why one integral fails
**On screen:** The two-region picture with the left region shaded "+5/12" and the right region shaded "−8/3" when integrated as f − g all at once. Board: ∫₋₁² (f − g) dx = 5/12 − 8/3 = −9/4, crossed out with "not an area: the pieces cancel". Then "5/12 + 8/3 = 37/12" circled as the area.
> What if you skip the split and integrate f minus g across the whole interval? [pause]
> The region where g is on top counts as negative and cancels part of the other region. You get negative nine fourths: not even positive, so it can't be an area. [pause]
> Area never cancels. Split at the crossings and add the pieces. [long pause]

## Three crossings
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the area of the region between y = x³ − x² and y = 2x." Board, one step per line: x³ − x² = 2x; x³ − x² − 2x = 0; x(x² − x − 2) = 0; x(x − 2)(x + 1) = 0; x = −1, 0, 2; let h(x) = (x³ − x²) − 2x; h(−½) = −⅛ − ¼ + 1 = ⅝ > 0; h(1) = 1 − 1 − 2 = −2 < 0; ∫₋₁⁰ h dx = [x⁴/4 − x³/3 − x²]₋₁⁰ = 0 − (¼ + ⅓ − 1) = 5/12; ∫₀² (−h) dx = −(4 − 8/3 − 4) = 8/3; A = 5/12 + 8/3 = 37/12. A sign chart for h fills in: + on (−1, 0), − on (0, 2).
> Find the area of the region between y equals x cubed minus x squared and y equals two x. [try it]
> Where do they cross? Set x cubed minus x squared equal to two x, and move everything to one side. [pause]
> Factor out an x: x times x squared minus x minus two. Then factor the quadratic: x times x minus two, times x plus one. The crossings are negative one, zero, and two. [pause]
> Call the difference h: the cubic minus the line. Test a value in each piece. At negative one half: negative one eighth minus one fourth plus one, five eighths. Positive, so the cubic is on top. [pause]
> At one: one minus one minus two, negative two. Negative, so the line is on top. [pause]
> On the left piece, integrate h from negative one to zero. The antiderivative is x to the fourth over four, minus x cubed over three, minus x squared. At zero it's zero; at negative one it's one fourth plus one third minus one, negative five twelfths. So this piece is five twelfths. [pause]
> On the right piece, integrate the line minus the cubic, which is negative h. At two the antiderivative of h is four minus eight thirds minus four, negative eight thirds. So this piece is eight thirds. [pause]
> Add the pieces: five twelfths plus thirty-two twelfths, thirty-seven twelfths. [long pause]

## With a calculator
**On screen:** A calculator screen showing "∫(abs(X³ − X² − 2X), X, −1, 2)" and the result "3.0833…", next to 37/12 ≈ 3.0833.
> On a calculator section, you don't have to split by hand. Type in the integral of the absolute value of f minus g, and the calculator handles the pieces. [pause]
> You still need the outer crossing points for the limits, and the calculator can find those too. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "area = ∫ₐᵇ |f(x) − g(x)| dx"; "sign chart for f − g: split at the crossings"; "add the pieces; never let them cancel".
> When curves cross more than twice, make a sign chart for f minus g, split at the crossings, and integrate top minus bottom on each piece. The area is the integral of the absolute value of the difference, and the pieces never cancel. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Sine and cosine on [0, π]
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = sin x and y = cos x for 0 ≤ x ≤ π." Board: sin x = cos x at x = π/4 in [0, π]; on (0, π/4) cos is on top; on (π/4, π) sin is on top; ∫₀^{π/4} (cos x − sin x) dx = √2 − 1; ∫_{π/4}^π (sin x − cos x) dx = [−cos x − sin x]_{π/4}^π = 1 − (−√2) = 1 + √2; A = (√2 − 1) + (1 + √2) = 2√2 ≈ 2.83.
> Find the area between sine x and cosine x from zero to pi. [pause]
> They cross at pi over four. Before that, cosine is on top; after it, sine is. [pause]
> The first piece is root two minus one. For the second, the antiderivative of sine minus cosine is negative cosine minus sine. At pi that's one; at pi over four it's negative root two. So the piece is one plus root two. [pause]
> Add them: two root two, about two point eight three. [long pause]

## Example 2: A symmetric pair
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = x³ − 3x and y = x." Board: x³ − 3x = x; x³ − 4x = 0; x(x − 2)(x + 2) = 0, so x = −2, 0, 2; on (−2, 0) the cubic is on top, on (0, 2) the line is; ∫₀² (x − (x³ − 3x)) dx = ∫₀² (4x − x³) dx = 8 − 4 = 4; by symmetry the left piece is also 4; A = 8.
> Find the area between x cubed minus three x and the line y equals x. [pause]
> Set them equal: x cubed minus four x is zero, so x times x minus two, times x plus two. The crossings are negative two, zero, and two. [pause]
> On the right piece the line is on top: integrate four x minus x cubed from zero to two. Eight minus four, four. [pause]
> Both curves are odd functions, so the left piece is a mirror image with the same area. The total is eight. [long pause]

## Example 3: Touching is not crossing
**On screen:** "Find the area between y = x⁴ − 2x² and the x-axis." Board: x⁴ − 2x² = x²(x² − 2) = 0 at x = 0, ±√2; sign chart for x⁴ − 2x²: − on (−√2, 0), − on (0, √2) (the curve touches at 0 without crossing); A = ∫_{−√2}^{√2} (2x² − x⁴) dx; = 2[⅔x³ − x⁵/5]₀^{√2}; = 2(4√2/3 − 4√2/5); = 16√2/15 ≈ 1.51.
> Find the area between x to the fourth minus two x squared and the x-axis. [pause]
> The zeros are zero and plus or minus root two. But the sign chart shows negative on both sides of zero: the curve touches the axis there without crossing. [pause]
> So the axis is on top the whole way, and one integral works: two x squared minus x to the fourth, from negative root two to root two. [pause]
> By symmetry that's twice the integral from zero to root two: sixteen root two over fifteen, about one point five one. [long pause]
