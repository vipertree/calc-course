# 8.7 Volumes with Cross Sections: Squares and Rectangles

## Slicing a loaf
**On screen:** A loaf of sandwich bread on a wooden cutting board, seen from above and to the side, so its crust, domed top and end are all visible. Thin cuts appear across it and the slices part a little, showing their crumb faces. One slice in the middle lifts up out of the loaf, slides forward and turns to face the viewer. Its face is labeled "A" (area) and its thickness gets a small double arrow. Board: "volume of a slice ≈ area of its face × thickness".
> Here's a loaf of bread on a cutting board. Slice it into thin, even slices. [pause]
> Pull one slice out and look at it. It's almost a flat slab: its volume is about the area of its face times its thickness. [pause]
> Add up the volumes of all the slices and you get the volume of the loaf. Make the slices thinner and thinner, and that sum becomes an integral. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.7 · Volumes with cross sections: squares and rectangles." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## One kind of volume problem
**On screen:** Heading: "Volume with known cross sections". A graph drawn flat, the way it is on paper: the curve y = f(x) over 0 ≤ x ≤ 4, and the region under it shaded: "the base". The picture tilts back, so the base lies on the floor and we see it at an angle. A segment is drawn straight across the base at one x, perpendicular to the x-axis. On that segment a square stands up; it becomes a rectangle of height 1, then a triangle, then a semicircle.
> Volume problems come in a few types. This lesson is about one of them: solids with known cross sections. [pause]
> It starts with a flat region, called the base. Here it's the region under the graph of a function, from zero to four. [pause]
> Lay the base flat on the floor and look at it from an angle. Now cut straight across it, perpendicular to the x-axis. The cut is a segment across the base. [pause]
> The problem decides what stands on that segment. It could be a square, or a rectangle of some height, or a triangle, or a semicircle. [long pause]

## Same base, different solids
**On screen:** Four copies of the same base, side by side, each turning slowly. On each, its cross sections stand up and fill out a solid: squares ("squares"), rectangles of height 1 ("rectangles, height 1"), equilateral triangles ("triangles"), semicircles ("semicircles"). Then: "This lesson: squares and rectangles. Next lesson: triangles and semicircles."
> Here's the same base four times, with a different shape standing on it each time. [pause]
> Squares give a tall, boxy solid. Rectangles of height one give a solid with a flat top. Triangles give a ridge, and semicircles give a rounded tunnel shape. [pause]
> In this lesson, the cross sections are squares and rectangles. Next time, triangles and semicircles. [long pause]

## Volume is a sum of slices
**On screen:** The squares solid, angled. One square slab at x is highlighted: its face labeled "A(x)" and its thickness marked with a double arrow "Δx". Board: "volume of one slab ≈ A(x) Δx". Then the solid is rebuilt from 4 slabs, then 8, then 16, then 40, then the smooth solid, with "n = 4", "n = 8", ... beside it. Board: "V ≈ Σ A(x) Δx", then "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx", boxed, with "add up infinitely many thin slices" under it. Last: "A(x) = ?".
> Take one slab of this solid, at x. Its face is a cross section, with area A of x, and its thickness is delta x. So its volume is about A of x times delta x. [pause]
> Build the solid from four slabs, and the sum of their volumes is a rough estimate. Use eight, then sixteen, then forty. The steps get smaller, and the stack looks more and more like the smooth solid. [pause]
> This is the same idea behind every integral in this unit. When we add up infinitely many thin pieces, the sum becomes an integral. The volume is the integral from a to b of A of x d x. [pause]
> So each of these problems comes down to one question: what is the area of one slice at x? [long pause]

## The side across the base
**On screen:** A base drawn flat: a region between two curves. A segment at x runs straight across it. Board: "s(x) = length of the segment". The sketch tilts and one thin square stands on the segment. Board: "A(x) = area of the cross section", then "square: A = s²".
> The segment across the base is one side of the cross section. Call its length s of x. [pause]
> A cross section stands on that segment, and what we need is its area: A of x. [pause]
> The shape tells us the area. A square with side s has area s squared. In each problem, read the side off the sketch of the base, then find the area. [long pause]

## Squares on a root
**On screen:** The problem: "The base of a solid is the region bounded by y = √x, the x-axis, and x = 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume." Formula card in the corner: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = s²". The graph is sketched flat as the board fills in: y = √x from the origin, the line x = 4, the base shaded. Board: "y = √x: starts at the origin and rises, bending over"; "the base: under y = √x, from x = 0 to x = 4"; a segment at x from the x-axis up to the curve: "s(x) = height of the region = √x". The sketch tilts to the angled view and one thin square slice stands up on the segment, then the whole solid rises around it as a see-through ghost; the sketch tilts back flat: "on paper: draw the base and one slice". Then: "A(x) = s² = (√x)² = x"; "V = ∫₀⁴ x dx"; "= [x²/2]₀⁴"; "= 8".
> The base of a solid is the region bounded by root x, the x-axis, and x equals four. The cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume. [pause]
> Start by sketching the base. Root x starts at the origin and rises, bending over as it goes. [pause]
> The base is the region under it, from zero to four. Shade it. [pause]
> Now draw one slice across the base, at some x. It runs from the x-axis up to the curve, so its length is the height of the region there: root x. That's the side s. [pause]
> Here's the solid from an angle. One thin square slice stands on that segment, and the whole solid is made of slices like it. On paper, though, you only draw the flat base and that one segment, and picture the square standing on it. [pause]
> Each cross section is a square, so its area is side squared: root x squared, which is x. [pause]
> The volume is the integral of x from zero to four: x squared over two, which is eight. [long pause]

## Rectangles
**On screen:** Board: "rectangle: A = base × height". The same base under y = √x, angled, twice: on the left, rectangles of height 3 stand on it, "height 3: A(x) = 3 s(x)"; on the right, rectangles twice as tall as their base, "height 2s: A(x) = s · 2s = 2s²". Under both: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx".
> Rectangles work the same way, but a rectangle needs two dimensions. Its base is the segment across the region, the side s. [pause]
> The problem gives the height. It might be a fixed number, such as three. Or it might be a multiple of the base, such as twice the base. [pause]
> Either way, the area is base times height, and the volume is the integral of that area. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx"; "A(x) = area of one cross section"; "square: s²; rectangle: base × height"; "sketch the base flat; picture the slice".
> The volume is the integral of the cross-sectional area. So find the area of one cross section at x: read its side off the base, then use the shape. For squares the area is side squared; for rectangles, base times height. Sketch the base flat, and picture one slice standing on it. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Squares between two curves
**On screen:** "The base is the region between y = x and y = x². Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = s²". Sketch first: the line y = x, the parabola y = x², the region between them shaded. Board: "y = x: a line through the origin; y = x²: a parabola"; "x² = x"; "x² − x = 0"; "x(x − 1) = 0, so x = 0, x = 1"; "On (0, 1), x is on top." A segment at x from the parabola up to the line: "The side runs from y = x² up to y = x:"; "s(x) = x − x²". The sketch tilts, one thin square slice stands on the segment and the solid rises around it as a see-through ghost, then it tilts back flat. "A(x) = s² = (x − x²)²"; "= x² − 2x³ + x⁴"; "V = ∫₀¹ (x² − 2x³ + x⁴) dx"; "= ⅓ − ½ + ⅕"; "= 1/30".
> The base is the region between y equals x and y equals x squared, and the cross sections are squares. Find the volume. [pause]
> Sketch the base. y equals x is a line through the origin, and y equals x squared is a parabola. [pause]
> Find where they meet: x squared equals x, so x squared minus x is zero. Factor: x times x minus one. They meet at zero and one. [pause]
> Between zero and one, the line is on top. Try one half: the line gives one half, the parabola one fourth. So a slice runs from the parabola up to the line, and its length is x minus x squared. [pause]
> From an angle, one thin square stands on that slice, and the solid is made of slices like it. They are small, because the region is thin. The slice is so thin that its thickness hardly shows. [pause]
> Each cross section is a square, so its area is the side squared: x squared minus two x cubed plus x to the fourth. [pause]
> Integrate from zero to one: one third minus one half plus one fifth, which is one thirtieth. [long pause]

## Example 2: Rectangles of height 3
**On screen:** "The base is the region between y = 4 − x² and the x-axis. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are rectangles of height 3. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = base × height". Sketch first: the parabola y = 4 − x², opening down from (0, 4), meeting the x-axis at ±2; the region shaded. Board: "4 − x² = 0 at x = ±2"; a segment at x from the axis up to the parabola: "base = height of the region = 4 − x²". The sketch tilts and one thin rectangle of height 3 stands on the segment, the solid rising around it as a see-through ghost, then it tilts back flat. "A(x) = base × height = 3(4 − x²)"; "V = ∫₋₂² 3(4 − x²) dx"; "= 3[4x − x³/3]₋₂²"; "= 3 · 32/3"; "= 32".
> The base is the region under four minus x squared, and the cross sections are rectangles of height three. Find the volume. [pause]
> Sketch it. Four minus x squared is a parabola opening down from four, and it meets the x-axis where four minus x squared is zero: at plus or minus two. [pause]
> A slice runs from the x-axis up to the parabola, so the base of each rectangle is the height of the region: four minus x squared. [pause]
> From an angle, one thin rectangle stands three tall on that slice. They all do, so the solid has a flat top. Its thickness is tiny. All we need is the area of its face. [pause]
> Each cross section is a rectangle, so its area is base times height: three times four minus x squared. [pause]
> The integral of four minus x squared from negative two to two is thirty-two thirds. Times three: thirty-two. [long pause]

## Example 3: Perpendicular to the y-axis
**On screen:** "The base is the region between x = y² and x = 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the y-axis are squares. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ A(y) dy, A = s²". Sketch first: the parabola x = y² opening to the right, the line x = 4, the region between them shaded. Board: "perpendicular to the y-axis: horizontal slices, so integrate in y"; "y² = 4 at y = ±2"; a horizontal segment at y from the parabola across to the line: "The side runs from x = y² across to x = 4:"; "s(y) = 4 − y²". The sketch tilts and one thin square slice stands on the horizontal segment, the solid rising around it as a see-through ghost, then it tilts back flat. "A(y) = s² = (4 − y²)²"; "= 16 − 8y² + y⁴"; "V = ∫₋₂² (16 − 8y² + y⁴) dy"; "= 2(32 − 64/3 + 32/5)"; "= 512/15 ≈ 34.13".
> The base is the region between x equals y squared and x equals four. This time the cross sections are perpendicular to the y-axis. Find the volume if they are squares. [pause]
> Sketch it. x equals y squared is a parabola opening to the right, and x equals four is a vertical line. Perpendicular to the y-axis means the slices are horizontal, so we integrate with respect to y. [pause]
> The curves meet where y squared is four: y equals plus or minus two. [pause]
> A horizontal slice starts at the parabola, x equals y squared, and runs across to the line x equals four. So its length is four minus y squared. [pause]
> From an angle, one thin square stands on that horizontal slice. The slices are tallest in the middle. Its thickness is too small to see; its face is the cross section whose area we need. [pause]
> Each cross section is a square, so its area is the side squared: sixteen minus eight y squared plus y to the fourth. [pause]
> The region is symmetric, so take twice the integral from zero to two: two times thirty-two minus sixty-four thirds plus thirty-two fifths. That's five hundred twelve fifteenths, about thirty-four point one three. [long pause]
