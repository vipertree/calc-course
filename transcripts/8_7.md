# 8.7 Volumes with Cross Sections: Squares and Rectangles

## Slicing a loaf
**On screen:** A loaf of bread sliced into thin, even slices. One slice lifts out and turns to face the viewer, showing its face (area A) and its thickness (a small double arrow). Board: "volume of a slice ≈ area of its face × thickness".
> Slice a loaf of bread into thin slices. Each slice is almost a flat slab: its volume is the area of its face times its thickness. [pause]
> Add up the slices and you get the volume of the loaf. [pause]
> Make the slices thinner and thinner, and the sum becomes an integral. That's the whole idea of this topic. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.7 · Volumes with cross sections: squares and rectangles." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Volume is the integral of area
**On screen:** A solid lying along the x-axis from x = a to x = b. One slice at x, its face shaded with "A(x)" and its thickness marked with a double arrow "dx". Board: V ≈ Σ A(x) Δx; V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx; boxed with the plain-words version "add up the areas of the slices".
> Put the solid along the x-axis from a to b. Slice it perpendicular to the axis, and call the area of the slice at x, A of x. [pause]
> A thin slice has volume A of x times d x. Adding them up and letting the slices get thin gives the volume: the integral from a to b of A of x d x. [pause]
> So every volume problem comes down to one question: what is the area of a slice at x? [long pause]

## A base and its cross sections
**On screen:** A region in the xy-plane, drawn lying flat and seen at a slant: bounded by y = √x, the x-axis, and x = 4. One vertical segment across the region at x, from the x-axis up to the curve, labeled "side = √x". A square rises straight up from that segment. Then more squares rise along the region, building the solid.
> Many AP volume problems describe the solid this way: a flat base region, and cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis with a given shape. [pause]
> Picture the base lying flat on the floor. At each x, draw the segment across the base, from the bottom curve to the top curve. That segment is one side of the cross section. [pause]
> Here the cross sections are squares, so a square stands straight up on every segment. Line them all up, and they build the solid. [long pause]

## Squares on a root
**On screen:** The problem: "The base of a solid is the region bounded by y = √x, the x-axis, and x = 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume." Board, one step per line: side s(x) = √x − 0 = √x; A(x) = s² = (√x)² = x; V = ∫₀⁴ x dx; = [x²/2]₀⁴; = 8. The picture shows the squares standing on the base.
> The base is the region under root x from zero to four, and the cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume. [try it]
> The side of the square at x runs across the base, from the bottom, zero, to the top, root x. So the side is root x. [pause]
> The area of a square is side squared: root x squared, which is just x. [pause]
> The volume is the integral of x from zero to four: x squared over two, eight. [long pause]

## Rectangles
**On screen:** Board: "rectangle: A = base × height". Two cases side by side: "height given (say 3): A(x) = 3·s(x)" and "height a multiple of the base (say 2s): A(x) = s · 2s = 2s²". Under both: "s(x) = top − bottom".
> Rectangles work the same way, but you need both dimensions. The base of the rectangle is the segment across the region: top minus bottom. [pause]
> The problem tells you the height. It might be a fixed number, like three, or a multiple of the base, like twice the base. [pause]
> Either way, the area is base times height, and the volume is the integral of that area. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx"; "side s(x) = top − bottom of the base"; "square: s²; rectangle: base × height".
> The volume is the integral of the cross-sectional area. The side of each cross section is top minus bottom across the base. For squares the area is side squared; for rectangles, base times height. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Squares between two curves
**On screen:** "The base is the region between y = x and y = x². Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume." Board: the curves meet at x = 0 and x = 1; on (0, 1), x is on top; s(x) = x − x²; A(x) = (x − x²)² = x² − 2x³ + x⁴; V = ∫₀¹ (x² − 2x³ + x⁴) dx; = ⅓ − ½ + ⅕; = 1/30.
> The base is the region between y equals x and y equals x squared, and the cross sections are squares. Find the volume. [pause]
> The curves meet at zero and one, and x is on top in between. So the side of the square is x minus x squared. [pause]
> Square it: x squared minus two x cubed plus x to the fourth. [pause]
> Integrate from zero to one: one third minus one half plus one fifth, which is one thirtieth. A thin solid. [long pause]

## Example 2: Rectangles of height 3
**On screen:** "The base is the region between y = 4 − x² and the x-axis. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are rectangles of height 3. Find the volume." Board: 4 − x² = 0 at x = ±2; base of rectangle s(x) = 4 − x²; A(x) = 3(4 − x²); V = ∫₋₂² 3(4 − x²) dx; = 3[4x − x³/3]₋₂²; = 3 · 32/3; = 32.
> The base is the region under four minus x squared, and the cross sections are rectangles of height three. Find the volume. [pause]
> The parabola meets the axis at plus or minus two. The rectangle's base runs across the region: four minus x squared. [pause]
> Its area is three times four minus x squared. [pause]
> The integral of four minus x squared from negative two to two is thirty-two thirds. Times three: thirty-two. [long pause]

## Example 3: Perpendicular to the y-axis
**On screen:** "The base is the region between x = y² and x = 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the y-axis are squares. Find the volume." Board: slices are horizontal, so integrate in y; x = y² meets x = 4 at y = ±2; s(y) = 4 − y² (right − left); A(y) = (4 − y²)² = 16 − 8y² + y⁴; V = ∫₋₂² (16 − 8y² + y⁴) dy; = 2(32 − 64/3 + 32/5); = 512/15 ≈ 34.13.
> The base is the region between x equals y squared and x equals four. This time the cross sections are perpendicular to the y-axis. Find the volume if they are squares. [pause]
> Perpendicular to the y-axis means horizontal slices, so integrate with respect to y. The curves meet at y equals plus or minus two. [pause]
> The side runs from left to right: four minus y squared. Square it: sixteen minus eight y squared plus y to the fourth. [pause]
> The region is symmetric, so take twice the integral from zero to two: two times thirty-two minus sixty-four thirds plus thirty-two fifths. That's five hundred twelve fifteenths, about thirty-four point one three. [long pause]
