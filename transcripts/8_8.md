# 8.8 Volumes with Cross Sections: Triangles and Semicircles

## Same base, new shapes
**On screen:** A base region lying flat on the floor, seen at an angle: the region under the parabola y = 4 − x². Squares stand all along it, building a solid that turns slowly. The squares sink back into the base and equilateral triangles rise in their place, then those sink and semicircles rise. Each solid is labeled as it appears: "squares", "triangles", "semicircles".
> Last time the cross sections were squares and rectangles. Keep the same base, but stand triangles on it instead, or semicircles. [pause]
> Each shape builds a different solid on the same base. Triangles make a ridge, and semicircles make a rounded tunnel. [pause]
> The method doesn't change: the volume is the integral of the cross-sectional area. The only new thing is the area formula for each shape. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 8.8 · Volumes with cross sections: triangles and semicircles." Unit 8 · Applications of Integration.

## Cross sections, again
**On screen:** Heading: "Volume with known cross sections". The base under y = f(x), drawn flat as on paper, then tilted to the angled view. A segment across it at x; a semicircle stands on the segment, "A(x)", with its thickness "Δx". Then the solid is built from 4 semicircular slabs, then 8, 16, 40, then the smooth solid, with "n = 4", "n = 8", ... beside it. Board: "V ≈ Σ A(x) Δx", then "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx", boxed.
> This is the same kind of volume problem as last time: a flat base, with cross sections standing on it, perpendicular to the x-axis. [pause]
> The base is the region under a function. Each slice across it is a segment, and a shape stands on that segment. This time the shapes are triangles and semicircles. [pause]
> Each slab has volume of about A of x times delta x. With four slabs, the stack is lumpy. With more and more slabs, it looks like the smooth solid, and the sum of infinitely many thin slabs becomes an integral. [pause]
> So once again, the whole job is the area of one slice. [long pause]

## Areas from the side s
**On screen:** Three shapes, each with its base side s marked along the bottom. Equilateral triangle: height drawn, "height = (√3/2)s", area = ½ · s · (√3/2)s = (√3/4)s². Semicircle: "diameter = s, so radius = s/2", area = ½π(s/2)² = (π/8)s². Isosceles right triangle with a leg on the base: "legs s and s", area = ½s².
> For an equilateral triangle with side s, the height is root three over two times s. Half base times height gives root three over four, s squared. [pause]
> For a semicircle, the side on the base is the diameter. So the radius is s over two, not s. Half of pi r squared is half pi times s over two squared: pi over eight, s squared. [pause]
> For an isosceles right triangle with a leg on the base, both legs are s, so the area is one half s squared. [pause]
> In every case: find s as top minus bottom, then plug it into the area formula. [long pause]

## Semicircles on a parabola
**On screen:** The problem: "The base of a solid is the region between y = 4 − x² and the x-axis. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are semicircles. Find the volume." Formula card in the corner: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = (π/8)s²". The graph is sketched flat as the board fills in: the parabola y = 4 − x² and the shaded region under it. Board, one step per line: "y = 4 − x²: opens down from (0, 4)"; "4 − x² = 0 at x = ±2"; a segment at x from the axis up to the parabola: "s(x) = 4 − x²". The sketch tilts to the angled view; a semicircle stands up on the segment, then semicircles stand all along the base; the sketch tilts back flat: "on paper: draw the base and one slice". Then: "r = s/2 = (4 − x²)/2"; "A(x) = ½π r² = (π/8)(4 − x²)²"; "(4 − x²)² = 16 − 8x² + x⁴"; "V = (π/8)∫₋₂² (16 − 8x² + x⁴) dx"; "= (π/8) · 2(32 − 64/3 + 32/5)"; "= (π/8)(512/15)"; "= 64π/15 ≈ 13.40".
> The base is the region under four minus x squared, and the cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are semicircles. Find the volume. [pause]
> Start with a sketch of the base. Four minus x squared is a parabola opening down from four. [pause]
> It meets the x-axis where four minus x squared is zero: at plus or minus two. Shade the region under it. [pause]
> Now one slice across the base, at some x. It runs from the axis up to the parabola, so its length is four minus x squared. That's the side s. [pause]
> Here's the solid from an angle. The semicircle stands on that segment, with the segment as its diameter, and semicircles stand all along the base. On paper, you only draw the flat base and the one slice. [pause]
> The segment is the diameter, so the radius is half of it: four minus x squared, over two. [pause]
> The area of a semicircle is half pi r squared. With r equal to s over two, that's pi over eight times four minus x squared, squared. [pause]
> Expand the square: sixteen minus eight x squared plus x to the fourth. [pause]
> The region is symmetric, so integrate from zero to two and double it: two times thirty-two minus sixty-four thirds plus thirty-two fifths, which is five hundred twelve fifteenths. [pause]
> Times pi over eight: sixty-four pi over fifteen, about thirteen point four. [long pause]

## Leg or hypotenuse?
**On screen:** Two isosceles right triangles, each standing as a slab on the same base segment of length s, seen at an angle, then drawn flat below. Left: a leg on the base, the other leg standing straight up: area ½s². Right: the hypotenuse on the base, the right angle at the top, each leg s/√2: area ½(s/√2)² = ¼s². Label: "read which side is on the base".
> Isosceles right triangles come in two versions, and the problem says which. [pause]
> If a leg is on the base, the other leg stands straight up. Both legs are s, and the area is one half s squared. [pause]
> If the hypotenuse is on the base, the right angle is at the top and each leg is s over root two. Then the area is one half times s squared over two: one fourth s squared. Read carefully. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** A summary table with s on the base: "square s²; equilateral triangle (√3/4)s²; isosceles right, leg on base ½s²; isosceles right, hypotenuse on base ¼s²; semicircle, diameter on base (π/8)s²". Beneath: "V = ∫ A(x) dx, s = top − bottom".
> Find the side s across the base as top minus bottom. Then use the shape's area in terms of s, and integrate. For a semicircle, remember the side is the diameter. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Equilateral triangles
**On screen:** "The base is the region under y = √x for 0 ≤ x ≤ 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are equilateral triangles. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = (√3/4)s²". Sketch first: y = √x from the origin, the line x = 4, the base shaded. Board: "the base: under y = √x, from 0 to 4"; a segment at x from the axis up to the curve: "s = √x". The sketch tilts and triangles stand on the base, then it tilts back flat. "A(x) = (√3/4)(√x)² = (√3/4)x"; "V = (√3/4)∫₀⁴ x dx"; "= (√3/4)(8)"; "= 2√3 ≈ 3.46".
> The base is under root x from zero to four, and the cross sections are equilateral triangles. Find the volume. [pause]
> Sketch the base: root x rises from the origin, bending over, and the base is the region under it out to x equals four. [pause]
> A slice at x runs from the axis up to the curve, so the side is root x. [pause]
> From an angle, the triangles stand on the slices and make a ridge that grows toward x equals four. [pause]
> The area is root three over four times root x squared, which is root three over four times x. [pause]
> The integral of x from zero to four is eight. Times root three over four: two root three, about three point four six. [long pause]

## Example 2: Hypotenuse on a disk
**On screen:** "The base is the disk x² + y² ≤ 4. Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are isosceles right triangles with the hypotenuse on the base. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = ¼s²". Sketch first: the circle of radius 2, the disk shaded. Board: "top: y = √(4 − x²); bottom: y = −√(4 − x²)"; a segment at x from the bottom of the circle to the top: "s = 2√(4 − x²)". The sketch tilts and the triangles stand on the disk, right angles on top; then it tilts back flat. "A = ¼s² = ¼ · 4(4 − x²) = 4 − x²"; "V = ∫₋₂² (4 − x²) dx"; "= 32/3".
> The base is a disk of radius two, and the cross sections are isosceles right triangles with the hypotenuse on the base. Find the volume. [pause]
> Sketch the circle of radius two, centered at the origin. Its top half is root of four minus x squared, and its bottom half is the negative of that. [pause]
> A slice at x runs from the bottom half up to the top half. So s is the top minus the bottom: twice root of four minus x squared. [pause]
> From an angle, each triangle lies with its hypotenuse across the disk and its right angle on top. [pause]
> With the hypotenuse on the base, the area is one fourth s squared: one fourth times four times four minus x squared, which is four minus x squared. [pause]
> Integrate from negative two to two: thirty-two thirds. [long pause]

## Example 3: Leg on the base
**On screen:** "The base is the region between y = 2x and y = x². Cross sections perpendicular to the x-axis are isosceles right triangles with a leg on the base. Find the volume." Formula card: "V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx, A = ½s²". Sketch first: the line y = 2x, the parabola y = x², the region between them shaded. Board: "y = 2x: a line, slope 2; y = x²: a parabola"; "x² = 2x"; "x(x − 2) = 0, so x = 0, x = 2"; "On (0, 2), 2x is on top."; a segment at x from the parabola up to the line: "s = 2x − x²". The sketch tilts and the triangles stand with one leg straight up; then it tilts back flat. "A = ½s² = ½(2x − x²)²"; "(2x − x²)² = 4x² − 4x³ + x⁴"; "V = ½∫₀² (4x² − 4x³ + x⁴) dx"; "= ½(32/3 − 16 + 32/5)"; "= ½ · 16/15 = 8/15".
> The base is the region between two x and x squared, and the cross sections are isosceles right triangles with a leg on the base. Find the volume. [pause]
> Sketch the base: y equals two x is a line through the origin with slope two, and y equals x squared is a parabola. [pause]
> They meet where x squared equals two x. Move everything to one side and factor: x times x minus two. So they meet at zero and two. [pause]
> Check x equals one: the line gives two and the parabola gives one, so two x is on top. The side is two x minus x squared. [pause]
> From an angle, each triangle has one leg along the slice and the other leg standing straight up. [pause]
> The area is one half s squared. Expand the square: four x squared minus four x cubed plus x to the fourth. [pause]
> Integrated from zero to two, that's thirty-two thirds minus sixteen plus thirty-two fifths: sixteen fifteenths. [pause]
> Half of that: eight fifteenths. [long pause]
