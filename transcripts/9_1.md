# 9.1 Defining and Differentiating Parametric Equations

## A path with a clock
**On screen:** A ladybug walks along the curve x = t² − 1, y = t³ − 3t from t = −2 to t = 2 (the same curve the table plots after the title), turning as she goes so she always faces the way she is walking. A clock in the corner reads t, and her positions at t = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 are stamped on the trail: (x(t), y(t)). A dashed vertical line at x = 1 meets the trail at two points: "one x, two heights: not a function of x". Then "x(t) = t² − 1", "y(t) = t³ − 3t".
> A ladybug walks across a table. To describe her path, you could try to write y as a function of x. [pause]
> But she comes back over x values she has already passed. Above x equals one, for example, she is at two different heights at two different times. So the path fails the vertical line test, and y is not a function of x. [pause]
> Instead, give each coordinate its own formula in terms of time: x of t and y of t. Here x is t squared minus one, and y is t cubed minus three t. [pause]
> Those two equations together are called parametric equations, and t is the parameter. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.1 · Defining and differentiating parametric equations." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions.

## Plotting a parametric curve
**On screen:** A table with columns t, x = t² − 1, y = t³ − 3t for t = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2: (3, −2), (0, 2), (−1, 0), (0, −2), (3, 2). The points are plotted in order and joined with arrows showing the direction of increasing t, tracing the ladybug's path from the opening.
> Here's the ladybug's path again: x equals t squared minus one, y equals t cubed minus three t. Make a table: for each t, compute x and y. [pause]
> Plot the points in order of t and connect them. The arrows show the direction the point moves as t increases. That direction is called the orientation. [pause]
> Notice the curve passes through x equals zero twice, at different times. As a function of x, it would fail the vertical line test, but as parametric equations it's no problem. [long pause]

## The slope of a parametric curve
**On screen:** Board: the chain rule dy/dt = (dy/dx)(dx/dt); divide by dx/dt; boxed: dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), provided dx/dt ≠ 0. The chain rule sits at the top left with the boxed formula well below it; a small picture on the right: a short step along the curve with horizontal part dx and vertical part dy, both happening in the same tiny time dt.
> How steep is the curve at a point? We want d y d x, but x and y are both given in terms of t. [pause]
> The chain rule says d y d t equals d y d x times d x d t. Divide both sides by d x d t. [pause]
> So the slope is d y d t over d x d t. In a tiny time d t, the point moves d x sideways and d y up, and the slope is rise over run. [long pause]

## A tangent line
**On screen:** The problem: "For x = t² − 1, y = t³ − 3t, find dy/dx and the equation of the tangent line at t = 2." Board, one step per line: dx/dt = 2t; dy/dt = 3t² − 3; dy/dx = (3t² − 3)/(2t); slope: at t = 2, m = (12 − 3)/4 = 9/4; point: x(2) = 3, y(2) = 8 − 6 = 2, so (3, 2); then, set aside and labeled, "point (3, 2), slope m = 9/4"; y − 2 = (9/4)(x − 3). The tangent line is drawn on the curve at (3, 2). The slope formula dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt) stays in the top corner throughout.
> For this curve, find d y d x and the tangent line at t equals two. [pause]
> First the two derivatives with respect to t. D x d t is two t. D y d t is three t squared minus three. [pause]
> Divide: d y d x is three t squared minus three, over two t. [pause]
> At t equals two: twelve minus three is nine, over four. The slope m is nine fourths. [pause]
> For the point, plug t equals two into x and y. X is four minus one, three. Y is eight minus six, two. So the point is three, two. [pause]
> Set them aside: the point is three, two, and the slope is m equals nine fourths. [pause]
> Put them into point-slope form: y minus two equals nine fourths times x minus three. [long pause]

## Horizontal and vertical tangents
**On screen:** The same curve on the left. Board: "horizontal: dy/dt = 0, dx/dt ≠ 0"; "vertical: dx/dt = 0, dy/dt ≠ 0"; "both 0: look closer".
> A horizontal tangent has slope zero, so the top of the fraction is zero: d y d t equals zero, while d x d t isn't. [pause]
> A vertical tangent comes from the bottom: d x d t equals zero, while d y d t isn't. If both are zero at once, you need to look more closely. [pause]

## Why a zero on the bottom means vertical
**On screen:** The board clears. "dy/dx = (3t² − 3)/(2t)". A tangent line rides the curve toward (−1, 0) as t shrinks: a table fills in t = 0.5, 0.2, 0.05 with dx/dt = 1, 0.4, 0.1 and dy/dx = −2.25, −7.2, ≈ −29.9, and at t = 0 the line stands straight up. "bottom → 0, so the slope goes off to infinity: a vertical line". Then a small circle x² + y² = 25 with "dy/dx = −x/y" and vertical tangent lines at (±5, 0): "bottom 0 at y = 0: vertical".
> Pause on that vertical one. On our curve, d y d x is three t squared minus three, over two t. Watch the tangent line as t shrinks toward zero. [pause]
> The bottom, d x d t, shrinks toward zero, while the top stays near negative three. The slope gets steeper and steeper: negative two point two five, then negative seven point two, then about negative thirty. [pause]
> Dividing by numbers closer and closer to zero sends the slope off to infinity. A line with an infinite slope is a vertical line, straight up and down. [pause]
> We've seen this with regular functions. For the circle x squared plus y squared equals twenty-five, the slope was negative x over y, and the vertical tangents were where the denominator y was zero. It's the same here: a zero on the bottom of the derivative means a vertical tangent. [long pause]

## The tangents on our curve
**On screen:** The two rules come back. For this curve: "3t² − 3 = 0: t = ±1", points (0, −2) and (0, 2), with horizontal segments drawn; "2t = 0: t = 0", point (−1, 0), with a vertical segment drawn.
> On our curve, three t squared minus three is zero at t equals plus or minus one: horizontal tangents at zero, negative two and at zero, two. [pause]
> And two t is zero at t equals zero, where d y d t is negative three: a vertical tangent at negative one, zero. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "x = x(t), y = y(t): t is the parameter"; "dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt)"; "horizontal: dy/dt = 0; vertical: dx/dt = 0".
> Parametric equations give x and y separately in terms of a parameter. The slope is d y d t over d x d t. Horizontal tangents come from d y d t equals zero, and vertical tangents from d x d t equals zero. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Around a circle
**On screen:** "For x = cos t, y = sin t, find dy/dx and the slope at t = π/4." Board: dx/dt = −sin t; dy/dt = cos t; dy/dx = cos t/(−sin t) = −cot t; at t = π/4: −cot(π/4) = −1. The unit circle with the tangent line at (√2/2, √2/2). The slope formula stays in the top corner.
> For x equals cosine t and y equals sine t, find d y d x and the slope at t equals pi over four. [pause]
> D x d t is negative sine t, and d y d t is cosine t. [pause]
> Divide: negative cotangent t. At pi over four, cotangent is one, so the slope is negative one. [pause]
> These equations trace the unit circle, and at forty-five degrees the tangent line does slope down at forty-five degrees. [long pause]

## Example 2: Finding the tangents
**On screen:** "Find the points where x = t² − 2t, y = t³ − 12t has horizontal or vertical tangents." Board: dy/dt = 3t² − 12 = 0 at t = ±2, where dx/dt = 2t − 2 ≠ 0; t = 2: (0, −16); t = −2: (8, 16): horizontal; dx/dt = 2t − 2 = 0 at t = 1, where dy/dt = −9 ≠ 0; t = 1: (−1, −11): vertical. The horizontal and vertical tangent rules stay in the top corner.
> Find where x equals t squared minus two t, y equals t cubed minus twelve t, has horizontal or vertical tangents. [pause]
> D y d t is three t squared minus twelve, zero at t equals plus or minus two. D x d t isn't zero there, so these are horizontal tangents: at zero, negative sixteen, and at eight, sixteen. [pause]
> D x d t is two t minus two, zero at t equals one. D y d t is negative nine there, not zero. [pause]
> So there's a vertical tangent at negative one, negative eleven. [long pause]

## Example 3: Eliminating the parameter
**On screen:** "Write x = 2t + 1, y = t² as an equation in x and y, and check the slope both ways." Board: t = (x − 1)/2; y = ((x − 1)/2)² = (x − 1)²/4; dy/dx = (x − 1)/2; parametric: dy/dx = (2t)/2 = t = (x − 1)/2. Same. The slope formula stays in the top corner.
> Sometimes you can eliminate the parameter. For x equals two t plus one and y equals t squared, solve the first equation for t: t is x minus one, over two. [pause]
> Substitute: y equals x minus one squared, over four. A parabola. [pause]
> Its derivative is x minus one over two. The parametric formula gives two t over two, which is t, and t is x minus one over two. The two methods agree. [long pause]
