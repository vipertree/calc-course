# 9.3 Finding Arc Lengths of Curves Given by Parametric Equations

## How far did it go?
**On screen:** The ladybug from 9.1 walking a looping parametric path from t = 0 to t = 3, with a pedometer counting. Beside it, the formula from 8.13 for y = f(x), "L = ∫ √(1 + [f′(x)]²) dx", with a question mark: the path loops, so it isn't a function of x.
> In topic 8.13 we found the length of a curve y equals f of x. But a parametric path can loop back, so it isn't a function of x at all. [pause]
> How far does the ladybug walk from time zero to time three? [pause]
> The same idea works: add up tiny hypotenuses. Only the bookkeeping changes. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.3 · Finding arc lengths of curves given by parametric equations." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions. A small "BC" tag.

## Tiny hypotenuses in time
**On screen:** A tiny right triangle on the path with legs dx and dy and hypotenuse ds, all happening in time dt. Board: ds² = dx² + dy²; divide by dt²: (ds/dt)² = (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²; ds = √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt; boxed: L = ∫ₐᵇ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt. Plain words: "speed = √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²); length = ∫ speed dt".
> Zoom in on a tiny piece of the path, traced in a tiny time d t. It's a hypotenuse with legs d x and d y. [pause]
> Pythagoras again: d s squared equals d x squared plus d y squared. Divide by d t squared, and take the square root. [pause]
> So d s equals the square root of d x d t squared plus d y d t squared, times d t. The arc length from t equals a to t equals b is the integral of that. [pause]
> The square root is the speed of the moving point. Length is speed integrated over time: distance traveled. [long pause]

## A check with a circle
**On screen:** x = r cos t, y = r sin t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 2π. Board: dx/dt = −r sin t, dy/dt = r cos t; (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² = r²(sin²t + cos²t) = r²; L = ∫₀^{2π} r dt = 2πr. "the circumference". A check mark.
> Test it on a circle of radius r: x equals r cosine t, y equals r sine t, once around. [pause]
> The derivatives are negative r sine t and r cosine t. Square and add: r squared times sine squared plus cosine squared, which is r squared. [pause]
> The square root is r, and the integral from zero to two pi is two pi r. The circumference, as it should be. [long pause]

## An exact length
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the length of the curve x = t², y = ⅔t³ for 0 ≤ t ≤ √3." Board, one step per line: dx/dt = 2t; dy/dt = 2t²; (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² = 4t² + 4t⁴; = 4t²(1 + t²); √(4t²(1 + t²)) = 2t√(1 + t²) (t ≥ 0); L = ∫₀^{√3} 2t√(1 + t²) dt; u = 1 + t², du = 2t dt, u from 1 to 4; = ∫₁⁴ √u du = [⅔u^{3/2}]₁⁴; = ⅔(8 − 1) = 14/3 ≈ 4.67.
> Find the length of x equals t squared, y equals two thirds t cubed, from t equals zero to root three. [try it]
> The derivatives: d x d t is two t, and d y d t is two t squared. [pause]
> Square and add: four t squared plus four t to the fourth. Factor out four t squared: four t squared times one plus t squared. [pause]
> The square root is two t times root of one plus t squared. Since t isn't negative here, the square root of t squared is just t. [pause]
> So the length is the integral of two t root of one plus t squared. Substitute u equals one plus t squared; d u is two t d t, and u runs from one to four. [pause]
> That's the integral of root u from one to four: two thirds u to the three halves. Eight minus one, times two thirds: fourteen thirds. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "L = ∫ₐᵇ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt"; "the integrand is the speed"; "check: a circle gives 2πr"; "usually: set up, then calculator".
> The length of a parametric curve is the integral of the square root of d x d t squared plus d y d t squared. The integrand is the speed, so the length is the distance traveled. As in 8.13, most of these are set up by hand and evaluated with a calculator. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Half a circle
**On screen:** "Find the length of x = 3cos t, y = 3sin t for 0 ≤ t ≤ π." Board: dx/dt = −3sin t, dy/dt = 3cos t; (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² = 9; L = ∫₀^π 3 dt = 3π ≈ 9.42.
> Find the length of x equals three cosine t, y equals three sine t, from zero to pi. [pause]
> Square and add the derivatives: nine sine squared plus nine cosine squared, nine. [pause]
> The square root is three, and the integral from zero to pi is three pi. Half the circumference of a circle of radius three. [long pause]

## Example 2: One arch of a cycloid
**On screen:** "Find the length of one arch of the cycloid x = t − sin t, y = 1 − cos t, 0 ≤ t ≤ 2π." A rolling wheel with a dot on its rim traces the arch. Board: dx/dt = 1 − cos t, dy/dt = sin t; sum of squares = 1 − 2cos t + cos²t + sin²t = 2 − 2cos t; 2 − 2cos t = 4sin²(t/2); √ = 2sin(t/2) (≥ 0 on [0, 2π]); L = ∫₀^{2π} 2sin(t/2) dt = [−4cos(t/2)]₀^{2π} = 4 + 4 = 8.
> A point on the rim of a rolling wheel traces a cycloid. Find the length of one arch, from zero to two pi. [pause]
> The derivatives are one minus cosine t and sine t. Squared and added: one minus two cosine t plus cosine squared plus sine squared, which is two minus two cosine t. [pause]
> The half-angle identity turns two minus two cosine t into four sine squared of t over two. Its square root is two sine of t over two, which isn't negative on this interval. [pause]
> Integrate: negative four cosine of t over two, from zero to two pi. Four plus four: eight. A wheel of radius one traces an arch of length exactly eight. [long pause]

## Example 3: A calculator length
**On screen:** "Find the length of x = eᵗ, y = t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1." Board: dx/dt = eᵗ, dy/dt = 1; L = ∫₀¹ √(e²ᵗ + 1) dt; ≈ 2.003 (calculator).
> Find the length of x equals e to the t, y equals t, from zero to one. [pause]
> The derivatives are e to the t and one, so the integrand is the square root of e to the two t plus one. [pause]
> That has no easy antiderivative. A calculator gives about two point zero zero three. [long pause]
