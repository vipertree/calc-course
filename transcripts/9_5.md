# 9.5 Integrating Vector-Valued Functions

## Running the film backward
**On screen:** A velocity arrow riding along an unseen path, its direction and length changing. A question mark where the path should be. Then the path is drawn in behind the arrow, starting from a marked point labeled "start".
> Last topic, we went from position to velocity by differentiating. Now run it backward: if you know the velocity at every moment, can you find the path? [pause]
> Yes, if you also know where it started. Integrate the velocity, component by component, and use the starting point to pin down the constants. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.5 · Integrating vector-valued functions." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions. A small "BC" tag.

## Integrate each component
**On screen:** Board: ∫⟨x(t), y(t)⟩ dt = ⟨∫x(t) dt, ∫y(t) dt⟩; "+ C becomes a constant vector ⟨C₁, C₂⟩". Then the definite version: ∫ₐᵇ v(t) dt = r(b) − r(a): "displacement vector". Then: r(t) = r(0) + ∫₀ᵗ v(u) du.
> Integrating a vector-valued function works the same way as differentiating: one component at a time. [pause]
> Each component gets its own constant, so the constant of integration is a constant vector, C one, C two. [pause]
> A definite integral of velocity gives the displacement vector, r of b minus r of a: how far the point moved in x and how far in y. [pause]
> And just like on a number line, position equals the starting position plus the integral of velocity. [long pause]

## Position from velocity
**On screen:** The problem: "A particle has velocity v(t) = ⟨2t, 3t²⟩ and r(0) = ⟨1, −2⟩. Find r(t) and r(2)." Board, one step per line: r(t) = ⟨∫2t dt, ∫3t² dt⟩ = ⟨t² + C₁, t³ + C₂⟩; r(0) = ⟨C₁, C₂⟩ = ⟨1, −2⟩; r(t) = ⟨t² + 1, t³ − 2⟩; r(2) = ⟨4 + 1, 8 − 2⟩ = ⟨5, 6⟩. Alternative line: r(2) = ⟨1, −2⟩ + ∫₀² ⟨2t, 3t²⟩ dt = ⟨1, −2⟩ + ⟨4, 8⟩ = ⟨5, 6⟩.
> A particle has velocity two t, three t squared, and starts at one, negative two. Find its position function, and where it is at time two. [pause]
> Integrate each component: t squared plus C one, and t cubed plus C two. [pause]
> At time zero the position is C one, C two, and that has to equal one, negative two. [pause]
> So the position is t squared plus one, t cubed minus two. At time two: five, six. [pause]
> Or skip the general formula: start at one, negative two, and add the displacement from zero to two, which is four, eight. Same point: five, six. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "∫⟨x, y⟩ dt = ⟨∫x dt, ∫y dt⟩ + ⟨C₁, C₂⟩"; "∫ₐᵇ v dt = displacement"; "r(t) = r(0) + ∫₀ᵗ v".
> Integrate vector-valued functions componentwise, with a constant vector. The definite integral of velocity is the displacement vector, and position is the starting position plus that displacement. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: A definite integral
**On screen:** "Evaluate ∫₀^π ⟨sin t, cos t⟩ dt." Board: = ⟨∫₀^π sin t dt, ∫₀^π cos t dt⟩; = ⟨[−cos t]₀^π, [sin t]₀^π⟩; = ⟨2, 0⟩.
> Evaluate the integral from zero to pi of sine t, cosine t. [pause]
> Do each component. The integral of sine from zero to pi is two. The integral of cosine from zero to pi is zero. [pause]
> So the answer is the vector two, zero. If this were a velocity, the particle would end up two units to the right of where it started, at the same height. [long pause]

## Example 2: A thrown ball
**On screen:** "A ball has acceleration a(t) = ⟨0, −32⟩ ft/s², initial velocity ⟨40, 30⟩, and starts at the origin. When and where does it land (y = 0)?" A parabolic arc on the ground. Board: v(t) = ⟨40, 30 − 32t⟩; r(t) = ⟨40t, 30t − 16t²⟩; 30t − 16t² = 0; t(30 − 16t) = 0; t = 15/8 s; x = 40 · 15/8 = 75 ft.
> A ball is thrown from the origin with velocity forty, thirty feet per second, and gravity gives it acceleration zero, negative thirty-two. When and where does it land? [pause]
> Integrate the acceleration: the velocity is forty, thirty minus thirty-two t, using the starting velocity for the constants. [pause]
> Integrate again: the position is forty t, thirty t minus sixteen t squared, since it starts at the origin. [pause]
> It lands when the height is zero: t times thirty minus sixteen t equals zero, so t is fifteen eighths of a second. Then x is forty times fifteen eighths: seventy-five feet. [long pause]

## Example 3: Exponential and log components
**On screen:** "v(t) = ⟨eᵗ, 1/(t + 1)⟩ and r(0) = ⟨2, 0⟩. Find r(1)." Board: r(t) = ⟨eᵗ + C₁, ln(t + 1) + C₂⟩; r(0) = ⟨1 + C₁, C₂⟩ = ⟨2, 0⟩, so C₁ = 1, C₂ = 0; r(t) = ⟨eᵗ + 1, ln(t + 1)⟩; r(1) = ⟨e + 1, ln 2⟩ ≈ ⟨3.72, 0.69⟩.
> The velocity is e to the t, one over t plus one, and the particle starts at two, zero. Find its position at time one. [pause]
> Integrate: e to the t plus C one, and ln of t plus one plus C two. [pause]
> At time zero that's one plus C one, and C two. Matching two, zero: C one is one and C two is zero. [pause]
> At time one: e plus one, ln two. About three point seven two, point six nine. [long pause]
