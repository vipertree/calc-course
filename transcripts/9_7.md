# 9.7 Defining Polar Coordinates and Differentiating in Polar Form

## A different way to say where
**On screen:** A lighthouse at the origin sweeping its beam around. A boat out on the water: first located by a grid (x and y, "3 east, 4 north"), then by the beam (distance 5 along the beam at angle θ). Labels: "rectangular (x, y)" and "polar (r, θ)".
> To tell someone where a boat is, you could say three miles east and four miles north. That's rectangular coordinates. [pause]
> Or you could stand at the lighthouse and say: turn to this angle, then go five miles out along the beam. That's polar coordinates: a distance r and an angle theta. [pause]
> Some curves are much simpler to describe this way. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.7 · Defining polar coordinates and differentiating in polar form." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions. A small "BC" tag.

## Converting between the two
**On screen:** A point P with r and θ marked and a right triangle dropped to the x-axis. Board: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ; r² = x² + y²; tan θ = y/x. Example: (r, θ) = (2, π/3) becomes (1, √3).
> Drop a right triangle from the point to the x-axis. The hypotenuse is r and the angle is theta. [pause]
> So x is r cosine theta and y is r sine theta. Going back, r squared is x squared plus y squared, and tangent theta is y over x. [pause]
> For instance, distance two at angle pi over three is the point one, root three. [long pause]

## Polar curves
**On screen:** Three polar graphs drawn as θ sweeps: r = 2 (a circle), r = θ (a spiral), r = 1 + cos θ (a cardioid). For the cardioid, a radius sweeps around with its length changing, tracing the curve; a small table of θ and r values on the side.
> A polar curve gives r as a function of theta. As the angle sweeps around, the distance from the origin changes, and the point traces a curve. [pause]
> R equals two is a circle. R equals theta spirals outward. R equals one plus cosine theta is a heart shape called a cardioid. [long pause]

## Slopes of polar curves
**On screen:** Board: r = f(θ); x = f(θ)cos θ, y = f(θ)sin θ: "polar is parametric with parameter θ"; dy/dx = (dy/dθ)/(dx/dθ); dx/dθ = f′(θ)cos θ − f(θ)sin θ; dy/dθ = f′(θ)sin θ + f(θ)cos θ (product rule). A note: "dy/dx is not dr/dθ".
> Here's the key idea: a polar curve is a parametric curve in disguise. With r equals f of theta, x is f of theta cosine theta and y is f of theta sine theta. The parameter is theta. [pause]
> So the slope is d y d theta over d x d theta, exactly as in 9.1. Each of those needs the product rule. [pause]
> Be careful: d r d theta is not the slope. It tells you something else, which we'll see in a moment. [long pause]

## The cardioid's tangent line
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the slope of r = 1 + cos θ at θ = π/2, and the tangent line there." Board, one step per line: x = (1 + cos θ)cos θ, y = (1 + cos θ)sin θ; dx/dθ = −sin θ cos θ − (1 + cos θ)sin θ = −sin θ(1 + 2cos θ); dy/dθ = −sin²θ + (1 + cos θ)cos θ = cos θ + cos 2θ; at θ = π/2: dx/dθ = −1, dy/dθ = 0 + cos π = −1; dy/dx = (−1)/(−1) = 1; r = 1, point (0, 1); y − 1 = x. The cardioid with the tangent line at (0, 1).
> Find the slope of the cardioid r equals one plus cosine theta at theta equals pi over two, and the tangent line there. [pause]
> Write x and y: x is one plus cosine theta, times cosine theta; y is one plus cosine theta, times sine theta. [pause]
> D x d theta, by the product rule: negative sine theta cosine theta, minus one plus cosine theta times sine theta. Factor out negative sine theta: negative sine theta times one plus two cosine theta. [pause]
> D y d theta: negative sine squared theta, plus one plus cosine theta times cosine theta. That simplifies to cosine theta plus cosine two theta. [pause]
> At pi over two: d x d theta is negative one times one, negative one. D y d theta is zero plus cosine pi, negative one. [pause]
> The slope is negative one over negative one: one. At pi over two, r is one, so the point is zero, one. The tangent line is y minus one equals x. [long pause]

## What dr/dθ tells you
**On screen:** The cardioid with a radius at angle θ. As θ increases, the radius lengthens (dr/dθ > 0, "moving away from the origin") or shortens (dr/dθ < 0, "moving toward the origin"). Board: "dr/dθ > 0: r increasing, the curve moves away from the pole"; "dr/dθ < 0: toward the pole".
> So what is d r d theta? It's the rate at which the distance from the origin changes as the angle turns. [pause]
> If d r d theta is positive and r is positive, the curve is moving away from the origin. If it's negative, the curve is moving toward the origin. AP questions ask about this often. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r² = x² + y²"; "polar curve r = f(θ) is parametric in θ"; "dy/dx = (dy/dθ)/(dx/dθ)"; "dr/dθ: toward or away from the origin".
> Polar coordinates locate a point by distance and angle. A polar curve is parametric in theta, so its slope is d y d theta over d x d theta, using the product rule. And d r d theta tells you whether the curve is moving toward or away from the origin. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Converting a polar equation
**On screen:** "Write r = 4sin θ in rectangular form and identify the curve." Board: multiply by r: r² = 4r sin θ; x² + y² = 4y; x² + y² − 4y + 4 = 4; x² + (y − 2)² = 4: a circle of radius 2 centered at (0, 2).
> Write r equals four sine theta in rectangular coordinates. [pause]
> Multiply both sides by r, so you can use the conversions: r squared equals four r sine theta. That's x squared plus y squared equals four y. [pause]
> Complete the square in y: x squared plus y minus two squared equals four. A circle of radius two centered at zero, two. [long pause]

## Example 2: A spiral's slope
**On screen:** "Find the slope of r = θ at θ = π/2." Board: x = θ cos θ, y = θ sin θ; dx/dθ = cos θ − θ sin θ; dy/dθ = sin θ + θ cos θ; at π/2: dx/dθ = 0 − π/2 = −π/2; dy/dθ = 1 + 0 = 1; dy/dx = 1/(−π/2) = −2/π ≈ −0.64.
> Find the slope of the spiral r equals theta at theta equals pi over two. [pause]
> X is theta cosine theta and y is theta sine theta. Product rule: d x d theta is cosine theta minus theta sine theta, and d y d theta is sine theta plus theta cosine theta. [pause]
> At pi over two: d x d theta is negative pi over two, and d y d theta is one. [pause]
> The slope is one over negative pi over two: negative two over pi, about negative point six four. [long pause]

## Example 3: Toward or away?
**On screen:** "For r = 1 + 2sin θ, is the curve moving toward or away from the origin at θ = π/3?" Board: r(π/3) = 1 + √3 > 0; dr/dθ = 2cos θ; at π/3: 2 · ½ = 1 > 0; r > 0 and increasing: moving away from the origin.
> For r equals one plus two sine theta, is the curve moving toward or away from the origin at theta equals pi over three? [pause]
> First, r is one plus root three there, positive. [pause]
> D r d theta is two cosine theta, which is one at pi over three. Positive. [pause]
> R is positive and increasing, so the curve is moving away from the origin. [long pause]
