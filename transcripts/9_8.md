# 9.8 Find the Area of a Polar Region or the Area Bounded by a Single Polar Curve

## Slicing a pizza instead
**On screen:** A cardioid filled in. First, vertical rectangle slices are tried (they fit badly around the curve). Then the region is cut into thin wedges from the origin, like pizza slices, each wedge almost a sector of a circle.
> To find the area under a curve y equals f of x, we sliced it into vertical rectangles. For a polar curve, that's awkward: the curve wraps around the origin. [pause]
> Polar curves come with a better slicing: thin wedges from the origin, like slices of pizza. [pause]
> Each thin wedge is almost a sector of a circle, and we know the area of a sector. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.8 · Finding the area of a polar region." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions. A small "BC" tag.

## The area of a sector
**On screen:** A circle of radius r with a sector of angle Δθ shaded. Board: "whole circle: angle 2π, area πr²"; "sector: fraction Δθ/2π of the circle"; area = (Δθ/2π)·πr² = ½r²Δθ. Then a thin wedge of the cardioid with radius r = f(θ): area ≈ ½[f(θ)]²Δθ; sum and limit: A = ½∫_α^β r² dθ, boxed.
> A sector with angle delta theta is a fraction of the whole circle: delta theta out of two pi. [pause]
> So its area is that fraction of pi r squared: one half r squared delta theta. [pause]
> For a thin wedge of a polar region, r is almost constant, so the wedge has area about one half f of theta squared, delta theta. [pause]
> Add up the wedges and let them get thin: the area is one half the integral of r squared, d theta, from alpha to beta. [long pause]

## The area inside a cardioid
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the area inside r = 1 + cos θ." Board, one step per line: the curve is traced once for 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π; A = ½∫₀^{2π} (1 + cos θ)² dθ; (1 + cos θ)² = 1 + 2cos θ + cos²θ; cos²θ = ½(1 + cos 2θ); = 3/2 + 2cos θ + ½cos 2θ; A = ½[3θ/2 + 2sin θ + ¼sin 2θ]₀^{2π}; = ½(3π); = 3π/2 ≈ 4.71.
> Find the area inside the cardioid r equals one plus cosine theta. [pause]
> The curve is traced once as theta goes from zero to two pi, so those are the limits. [pause]
> The area is one half the integral of one plus cosine theta, squared. Expand: one plus two cosine theta plus cosine squared theta. [pause]
> For cosine squared, use the power-reducing identity: one half, times one plus cosine two theta. [pause]
> So the integrand is three halves, plus two cosine theta, plus one half cosine two theta. [pause]
> Over a full turn, the cosine terms integrate to zero. Three halves times two pi is three pi, and half of that is three pi over two. [long pause]

## Choosing the limits
**On screen:** The rose r = sin 2θ with four petals. One petal is highlighted as θ runs from 0 to π/2, the radius sweeping it out from the origin and back. Board: "start and end where r = 0"; sin 2θ = 0 at θ = 0, π/2. A note: "trace the curve to make sure each piece is covered once".
> The hardest part is usually the limits. Think about where the radius starts and stops sweeping out the region you want. [pause]
> For one petal of r equals sine two theta, the petal starts at the origin and returns to it. R is zero when sine two theta is zero: at zero and at pi over two. Those are the limits for the first petal. [pause]
> Always trace the curve and make sure the region is swept exactly once. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "sector: ½r²Δθ"; "A = ½∫_α^β r² dθ"; "limits: where the sweep starts and stops (often r = 0)"; "cos²θ = ½(1 + cos 2θ), sin²θ = ½(1 − cos 2θ)".
> Polar area slices the region into wedges: one half the integral of r squared, d theta. Choose the limits so the radius sweeps the region exactly once, and keep the power-reducing identities handy. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: One petal of a rose
**On screen:** "Find the area of one petal of r = sin 2θ." Board: one petal: 0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2; A = ½∫₀^{π/2} sin²2θ dθ; sin²2θ = ½(1 − cos 4θ); = ¼[θ − ¼sin 4θ]₀^{π/2}; = ¼ · π/2 = π/8 ≈ 0.39.
> Find the area of one petal of r equals sine two theta. [pause]
> One petal runs from zero to pi over two. The area is one half the integral of sine squared two theta. [pause]
> Power-reduce: sine squared two theta is one half times one minus cosine four theta. [pause]
> One fourth times theta minus a quarter sine four theta, from zero to pi over two. The sine term is zero at both ends, so the area is one fourth of pi over two: pi over eight. [long pause]

## Example 2: A circle, checked
**On screen:** "Find the area inside r = 4sin θ." Board: traced once for 0 ≤ θ ≤ π; A = ½∫₀^π 16sin²θ dθ; = 8 · ½∫₀^π (1 − cos 2θ) dθ; = 4π; check: the circle x² + (y − 2)² = 4 has area π · 2² = 4π.
> Find the area inside r equals four sine theta. [pause]
> This circle is traced once as theta goes from zero to pi, not two pi. Going around again would count the area twice. [pause]
> One half the integral of sixteen sine squared theta: eight times one half the integral of one minus cosine two theta. That's four pi. [pause]
> Check: this is a circle of radius two, with area four pi. [long pause]

## Example 3: A spiral's sweep
**On screen:** "Find the area swept out by the spiral r = θ for 0 ≤ θ ≤ π." Board: A = ½∫₀^π θ² dθ; = ½[θ³/3]₀^π; = π³/6 ≈ 5.17.
> Find the area swept out by the spiral r equals theta, from zero to pi. [pause]
> One half the integral of theta squared: one half times pi cubed over three. [pause]
> The area is pi cubed over six, about five point one seven. [long pause]
