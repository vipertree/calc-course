# 9.9 Finding the Area of the Region Bounded by Two Polar Curves

## Between two polar curves
**On screen:** A circle r = 3cos θ and a cardioid r = 1 + cos θ drawn together. The crescent-shaped region inside the circle but outside the cardioid is shaded. A thin wedge from the origin crosses it: the part between the curves is highlighted, the part inside the cardioid is dimmed.
> Here are two polar curves: a circle and a cardioid. We want the area of the crescent inside the circle but outside the cardioid. [pause]
> Slice with wedges from the origin, as before. Each wedge has an outer piece we want and an inner piece we don't. [pause]
> So the area is the outer region minus the inner region: one integral, with the outer radius squared minus the inner radius squared. [long pause]

## Title
**On screen:** (No narration: the card shows with the intro music.) Title card: "Topic 9.9 · Finding the area of the region bounded by two polar curves." Unit 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions. A small "BC" tag.

## Outer squared minus inner squared
**On screen:** One wedge: a big sector of radius R minus a small sector of radius r. Board: area ≈ ½R²Δθ − ½r²Δθ = ½(R² − r²)Δθ; A = ½∫_α^β (R² − r²) dθ, boxed. A warning: "(R − r)² is not R² − r²". Then: "limits: where the curves intersect: set the two r's equal".
> A wedge of the crescent is a big sector of radius R minus a small sector of radius r. [pause]
> So its area is one half R squared minus r squared, times delta theta. The total area is one half the integral of R squared minus r squared. [pause]
> Same warning as with washers: square each radius separately. R minus r, squared, is wrong. [pause]
> The limits are usually where the curves cross: set the two r's equal and solve for theta. [long pause]

## Inside the circle, outside the cardioid
**On screen:** The problem: "Find the area inside r = 3cos θ and outside r = 1 + cos θ." Board, one step per line: 3cos θ = 1 + cos θ; cos θ = ½; θ = ±π/3; R = 3cos θ, r = 1 + cos θ; R² − r² = 9cos²θ − (1 + 2cos θ + cos²θ) = 8cos²θ − 2cos θ − 1; 8cos²θ = 4 + 4cos 2θ, so R² − r² = 3 + 4cos 2θ − 2cos θ; A = ½∫_{−π/3}^{π/3} (3 + 4cos 2θ − 2cos θ) dθ; = ½[3θ + 2sin 2θ − 2sin θ]_{−π/3}^{π/3}; = ½(2π) = π.
> Find the area inside the circle r equals three cosine theta and outside the cardioid r equals one plus cosine theta. [pause]
> Where do they meet? Three cosine theta equals one plus cosine theta, so cosine theta is one half, and theta is plus or minus pi over three. [pause]
> Between those angles, the circle is outside: capital R is three cosine theta, little r is one plus cosine theta. [pause]
> R squared minus r squared: nine cosine squared, minus one plus two cosine theta plus cosine squared. That's eight cosine squared theta minus two cosine theta minus one. [pause]
> Power-reduce: eight cosine squared theta is four plus four cosine two theta. So the integrand is three plus four cosine two theta minus two cosine theta. [pause]
> Antiderivative: three theta plus two sine two theta minus two sine theta. At pi over three, the sine terms are root three and negative root three: they cancel, leaving pi. At negative pi over three, negative pi. [pause]
> The difference is two pi, and half of that is pi. [long pause]

## Close
**On screen:** "A = ½∫_α^β (R² − r²) dθ"; "R: outer curve, r: inner curve (from the origin)"; "limits: set the r's equal"; "square each radius separately".
> The area between two polar curves is one half the integral of the outer radius squared minus the inner radius squared. Find the limits by setting the curves equal, and square each radius separately. [long pause]

# Worked examples

## Example 1: Inside a circle, outside another
**On screen:** "Find the area inside r = 2sin θ and outside r = 1." Board: 2sin θ = 1 at θ = π/6, 5π/6; A = ½∫_{π/6}^{5π/6} (4sin²θ − 1) dθ; 4sin²θ = 2 − 2cos 2θ, so the integrand is 1 − 2cos 2θ; = ½[θ − sin 2θ]_{π/6}^{5π/6}; = ½(2π/3 + √3); = π/3 + √3/2 ≈ 1.91.
> Find the area inside the circle r equals two sine theta and outside the circle r equals one. [pause]
> They meet where sine theta is one half: pi over six and five pi over six. [pause]
> The integrand is four sine squared theta minus one, which power-reduces to one minus two cosine two theta. [pause]
> The antiderivative is theta minus sine two theta. Evaluate and halve: pi over three plus root three over two, about one point nine one. [long pause]

## Example 2: The region inside both
**On screen:** "Find the area of the region inside both r = 1 and r = 2cos θ." The lens-shaped overlap of the two circles. Board: they meet where 2cos θ = 1: θ = ±π/3; for |θ| ≤ π/3 the boundary is r = 1, for π/3 ≤ |θ| ≤ π/2 it is r = 2cos θ; by symmetry A = 2[½∫₀^{π/3} 1² dθ + ½∫_{π/3}^{π/2} 4cos²θ dθ]; = π/3 + [2θ + sin 2θ]_{π/3}^{π/2}; = π/3 + π/3 − √3/2; = 2π/3 − √3/2 ≈ 1.23.
> Find the area of the region inside both circles: r equals one and r equals two cosine theta. [pause]
> They meet at plus or minus pi over three. Here the boundary changes: near the x-axis, the region is bounded by r equals one; beyond pi over three, by r equals two cosine theta. So split the integral. [pause]
> Use symmetry and double the top half: one half the integral of one from zero to pi over three, plus one half the integral of four cosine squared theta from pi over three to pi over two. [pause]
> That's pi over three, plus pi over three minus root three over two. The overlap has area two pi over three minus root three over two, about one point two three. [long pause]

## Example 3: A calculator problem
**On screen:** "Find the area inside r = 2 + 2sin θ and outside r = 3." Board: 2 + 2sin θ = 3 at θ = π/6, 5π/6; A = ½∫_{π/6}^{5π/6} ((2 + 2sin θ)² − 9) dθ; ≈ 4.653 (calculator).
> Find the area inside r equals two plus two sine theta and outside the circle r equals three. [pause]
> They meet where sine theta is one half: pi over six and five pi over six. [pause]
> Set it up: one half the integral of two plus two sine theta, squared, minus nine. A calculator gives about four point six five three. [long pause]
